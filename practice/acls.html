Question 1 · Basic Life Support (BLS)

In a hospital cafeteria, a 58-year-old man suddenly stands and grabs his neck. He cannot answer when asked whether he is choking, his attempts to cough are silent, and his lips are turning dusky. After calling for help, which sequence should the nurse use?

  1. Cycles of 5 back blows followed by 5 abdominal thrusts
  2. Abdominal thrusts repeated until the object is expelled
  3. Cycles of 5 back blows followed by 5 chest thrusts
  4. Lowering him to the floor and starting chest compressions

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The 2025 guideline recommends repeated cycles of 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts for severe obstruction in a responsive adult.
  • B. Abdominal thrusts alone were the earlier adult approach; back blows were added in 2025 because they relieved obstruction with fewer injuries in a cohort study.
  • C. Chest thrusts replace abdominal thrusts in late pregnancy or when the rescuer cannot encircle the abdomen, which is not the case here.
  • D. Compressions begin once the patient becomes unresponsive; while he is conscious, back blows and thrusts are used.

Inability to speak, a silent or absent cough, and cyanosis signal a severe foreign-body airway obstruction, which can progress quickly to unconsciousness and cardiac arrest. Because a cohort of 709 patients linked back blows to better relief and fewer injuries than abdominal thrusts, and thrusts have caused injuries such as gastric rupture, the 2025 AHA guideline adopts alternating sets of 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts, matching the pediatric approach. Cycles continue until the object is expelled or the patient becomes unresponsive.

Sources: AHA 2025 Part 7: Adult BLS

Question 2 · Rhythm Recognition

An intubated patient with ARDS is prone in the ICU when the monitor shows VF and the arterial waveform flattens. The team cannot turn him supine safely and starts compressions with him prone. The defibrillator is at the bedside. Which approach to defibrillation is BEST?

  1. Compress prone for 2 minutes before the first shock
  2. Bi-axillary pads, one in each midaxillary line
  3. Turn him supine first so anterolateral pads can be placed
  4. Both pads on the back, one on each side of the spine

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. CPR before the first shock does not help when VF is monitored and a defibrillator is at hand; the shock is given as soon as possible.
  • B. Correct. Guidelines suggest an alternative pad position such as bi-axillary when the patient is prone, which lets the team shock monitored VF without turning him.
  • C. Anterolateral is the usual first position, but the team has judged the turn unsafe, and turning would delay the shock for monitored VF and risk the airway and lines.
  • D. This keeps the patient prone, which is tempting, but both electrodes sit behind the heart, so much of the current crosses the back instead of passing through the ventricles.

Anterolateral placement is the usual first choice, but a prone patient's anterior chest lies against the bed. Resuscitation guidelines suggest an alternative pad position when the patient is prone, such as bi-axillary, so the shock is not held up for a turn that cannot be done safely. Monitored VF with a defibrillator at hand is shocked as soon as possible, and compressions resume immediately afterward. Prone CPR is reserved for patients who cannot be turned safely; if the team later turns him supine, the pads can be moved to the standard position during a planned pause.

Sources: Resuscitation Council UK 2025: Adult Advanced Life Support; AHA 2025 Part 7: Adult BLS

Question 3 · ACLS Pharmacology

An 81-year-old who weighs 72 kg is in the catheterization laboratory for PCI of an NSTEMI. He has no diabetes and no prior MI, stroke or TIA, and he chewed aspirin in the emergency department. The order reads prasugrel 60 mg orally now. Which response by the nurse is BEST?

  1. Give it, then ask for a 5 mg daily maintenance dose
  2. Give it as a reduced 30 mg loading dose
  3. Give it, because he has no prior stroke or TIA
  4. Hold it and question the order because of his age

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The label suggests 5 mg maintenance for patients under 60 kg; at 72 kg that rule does not apply, and a smaller maintenance dose does not remove the age-related bleeding risk.
  • B. The label gives a single 60 mg loading dose and no reduced load for older patients, so halving the load is not a labeled way to make the drug safer.
  • C. A prior stroke or TIA is the contraindication, and he has neither, but age 75 or older is a separate reason the label advises against prasugrel without diabetes or a prior MI.
  • D. Correct. The prasugrel label calls the drug generally not recommended at 75 or older because of fatal and intracranial bleeding with uncertain benefit, except with diabetes or a prior MI, and he has neither.

Prasugrel is a potent P2Y12 inhibitor given with aspirin for ACS managed with PCI. Its label carries contraindications for active pathological bleeding and any prior TIA or stroke, and a separate caution for age 75 or older: in TRITON-TIMI 38 these patients had more fatal and intracranial bleeding with uncertain benefit, so the drug is generally not recommended unless the patient has diabetes or a prior MI, where its effect appears greater. Weight under 60 kg is another bleeding risk, handled by a 5 mg maintenance dose. Clarifying the order lets the team choose ticagrelor or clopidogrel instead.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); ACC/AHA ACS Guideline (2025)

Question 4 · ACLS Algorithms

During a PEA arrest, an intubated patient is log-rolled onto a backboard. Before the move, waveform capnography showed a steady trace with EtCO₂ 18 mm Hg. Now the trace is flat at 0, no breath sounds are heard on either side, and gurgling is heard over the epigastrium. Compressions are still high quality. Which action is MOST appropriate?

  1. Give epinephrine now, since a flat EtCO₂ means no cardiac output
  2. Advance the tube 2 cm and listen again at the next rhythm check
  3. Remove the tube, ventilate by bag-mask, and replace the airway
  4. Raise the ventilation rate to bring the EtCO₂ back up

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Falling output lowers EtCO₂ gradually as compressions tire. A sudden drop to zero with high-quality compressions, absent breath sounds and epigastric sounds points to the airway, not the circulation.
  • B. Advancing helps a tube that has backed up toward the cords, but epigastric gurgling with no breath sounds means the tip is in the esophagus; pushing it deeper keeps the patient unventilated for another 2 minutes.
  • C. Correct. The findings show a tube that has slipped into the esophagus. Remove it, oxygenate by bag-mask or a supraglottic airway, then place a new airway and confirm it with waveform capnography.
  • D. Faster breaths cannot raise the EtCO₂ when the tube no longer sits in the trachea, and overventilation in arrest raises intrathoracic pressure and lowers venous return.

Hypoxia is one of the Hs, and a displaced tube is a common cause of it during resuscitation. Waveform capnography is recommended both to confirm tube placement and to monitor it continuously, and the position is rechecked after every move or transport. A gradual fall in EtCO₂ suggests tiring compressions or falling output. A sudden loss of the waveform while compressions stay high quality, with no breath sounds and gurgling over the stomach, means esophageal displacement. The fix is to remove the tube, oxygenate with a bag-mask device or supraglottic airway, and replace the airway with capnography confirmation, keeping any compression pause under about 5 seconds.

Sources: 2025 Korean CPR Guidelines: Adult ALS; AHA 2025 Part 9: Adult ALS; AHA ACLS Provider Manual (2025)

Question 5 · Post-Cardiac Arrest Care

After ROSC, a patient with an inferior STEMI and cardiogenic shock undergoes emergent angiography. It shows an occluded right coronary artery and severe stenoses in the left anterior descending and circumflex arteries. Which revascularization strategy is recommended NOW?

  1. Defer all PCI until neurologic recovery is known
  2. PCI of the culprit right coronary artery only
  3. PCI of all three vessels while access is in place
  4. Routine temporary MCS before treating any vessel

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because brain injury often decides the outcome after arrest, but STEMI with shock warrants emergent reperfusion regardless of coma, and delay risks further instability and rearrest.
  • B. Correct. In shock after arrest, initial revascularization of only the infarct-related artery is recommended over immediate multivessel PCI.
  • C. Tempting because complete revascularization seems to rescue the most myocardium, but immediate multivessel PCI in shock raised 30-day death or renal replacement therapy in CULPRIT-SHOCK, with similar harm in the arrest subgroup.
  • D. Tempting because the patient is in shock, but routine MCS after cardiac arrest does not improve survival and adds bleeding and ischemic vascular complications (class 3, no benefit).

Multivessel disease is found in up to half of patients who undergo angiography after out-of-hospital arrest. The CULPRIT-SHOCK trial randomized patients with infarct-related cardiogenic shock, 55% of them after out-of-hospital arrest, to culprit-only PCI or immediate multivessel PCI; immediate multivessel PCI had higher mortality and more complications, including in the arrest subgroup. The AHA therefore rates immediate non-culprit revascularization as class 3 (no benefit), consistent with the 2021 revascularization and 2025 ACS guidelines, so the other lesions are reassessed and treated selectively later.

Sources: AHA 2025 Part 11: Post-Cardiac Arrest Care

Question 6 · Special Situations (LVAD/Peds)

A 70-year-old found on his kitchen floor after a night without heat responds only to pain. Data are: epitympanic thermistor temperature 26 °C (78.8 °F), sinus bradycardia 32/min, BP 82/48 by arterial line with a steady waveform, weak carotid pulse. Forced-air warming and warmed IV fluids are running. A resident asks for transcutaneous pacing. What is the BEST response?

  1. Give atropine 1 mg IV and repeat in 3 to 5 minutes
  2. Start transcutaneous pacing at a rate of 60/min
  3. Keep rewarming and hold off on pacing or atropine
  4. Begin chest compressions for the rate below 40/min

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because atropine is first-line for symptomatic bradycardia, but most drugs work poorly in a cold heart, and repeated doses can accumulate until rewarming.
  • B. Tempting because the rate is very slow, but hypothermic bradycardia is physiologic and settles as the core warms, so pacing adds stimulation without treating the cause.
  • C. Correct. Bradycardia and hypotension at this temperature are responses to the global fall in metabolism and need no treatment beyond rewarming.
  • D. Tempting because the rate and pressure are low, but he has a palpable pulse and an arterial waveform, and compressions are for a patient without signs of circulation.

In moderate or severe hypothermia, bradycardia and low blood pressure reflect the general slowing of cellular metabolism, so they need no specific treatment other than rewarming; supraventricular arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation also tend to resolve as the patient warms. Drugs are largely ineffective in a cold heart, and interventions such as central line placement can trigger arrhythmias, so the team handles him gently, insulates him and continues active rewarming. ECLS rewarming is the escalation if the circulation fails, and AHA 2025 finds it may be reasonable below 28 °C.

Sources: Paal et al., Accidental Hypothermia: 2021 Update; AHA 2025 Part 10: Special Circumstances

Question 7 · Basic Life Support (BLS)

A 58-year-old man is found unresponsive and pulseless on the floor of a shower stall, lying in standing water with the shower still running. A nurse starts compressions as a colleague arrives with the AED. What should the team do BEFORE the pads are applied?

  1. Apply the pads at once and have the team stand clear of the water
  2. Move him out of the water onto dry floor and dry his chest
  3. Switch to front-and-back pad placement to keep the pads away from the water
  4. Hold the AED until he can be lifted onto a dry stretcher

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Speed matters, but standing clear does not fix the problem: the patient is lying in water and his chest is wet, and the guideline says not to use the AED on a wet surface.
  • B. Correct. The Singapore 2026 guideline says to make sure the patient is not on a wet or metal surface and to wipe a wet chest dry before the pads go on.
  • C. Changing pad position does not take the patient out of the water or dry the skin, so it does not solve the problem.
  • D. Moving him to a nearby dry floor is enough. Waiting for a stretcher adds minutes before the first shock and takes staff away from compressions.

Resuscitation normally starts where the patient is found, but a site that is unsafe or unsuitable is a reason to move the patient to a safe, flat space as soon as possible. Standing water under the patient and a wet chest are both on the guideline's list of things to correct before pads go on. Rescuers kneeling in the water are also at risk. Dragging him a short distance onto dry tile and toweling the chest costs a few seconds, and compressions restart right away while the pads are placed.

Sources: Lim et al.: Singapore BCLS and AED Guidelines (2026); Korean CPR Guidelines 2025 Part 3: Adult BLS

Question 8 · Rhythm Recognition

A 61-year-old with metastatic lung cancer becomes pulseless on an oncology unit. The monitor shows a narrow-complex rhythm at 118/min. Data are: neck veins distended, breath sounds equal, trachea midline. During a pulse check, ultrasound shows a large pericardial effusion with a collapsed right ventricle. CPR and epinephrine continue. Which intervention is MOST likely to restore circulation?

  1. A rapid 1-L crystalloid bolus to fill the right ventricle
  2. Pericardiocentesis while compressions continue
  3. Fibrinolytic therapy for a presumed massive pulmonary embolism
  4. Needle decompression of both sides of the chest

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Volume can briefly support filling in tamponade, but it does not remove the fluid compressing the heart, so circulation is unlikely to return without drainage.
  • B. Correct. Tamponade is a reversible T, and draining the effusion relieves the pressure that keeps the heart from filling; the procedure runs alongside CPR rather than pausing it.
  • C. Massive PE causes PEA with a dilated right ventricle; here the right ventricle is collapsed by surrounding fluid, the hallmark of tamponade rather than a clot.
  • D. Distended neck veins also occur with tension pneumothorax, but equal breath sounds, a midline trachea and an effusion on ultrasound point to the pericardium, not the pleura.

Organized electrical activity without a pulse sends the team searching for the Hs and Ts while CPR and epinephrine continue. A narrow, fast complex suggests a mechanical cause that ultrasound can show, such as tamponade, tension pneumothorax, hypovolemia or massive pulmonary embolism, whereas a wide, slow complex suggests a metabolic or toxic cause such as hyperkalemia. The 2025 Korean guideline notes that an experienced operator can use ultrasound to diagnose tamponade, provided the scan does not interrupt CPR. Because the effusion is what stops the heart from filling, draining it is the treatment most likely to restore output.

Sources: 2025 Korean CPR Guidelines: Adult ALS; AHA 2025 Part 9: Adult ALS

Question 9 · ACLS Pharmacology

A patient found with pinpoint pupils, RR 4, cyanosis, and a weak pulse of 52 has a used syringe nearby. According to the opioid-associated emergency algorithm, what is the IMMEDIATE management?

  1. Intubate before any other intervention
  2. Start chest compressions at 100–120/min
  3. Give epinephrine 1 mg IV push
  4. Bag-mask ventilate and give naloxone

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Bag-mask ventilation and naloxone come first, and intubation may become unnecessary.
  • B. The patient has a pulse. The priority is ventilation.
  • C. Arrest-dose epinephrine is not indicated in a patient with a pulse.
  • D. Correct. Supporting ventilation and giving naloxone (IV, IM, or IN) reverses the opioid effect.

For opioid-associated respiratory arrest with a pulse, the priorities are to open the airway, provide bag-mask ventilation, and give naloxone (for example, 0.04–0.4 mg IV titrated, or 2–4 mg IN, repeated as needed). If there is no pulse, standard CPR takes priority, and naloxone may be given without delaying compressions. Patients need observation for renarcotization, especially with long-acting opioids.

Sources: AHA ACLS Provider Manual (2025); AHA 2025 Part 10: Special Circumstances

Question 10 · ACLS Algorithms

A 34-year-old has had chest pressure for 1 hour after using cocaine. He is agitated and diaphoretic. Data are: HR 128, BP 176/104, temperature 38.1 °C (100.6 °F), SpO₂ 97%. The 12-lead ECG shows sinus tachycardia with 1-mm ST depression in V4 to V6 and no ST elevation. Aspirin has been given. Which drug is FIRST-line now?

  1. Phentolamine 5 mg IV before any sedation
  2. IV metoprolol to control the rate and pressure
  3. A fibrinolytic for the ischemic ST changes
  4. An IV benzodiazepine, such as lorazepam 2 mg

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Phentolamine may be considered when vasoconstriction or hypertension persists after a benzodiazepine, but it is not the first step, and sedation treats the cause.
  • B. β-blockers are contraindicated in cocaine toxicity: blocking β receptors leaves α stimulation unopposed, which can worsen coronary vasoconstriction and raise the blood pressure further.
  • C. Fibrinolysis is not given for ST depression without ST elevation, and cocaine-related ischemia usually comes from vasospasm and higher demand rather than an occluded artery.
  • D. Correct. Benzodiazepines are first-line in cocaine toxicity because they calm the sympathetic surge behind the tachycardia, hypertension, agitation and heat, which lowers myocardial oxygen demand; nitrates follow if ischemia persists.

Cocaine blocks the reuptake of norepinephrine and dopamine, so heart rate, blood pressure and contractility rise while the coronary arteries constrict and clotting is promoted; chest pain after use can be true ischemia even in young patients. Treatment starts with a benzodiazepine, which lowers the sympathetic drive, along with aspirin as for any suspected ACS. If coronary vasodilation is still needed, nitrates come next; phentolamine may be considered, and persistent hypertension can be treated with nitroprusside or nicardipine. Hyperthermia is treated aggressively with sedation and cooling, and ST elevation would still call for emergency reperfusion.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Cocaine (2026); ACC/AHA ACS Guideline (2025)

Question 11 · Post-Cardiac Arrest Care

Twenty-four hours after resuscitation from cardiac arrest, a ventilated patient on volume control (tidal volume 450 mL, RR 12, FiO₂ 0.4) has an ABG of pH 7.30, PaCO₂ 50 mm Hg, PaO₂ 64 mm Hg, HCO₃⁻ 25 mEq/L, and SpO₂ 93%. Plateau pressure is 20 cm H₂O. Which ventilator change is MOST appropriate?

  1. Increase the RR to lower the PaCO₂ toward 35–45
  2. Give sodium bicarbonate for the acidemia
  3. Increase the FiO₂ to 1.0
  4. Decrease the RR to allow more exhalation time

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. This is an uncompensated respiratory acidosis, and post-arrest care targets normocapnia.
  • B. The acidosis is respiratory and is corrected by adjusting ventilation.
  • C. Oxygenation is acceptable (SpO₂ 93%), and hyperoxia is avoided after arrest.
  • D. A lower rate would reduce minute ventilation and raise the PaCO₂ further.

The pH is acidemic with an elevated PaCO₂ and normal HCO₃⁻, so this is an acute, uncompensated respiratory acidosis from inadequate minute ventilation. After cardiac arrest, targets include normocapnia (PaCO₂ 35–45), since hypocapnia causes cerebral vasoconstriction and hypercapnia raises ICP, and SpO₂ of 90–98%, avoiding hyperoxia. Minute ventilation is adjusted mainly by rate while maintaining a lung-protective tidal volume.

Sources: AHA 2025 Part 11: Post-Cardiac Arrest Care; AHA ACLS Provider Manual (2025)

Question 12 · Special Situations (LVAD/Peds)

A 3-year-old with an arterial line in the PICU has a cardiac arrest. During compressions, the arterial waveform shows a diastolic pressure of 22 mm Hg. The team leader wants to use the arterial line to judge CPR quality. Which diastolic pressure should the team aim for during compressions?

  1. 25 mm Hg or higher
  2. 30 mm Hg or more
  3. 45 mm Hg or higher
  4. 20 mm Hg or more

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because it is the 2025 target, but for infants younger than 1 year; a child aged 1 year or older has the higher target.
  • B. Correct. When invasive arterial pressure is available during CPR, a diastolic pressure of at least 30 mm Hg is a reasonable target in children aged 1 year or older.
  • C. Tempting because a higher number sounds safer, but no guideline sets this target, and chasing it could prompt excess epinephrine or pauses to adjust the line.
  • D. Tempting because 20 mm Hg was once quoted as a minimum relaxation pressure for adults, but it is below the 2025 pediatric targets for both infants and children.

Diastolic pressure during CPR drives coronary perfusion, so the 2025 pediatric guideline added hemodynamic targets for patients who already have an arterial line: at least 25 mm Hg in infants and at least 30 mm Hg in children aged 1 year or older. A reading of 22 mm Hg in this 3-year-old means the team should look first at compression quality (depth of at least one-third of the chest's front-to-back diameter, rate 100 to 120 per minute, full recoil, fewer pauses), confirm epinephrine is on schedule, and keep ventilation from becoming excessive, rather than pausing to troubleshoot the transducer.

Sources: AHA/AAP 2025 Part 8: PALS; Lee et al., Updated Pediatric CPR: Review of the 2025 Guidelines (2026)

Question 13 · Basic Life Support (BLS)

After propofol sedation for an endoscopy, a patient becomes unresponsive with shallow respirations of 4/min and a strong carotid pulse. A nurse starts bag-mask ventilation at about 24 breaths/min. Data after 2 minutes are: HR 58, BP 88/50, SpO₂ 90% and rising, visible chest rise with each breath. Which change is BEST?

  1. Change to a nonrebreather mask now that the SpO₂ is rising
  2. Keep the current rate until the SpO₂ reaches 94%
  3. Slow the bagging to about one breath every 6 seconds
  4. Begin compressions, because the HR is under 60 with a low BP

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A mask helps a patient who is breathing adequately; at 4 shallow breaths/min this patient still needs assisted breaths.
  • B. Oxygenation is improving, but 24 breaths/min is more than twice the adult rescue-breathing rate, and the extra breaths raise intrathoracic pressure.
  • C. Correct. Adult rescue breathing for a patient with a pulse is 1 breath every 6 seconds, about 10/min, each just enough to make the chest rise.
  • D. A heart rate under 60 with poor perfusion is a pediatric trigger for compressions; an adult with a strong pulse needs ventilation, not CPR.

This patient is in respiratory arrest with a pulse, so the task is assisted ventilation at the adult rate: 1 breath every 6 seconds, about 10/min, with the pulse rechecked about every 2 minutes. The Korean 2025 guideline adopted this single rate to simplify BLS. Faster bagging is a common error under stress, and because each positive-pressure breath raises intrathoracic pressure and impedes venous return, overventilation can lower blood pressure in a patient whose circulation is already marginal. The soft BP here is a reason to slow down, not to start compressions.

Sources: Korean CPR Guidelines 2025 Part 3: Adult BLS; AHA 2025 Part 7: Adult BLS

Question 14 · Rhythm Recognition

A resident reads a lead II strip as atrial fibrillation. The ventricular rhythm is irregular at about 90/min, but between QRS complexes the baseline shows identical, evenly spaced negative sawtooth waves at 300/min, with 3 or 4 waves before each QRS. Leads III and aVF show the same waves. Which interpretation is MOST accurate?

  1. Sinus rhythm with frequent nonconducted PACs
  2. Multifocal atrial tachycardia
  3. Atrial flutter with variable AV conduction
  4. Coarse atrial fibrillation with a controlled rate

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. PACs are early P waves interrupting a sinus rhythm; here no sinus P waves are seen, and the atrial waves run continuously at 300/min.
  • B. MAT shows distinct P waves of at least three shapes with a flat baseline between them, at a rate over 100/min, rather than a continuous sawtooth.
  • C. Correct. Uniform negative sawtooth waves at about 300/min in II, III and aVF are typical flutter, and a changing conduction ratio makes the ventricular response irregular.
  • D. Coarse fibrillatory waves can look like flutter, but they vary in shape, size and timing; identical waves at a fixed 300/min are organized atrial activity.

Typical flutter is a macroreentrant circuit around the tricuspid annulus that fires at about 300/min (250 to 350), drawing negative sawtooth waves in II, III and aVF. The AV node passes only some waves, and when the ratio shifts between 3:1 and 4:1 the ventricular rhythm becomes irregular and can be misread as AF. The distinction matters because flutter is often harder to rate-control, cavotricuspid isthmus ablation is effective for recurrent typical flutter, and the 2023 guideline applies the same stroke-risk rules to anticoagulation for flutter as for AF.

Sources: ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF Guideline (2023); AHA 2025 Part 9: Adult ALS

Question 15 · ACLS Pharmacology

Megacode: During refractory VF, the team leader chooses lidocaine instead of amiodarone. Which dosing is correct?

  1. 1–1.5 mg/kg, then 0.5–0.75 mg/kg
  2. 300 mg IV bolus, then 150 mg IV
  3. 0.5 mg/kg IV, then 0.25 mg/kg IV
  4. 5 mg/kg IV, then 2.5 mg/kg IV

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Lidocaine is 1–1.5 mg/kg IV or IO first, then 0.5–0.75 mg/kg.
  • B. These are amiodarone doses.
  • C. These doses are too low.
  • D. These doses are excessive and risk toxicity.

Lidocaine and amiodarone are both acceptable antiarrhythmics for shock-refractory VF/pVT, with similar survival to admission and no proven difference in survival to discharge. Lidocaine is a class IB sodium channel blocker. Toxicity (CNS effects, seizures) is a concern with repeated doses, in liver disease, and in low-output states. After ROSC, a maintenance infusion may be considered.

Sources: AHA 2025 Adult ACLS Algorithms; AHA 2025 Part 9: Adult ALS
