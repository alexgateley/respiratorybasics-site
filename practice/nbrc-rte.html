Question 1 · Patient Data

A patient with a history of heart failure has orthopnea and fine, late-inspiratory crackles at both bases that do not clear with coughing. What do these findings suggest?

  1. Retained secretions in the large airways
  2. Pulmonary edema or interstitial fluid
  3. Upper airway obstruction
  4. Bronchospasm

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Secretions produce coarse crackles or rhonchi that change with coughing.
  • B. Correct. Fine late-inspiratory crackles that persist after coughing come from fluid or fibrosis in the alveoli and small airways; with orthopnea and heart failure, pulmonary edema is likely.
  • C. Obstruction above the thorax produces stridor.
  • D. Bronchospasm produces wheezing.

Breath sound interpretation: fine crackles (fluid, fibrosis, atelectasis), coarse crackles (secretions), wheezes (narrowed lower airways), rhonchi (secretions in large airways), stridor (upper-airway narrowing), pleural friction rub, and diminished or absent sounds (effusion, pneumothorax, consolidation, hyperinflation). Palpation adds tactile fremitus, crepitus and tracheal position; percussion adds dullness and hyperresonance.

Sources: Wilkins' Clinical Assessment in RC, 10th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 2 · Patient Data

A man pulled from a house fire has soot in his nares and an SpO₂ of 83% on a nonrebreathing mask. A multiwavelength pulse CO-oximeter displays a carboxyhemoglobin (SpCO) of 3%. What is the best interpretation of the SpCO?

  1. It is reliable if the plethysmographic waveform is clean
  2. It is accurate, because the device uses more than two wavelengths
  3. It rules out CO poisoning, so the oxygen can be titrated down
  4. It is unreliable at this saturation and cannot exclude poisoning

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A clean waveform shows that the sensor is detecting a pulse, which helps separate motion or perfusion artifact from a true SpO₂. It does not make the carboxyhemoglobin estimate accurate: agreement with blood values in suspected poisoning is wide, and the device can fail outright when the saturation is below about 85%.
  • B. Extra wavelengths let the device estimate dyshemoglobins, but its accuracy falls during hypoxemia, which is exactly when this patient needs a reliable number.
  • C. A low SpCO cannot be trusted at this level of hypoxemia, and stopping high-concentration oxygen on its strength could leave poisoning untreated.
  • D. Correct. Noninvasive SpCO agrees poorly with blood carboxyhemoglobin in suspected poisoning, and in one study the device could not measure carboxyhemoglobin once the SaO₂ fell below 85%. Only a blood sample on a laboratory CO-oximeter (venous is acceptable) can confirm or exclude poisoning, while 100% oxygen continues.

Standard two-wavelength oximeters cannot tell carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin, so they overestimate oxygenation in carbon monoxide poisoning. Pulse CO-oximeters add wavelengths to estimate carboxyhemoglobin and methemoglobin; they track blood values closely in healthy volunteers but show wide error in suspected poisoning (limits of agreement roughly −12% to +14%) and can fail when the SaO₂ is below 85%. Treat the SpCO as a screen at most, and confirm suspected poisoning with laboratory CO-oximetry; levels fall quickly once oxygen is given, so draw the sample early.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 3 · Devices and Patient Safety

During reprocessing, a flexible bronchoscope releases a steady stream of bubbles from the insertion tube during the leak test. What should be done?

  1. Stop reprocessing and send the scope for repair
  2. Seal the area with tape and return the scope to service
  3. Continue with manual cleaning and high-level disinfection as usual
  4. Soak the scope longer in disinfectant to compensate

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A failed leak test means the sheath or a channel is breached. Immersion lets fluid into the scope, damaging it and creating a place for organisms to survive.
  • B. A breached scope cannot be disinfected reliably.
  • C. Immersing a leaking scope causes fluid invasion.
  • D. A longer soak increases the damage.

Bronchoscope reprocessing steps: wipe and flush at the bedside, leak test, manual cleaning with brushing of every channel, high-level disinfection or sterilization, rinsing, drying with forced air, and hanging storage. Each use and each reprocessing cycle is logged to the patient and the scope.

Sources: Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 4 · Devices and Patient Safety

A 47-year-old with bronchiectasis is ordered PEP therapy four times a day. Data are: right chest tube placed 2 days ago for a spontaneous pneumothorax, lung re-expanded on today's radiograph; scattered expiratory wheezes that clear after albuterol; sputum about 40 mL/day; bright red bleeding from the left nostril that began 20 minutes ago. Which finding should lead the therapist to hold the PEP session?

  1. The right-sided pneumothorax diagnosed 2 days ago
  2. The expiratory wheezes heard before the albuterol
  3. The nosebleed that began 20 minutes ago
  4. The sputum volume of about 40 mL a day

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because pneumothorax is on the PEP precaution list, but the listed condition is an untreated pneumothorax. A chest tube with a re-expanded lung means it is treated, so it does not by itself stop therapy.
  • B. PEP is used to reduce air trapping in asthma and COPD and can be given with a bronchodilator. Wheezes that clear with albuterol are a reason to give the bronchodilator first, not to hold PEP.
  • C. Correct. Epistaxis is one of the conditions the AARC guideline says to evaluate before positive airway pressure therapy; raised airway pressure can worsen the bleeding, so the session waits until it is controlled.
  • D. Retained sputum is the indication for PEP. In a patient producing more than 30 mL a day, the guideline judges the therapy by whether it increases that volume.

The AARC guideline on positive airway pressure adjuncts reports no absolute contraindications but names conditions to weigh first: inability to tolerate added work of breathing, ICP over 20 mm Hg, hemodynamic instability, recent facial, oral or skull surgery or trauma, acute sinusitis, epistaxis, esophageal surgery, active hemoptysis, nausea, tympanic membrane rupture or middle ear disease, and an untreated pneumothorax. Here the nosebleed is active, while the pneumothorax already has a drain and a re-expanded lung. Once the bleeding stops, PEP can resume, ideally after the bronchodilator.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Hess, Respiratory Care, 4th ed.

Question 5 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 64-year-old with bronchiectasis not caused by cystic fibrosis is admitted with an exacerbation and thick, purulent sputum that he struggles to bring up. At home he uses an oscillating PEP device twice a day with good technique. The admitting resident, who has seen dornase alfa help patients with cystic fibrosis, asks the therapist to add it by nebulizer. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. A 3-day dornase trial, stopped if his sputum does not loosen
  2. Advise against dornase and keep his oscillating PEP sessions
  3. Nebulized acetylcysteine with albuterol before each session
  4. Dornase 2.5 mg daily, given just before each PEP session

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A short trial sounds like a careful compromise, but the guideline advises against the drug in patients without cystic fibrosis, so a trial still gives him a therapy that the evidence does not support.
  • B. Correct. The AARC guideline on pharmacologic airway clearance states that dornase alfa should not be used in hospitalized patients without cystic fibrosis. His established clearance technique continues while the infection is treated.
  • C. Acetylcysteine is an older mucolytic, and a bronchodilator is usually paired with it because it can provoke bronchospasm. The same guideline does not recommend routine acetylcysteine or routine bronchodilators to help clear secretions.
  • D. Giving the mucoactive drug before the clearance session is the right order in cystic fibrosis, which makes this look correct, but the drug itself is not recommended outside cystic fibrosis, whatever its timing.

Dornase alfa cleaves the DNA released by dying neutrophils, which thins cystic fibrosis sputum, and in cystic fibrosis it is given before the airway clearance session. That benefit does not carry over to other diseases. The 2015 AARC guideline on pharmacologic airway clearance in hospitalized patients states that dornase should not be used in adults or children without cystic fibrosis, and it does not recommend routine acetylcysteine or routine bronchodilators to aid secretion clearance, finding no high-level evidence for mucoactive drugs in these groups. The therapist recommends continuing his familiar oscillating PEP with huffing, treating the infection, and re-evaluating if secretions stay retained.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; CFF Airway Clearance Guideline (2009)

Question 6 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

Twenty minutes after extubation a patient develops a high-pitched inspiratory sound, mild retractions and an SpO₂ of 93% on a cool aerosol mask. What should the therapist recommend first?

  1. Immediate reintubation with a larger tube
  2. Nebulized racemic epinephrine
  3. A sedative to reduce the work of breathing
  4. Nebulized albuterol and ipratropium

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Reintubation is for failure of treatment, and a smaller tube would be used.
  • B. Correct. Post-extubation stridor is from laryngeal edema. Inhaled epinephrine constricts the mucosal vessels and reduces swelling within minutes.
  • C. Sedation can remove the drive that is keeping the airway open.
  • D. Bronchodilators act on the lower airways and do not shrink laryngeal edema.

Post-extubation stridor is treated with racemic epinephrine, an intravenous corticosteroid and close observation, because edema can return as the drug wears off. Heliox can reduce the work of breathing in the meantime. Worsening stridor, fatigue or hypoxemia calls for reintubation.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.

Question 7 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A ventilated patient with resolving pneumonia has a PaO₂ of 142 mm Hg on FiO₂ 0.80 and PEEP 10 cm H₂O. Which change should be made first?

  1. Increase the tidal volume to open more alveoli
  2. Reduce the FiO₂ in steps toward 0.50 or less
  3. Reduce the PEEP to 5 cm H₂O and leave the FiO₂ at 0.80
  4. Leave the settings until the radiograph is clear

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Oxygenation is already excessive.
  • B. Correct. The patient is over-oxygenated on a potentially toxic FiO₂. The FiO₂ is lowered first, and PEEP is reduced once the FiO₂ is at a safer level.
  • C. Removing PEEP first risks derecruitment while the toxic FiO₂ remains.
  • D. Radiographic change lags, and hyperoxia is harmful in the meantime.

Weaning oxygenation support: reduce the FiO₂ to about 0.50 to 0.60 or below, then reduce PEEP in steps of 2 cm H₂O, checking the saturation after each change. High FiO₂ causes absorption atelectasis and oxidant injury, so the target is the lowest FiO₂ that maintains an SpO₂ in the low to mid 90s.

Sources: Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.; AARC CPG: Adult Oxygen in Acute Care (2022); Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 8 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

An alert, competent patient with COPD declines a scheduled bronchodilator treatment, saying it makes her shaky. What should the therapist do?

  1. Tell her that refusing will lead to discharge
  2. Explore her concern, explain the options and respect her decision
  3. Skip the treatment without recording the refusal
  4. Give the treatment anyway, since the physician ordered it on a schedule

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Coercion is unethical.
  • B. Correct. A competent patient may refuse treatment. Understanding the reason often leads to a solution, such as a different dose or drug.
  • C. Respecting the refusal is right, but it and its reason are documented and reported to the prescriber so the plan can change.
  • D. Treating against her wishes violates her autonomy.

Patient-centered care respects autonomy and informed refusal. The therapist explains the benefits and risks in plain language, records the refusal and the reason, notifies the prescriber and suggests alternatives. Cultural background and past experiences shape how patients respond to care.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Joint Commission Performance Goals (2026)

Question 9 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

Under an oxygen titration protocol, a postoperative patient without lung disease has an SpO₂ of 99% on a nasal cannula at 4 L/min. What should the therapist do?

  1. Continue 4 L/min until the physician rewrites the order
  2. Remove the cannula and recheck the saturation tomorrow
  3. Change to a simple mask to keep the SpO₂ at 100%
  4. Reduce the flow in steps, keeping the SpO₂ on target

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The protocol already gives the therapist authority to adjust.
  • B. Oxygen is weaned stepwise with a check after each change.
  • C. A saturation of 100% is not a goal.
  • D. Correct. The protocol authorizes titration to the target range, usually 94% to 98% (92% to 96% in some protocols). A saturation of 99% means the patient is receiving more oxygen than needed.

Therapist-driven oxygen protocols define a target range, the steps for raising and lowering the flow or FiO₂, the reassessment interval and the criteria for stopping oxygen or calling the provider. They reduce both unrecognized hypoxemia and unnecessary oxygen.

Sources: AARC CPG: Adult Oxygen in Acute Care (2022); BTS Emergency Oxygen Guideline (2017)

Question 10 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 70-year-old with COPD arrives in the emergency department with increased dyspnea, wheezing and an SpO₂ of 86% on room air, and he is drowsy but arousable. Which information should the therapist obtain first to guide the level of support?

  1. A blood gas with the mental status assessment
  2. A chest CT scan
  3. A full pulmonary function test
  4. A sputum culture result from the previous admission

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Drowsiness in a COPD exacerbation suggests hypercapnic encephalopathy; the blood gas and mental status decide between controlled oxygen with bronchodilators, noninvasive ventilation, and intubation.
  • B. Imaging follows stabilization and does not determine the ventilatory support level.
  • C. Spirometry is not feasible or useful during an acute exacerbation.
  • D. Cultures take days and do not affect immediate support decisions.

Information gathering in acute chronic lung disease: mental status, work of breathing, blood gases, saturation, peak flow when possible, chest radiograph to exclude pneumonia or pneumothorax, medication history and adherence, home oxygen and ventilation use, prior intubations, and advance directives. These findings determine escalation.

Sources: GOLD COPD Report (2026); Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 11 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 66-year-old with COPD has been ventilated for 4 days and is now on pressure support 14 cm H₂O with PEEP 5 cm H₂O. The respiratory rate is 28/min, VT 540 mL and inspiratory time 1.5 s. On the graphics, inspiratory flow tapers slowly, and airway pressure spikes above the set level at the end of each breath while the patient's abdominal muscles tighten. Which adjustment should the respiratory therapist make?

  1. Raise the pressure support to 18 cm H₂O
  2. Make the flow trigger more sensitive (1 L/min)
  3. Raise the flow-cycle threshold to 45% of peak flow
  4. Raise the PEEP to 8 cm H₂O to offset auto-PEEP

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Higher pressure support enlarges each breath and, in COPD, carries mechanical inspiration even further past the end of the patient's effort. Delayed cycling, active exhalation against the breath and air trapping all get worse.
  • B. Trigger sensitivity controls how breaths begin, not how they end. A 1 L/min trigger does not shorten inspiration and invites auto-triggering from leaks or cardiac oscillations.
  • C. Correct. Ending the breath when flow has fallen to 45% of its peak, instead of the usual 25%, shortens inspiration so the ventilator stops when the patient stops. Expiratory time lengthens and auto-PEEP falls.
  • D. Applied PEEP helps when auto-PEEP makes efforts fail to trigger, which is a problem at the start of the breath. Here every effort triggers; the problem is the breath running past the end of the patient's own inspiration.

In pressure support the breath ends when inspiratory flow decays to a set fraction of its peak, often 25% by default. In COPD the long time constant makes flow decay slowly, so the ventilator keeps delivering gas after the patient's neural inspiration has ended. The patient then exhales actively against the breath, seen as a pressure spike at end-inspiration with expiratory muscle use, and the shortened expiratory time worsens hyperinflation. Raising the expiratory cycle setting, commonly to 40% to 50% in COPD, corrects this delayed cycling.

Sources: Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.; AARC CPG: Patient-Vent Assessment (2024)

Question 12 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 78-year-old with four rib fractures has an incentive spirometry volume that has fallen from 1,200 to 600 mL in 12 hours. She is splinting and cannot cough because of pain. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. A binder wrapped tightly around the chest wall
  2. Chest percussion over the fractures
  3. Sedation so that she can sleep through the night
  4. Better analgesia, such as a regional block

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Binders restrict ventilation and promote atelectasis.
  • B. Percussion is contraindicated over fractures.
  • C. Sedation reduces ventilation and cough further.
  • D. Correct. A falling inspiratory volume in an older patient with rib fractures predicts pneumonia and respiratory failure. Relieving the pain, for example with a regional block, restores the ability to breathe deeply and cough, and lung expansion therapy continues.

Older patients with several rib fractures are monitored with serial inspiratory volumes or vital capacity, pain scores and saturation. A volume under about 15 mL/kg, or a decline, prompts escalation of analgesia, a higher level of care and consideration of noninvasive support.

Sources: ACS ATLS, 11th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 13 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

On the first day after an open right hemicolectomy, a 71-year-old becomes increasingly short of breath. She is alert, follows commands and has a weak cough. Data are: RR 30/min, HR 112/min, BP 134/76 mm Hg, SpO₂ 88% on a 50% air-entrainment mask; ABG pH 7.43, PaCO₂ 36 mm Hg, PaO₂ 56 mm Hg; chest radiograph with bibasilar atelectasis. Her abdomen is soft, and the surgeon finds no sign of an anastomotic leak. What should the respiratory therapist recommend?

  1. Hourly incentive spirometry on her current mask
  2. A nonrebreathing mask at 15 L/min
  3. CPAP or bilevel noninvasive ventilation by face mask
  4. Intubation and lung-protective ventilation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Incentive spirometry helps prevent atelectasis in patients who can take deep breaths, but she already has respiratory failure with a PaO₂ of 56 mm Hg and a rate of 30, and it cannot correct that alone.
  • B. A higher FiO₂ may raise the saturation briefly, but it does nothing for the collapsed alveoli that cause the shunt, and her work of breathing stays high.
  • C. Correct. Positive airway pressure re-expands the atelectatic bases, improves oxygenation and lowers the work of breathing. In postoperative acute respiratory failure it reduces reintubation, pneumonia and mortality.
  • D. She is tachypneic and hypoxemic, so intubation crosses the mind, but she is alert with a normal pH and no shock. A monitored NIV trial is suggested first, with intubation if she does not improve.

Abdominal surgery, anesthesia and postoperative pain reduce lung volumes and cause atelectasis, partly through diaphragm dysfunction that can last up to a week. The ERS/ATS guideline suggests NIV for postoperative acute respiratory failure because CPAP and bilevel NIV both restore aeration and oxygenation, and pooled trials showed lower mortality, intubation and nosocomial pneumonia, particularly after abdominal and thoracic surgery. Surgical complications such as an anastomotic leak or intra-abdominal sepsis must be addressed first, which her surgeon has done. She needs close monitoring and prompt intubation if gas exchange or mental status worsens.

Sources: ERS/ATS Noninvasive Ventilation (2017)

Question 14 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

During a split-night study, a 58-year-old with severe obstructive sleep apnea (diagnostic AHI 52 events/h) has his obstructive apneas and snoring abolished at 11 cm H₂O. At that pressure, central apneas appear (central apnea index 11 events/h) without a crescendo-decrescendo pattern. He has no heart failure, takes no opioids and has a normal awake PaCO₂. What should the respiratory therapist recommend?

  1. CPAP at 11 cm H₂O, then reassess on therapy
  2. Bilevel pressure at 15/11 cm H₂O without a backup rate
  3. Adaptive servo-ventilation from the first night
  4. Add 2 L/min of oxygen into the CPAP circuit

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. These are treatment-emergent central apneas. In most patients they resolve over the following weeks of CPAP, so the pressure that controlled the obstruction is started and the central events are rechecked on the download and, if needed, a repeat study.
  • B. Bilevel pressure may look like a step up from CPAP, but without a backup rate it delivers no breath during a central apnea, and the extra ventilation can lower the PaCO₂ further. The AASM suggests against it for central apnea.
  • C. ASV is an option if central apneas persist despite a period of CPAP, which makes it tempting here. Starting with it treats a finding that usually resolves on its own, at greater cost and complexity.
  • D. Low-flow oxygen is suggested for central apnea from heart failure or high altitude. He has neither, and the obstructive events that drive his illness are already controlled by pressure.

Treatment-emergent central sleep apnea, formerly called complex sleep apnea, is the appearance of central apneas when PAP abolishes obstructive events in a patient whose central events were not prominent at diagnosis. In most cases these central apneas resolve over time with continued CPAP use. The 2025 AASM guideline suggests CPAP for this condition, and says that central apneas persisting after an appropriate CPAP trial may warrant an alternative such as ASV, bilevel pressure with a backup rate or acetazolamide. Because he has no heart failure, opioid use or hypoventilation, the right step is CPAP at the effective pressure with planned reassessment of the central apnea index.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 15 · Clinical Judgment: Pediatric and Neonatal

An 8-month-old former 26-week infant with bronchopulmonary dysplasia came off home oxygen at 5 months. He is admitted on day 2 of bronchiolitis. Data are: RR 54/min, HR 150/min, mild intercostal retractions, SpO₂ 90% to 91% on room air awake and asleep, feeding adequately. The unit follows the 2025 Australasian bronchiolitis guideline. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Low-flow oxygen to keep the SpO₂ at 92% or higher
  2. Room air, since his SpO₂ stays at or above 90%
  3. Heated high-flow nasal cannula because of his chronic lung disease
  4. Nebulized albuterol for the airway reactivity of BPD

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. For infants under 12 months with an underlying condition such as chronic lung disease, the guideline sets the oxygen threshold at a saturation below 92%, so his persistent 90% to 91% calls for low-flow oxygen.
  • B. A 90% threshold is used for otherwise healthy infants aged 6 weeks or more, which makes this tempting. His bronchopulmonary dysplasia puts him in the higher-risk group with a 92% threshold.
  • C. His history raises the risk of severe disease, but high flow is not routine first-line therapy for hypoxemic moderate bronchiolitis; it is considered when low-flow oxygen fails.
  • D. Infants with BPD can have reactive airways, which makes a bronchodilator tempting, but beta-2 agonists are recommended against in bronchiolitis in infants under 12 months, and they do not raise his saturation.

Saturation thresholds in bronchiolitis depend on age and risk. The 2025 Australasian guideline suggests oxygen when the SpO₂ is persistently below 90% in infants aged 6 weeks or more, and below 92% in infants under 6 weeks or under 12 months with an underlying health condition. Prematurity and chronic lung disease are also recognized risk factors for severe bronchiolitis and are reasons to consider admission. Low-flow oxygen is the first step; high flow is added if low flow fails, and bronchodilators, steroids and antibiotics are not routine. Former preterm infants with BPD also have less reserve, so the therapist watches closely for rising work of breathing.

Sources: AAP Bronchiolitis Guideline (2014); Merck Manual Professional: Bronchiolitis (2026)
