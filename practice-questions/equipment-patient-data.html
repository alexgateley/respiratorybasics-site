Question 1 · Devices and Patient Safety

A ventilator fails the leak portion of its pre-use test with a new circuit attached. What should the therapist do?

  1. Place it on the patient and compensate with a larger tidal volume
  2. Bypass the test, since new circuits rarely leak
  3. Send the ventilator to biomedical engineering at once
  4. Reseat the chamber and connections, then repeat the test

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A ventilator that fails its check is not placed on a patient.
  • B. The test also measures circuit compliance for volume compensation.
  • C. The circuit is the usual cause and is checked first.
  • D. Correct. Most leak failures come from the humidifier chamber, water traps, temperature probe ports or a loose limb. The test must pass before the ventilator is used.

A pre-use check (operational verification) is run before each new patient and after a circuit change. It tests for leaks, measures circuit compliance and resistance, and checks sensors and alarms. A unit that still fails after the circuit has been corrected is removed from service and tagged.

Sources: Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.; Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.

Question 2 · Devices and Patient Safety

A patient who uses oxygen at 2 L/min plans a 3-hour drive on a hot July day to visit family, with stops for meals along the way. He wants to keep two spare E cylinders in the trunk so the car is not cluttered. What should the therapist advise?

  1. Keep them in a padded box in the trunk, with the valves closed
  2. Lay them flat in the trunk, wedged with luggage so they cannot roll
  3. Secure them in the passenger cabin, and take them along at stops
  4. Leave them in the parked car with the windows open a few inches

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Closed valves and padding are sensible, which makes this tempting, but a box inside a trunk is two enclosures, and patient guidance warns against storing oxygen in either.
  • B. Keeping them from rolling is right, but a trunk is a closed space where heat builds and oxygen from a leak cannot disperse, and patient guidance specifically says not to store oxygen in a trunk.
  • C. Correct. Cylinders belong in a ventilated space away from heat, not in a trunk or other enclosure, and a car parked in summer can pass the 125 °F (52 °C) level above which cylinders should not be kept. Holding them secure stops them rolling or striking each other.
  • D. Open windows give some airflow, but a car parked in summer sun can still exceed the temperature at which cylinder pressure rises to unsafe levels, so cylinders are not left in a parked vehicle.

MedlinePlus advises patients not to store oxygen in a car trunk, a box or a small closet, because air needs to move freely around it, and to keep it away from heat sources. Cylinder handling guidance adds that temperatures above about 125 °F (52 °C) should be avoided because heat raises the internal pressure, and that cylinders are secured, in racks or dedicated holders or by chains, so they cannot fall or roll. OSHA likewise keeps cylinders away from radiators and other heat and stores them in well-ventilated places. For a drive, that means small cylinders secured in the passenger cabin, valves closed when not in use, and nothing left in a car parked in the sun.

Sources: MedlinePlus: Oxygen safety; Respiratory Therapy Zone: Oxygen Cylinder Sizes and Duration (2026); OSHA 29 CFR 1910.253: Oxygen Cylinder Handling

Question 3 · Patient Data

A 66-year-old former smoker with COPD has lost 6 kg in 4 months. When the backs of matching fingers are pressed together, the diamond-shaped gap between the nails is gone, and the nail-fold angle now exceeds 180 degrees. Neither change was present a year ago. Which evaluation should the therapist recommend?

  1. Overnight oximetry to assess for nocturnal hypoxemia
  2. Chest imaging to look for a lung tumor
  3. Spirometry to stage the progression of the COPD
  4. Blood cultures and an echocardiogram for endocarditis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Hypoxemia is a reasonable concern in COPD, but low oxygen does not explain new clubbing with weight loss, and testing for it would delay the search for a tumor.
  • B. Correct. New clubbing with weight loss in a former smoker points first to lung malignancy, the most common pulmonary cause of clubbing in adults.
  • C. Airflow obstruction is not a recognized cause of clubbing, so restaging the COPD would not explain the new finding.
  • D. Endocarditis can cause clubbing, but nothing here suggests infection (no fever or murmur), and malignancy is far more likely in this patient.

Loss of the Schamroth window and a profile (Lovibond) angle above 180 degrees indicate true digital clubbing rather than simple nail curving. Clubbing usually signals important underlying disease, and lung malignancy is the leading pulmonary cause, accounting for about 80% of clubbing from thoracic tumors. Other causes include bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, lung abscess, empyema, interstitial lung disease such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, asbestosis, cyanotic heart disease and endocarditis. COPD by itself does not cause clubbing, so new clubbing in a patient with COPD should prompt a search for cancer or bronchiectasis rather than be blamed on the COPD.

Sources: StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf); Wilkins' Clinical Assessment in RC, 10th ed.

Question 4 · Devices and Patient Safety

A new blood gas analyzer runs automated electronic quality control every 4 hours, checking its sensors and electronics. The laboratory manager proposes stopping all testing of liquid control material. What is the BEST response?

  1. Replace them with daily duplicate patient samples on a second analyzer
  2. Keep running liquid controls at regular set intervals
  3. Replace liquid controls with monthly tonometered whole blood
  4. Stop liquid controls while electronic checks pass

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Duplicate analysis on a second instrument is a useful additional check, but it compares two analyzers that could share a bias with each other and is not a substitute for control material of known values.
  • B. Correct. Electronic quality control monitors only instrument performance. Liquid controls pass through the sample path like a patient specimen and test the whole analytic process, so they remain part of the plan at defined intervals.
  • C. Tonometry is the reference method for checking true accuracy when a question arises, but a monthly check is far too infrequent for routine quality control and is labor-intensive.
  • D. Tempting because electronic checks are frequent and automatic, but they bypass the sample path, the sensor's chemistry with a real fluid and the operator's handling, so errors there would go unseen.

The AARC guideline on blood gas analysis notes that electronic quality control monitors only equipment performance and that non-electronic controls should also be used at periodic intervals to evaluate the testing process. Liquid control material with known target values travels through the same probe, tubing and sensor chamber as a patient sample, so it detects clots, protein buildup, bubbles and fluidic faults that an electronic simulator cannot. Laboratories set the frequency and number of levels from regulatory requirements and the manufacturer's recommendations, and they document every run.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.

Question 5 · Patient Data

A supine portable radiograph of a ventilated trauma patient shows an abnormally deep, lucent left costophrenic angle that extends toward the abdomen. No pleural line is seen at the apex, and the peak pressure has been rising. What does the finding suggest?

  1. Left lower lobe collapse behind the heart
  2. A left pneumothorax (deep sulcus sign)
  3. A normal variant caused by a deep inspiration
  4. A left pleural effusion layering along the posterior chest

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Collapse appears as a retrocardiac density with volume loss.
  • B. Correct. In a supine patient pleural air rises to the anterior and basal pleural space, producing the deep sulcus sign instead of an apical line.
  • C. With rising pressures in a trauma patient the sign cannot be dismissed.
  • D. A supine effusion causes a hazy veil of increased density, not lucency.

A supine pneumothorax is easy to miss. The deep sulcus sign, a sharply outlined heart border or diaphragm and a hyperlucent upper abdomen are clues. Ultrasound (absent lung sliding) or CT confirms it. A ventilated patient with a pneumothorax needs a chest tube, and signs of tension call for immediate decompression.

Sources: Wilkins' Clinical Assessment in RC, 10th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 6 · Devices and Patient Safety

Before a sedation case in the emergency department, a respiratory therapist checks an E cylinder of nitrous oxide that has been used for several earlier procedures. At room temperature its gauge reads the same pressure it showed when the cylinder was new. Which conclusion about the contents is correct?

  1. The gauge has failed, because use should have lowered the pressure
  2. It is still full, because the pressure has not fallen
  3. The volume is the gauge pressure times the cylinder's tank factor
  4. Liquid remains, and weighing the cylinder estimates the contents

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A failed gauge is worth ruling out when a reading looks wrong, but a steady pressure despite use is expected with nitrous oxide while liquid remains; a new gauge would show the same reading.
  • B. With an oxygen cylinder an unchanged pressure would mean it is still full, but nitrous oxide is stored largely as a liquid, so the pressure stays the same while the liquid is used up.
  • C. Pressure times a tank factor estimates the contents of compressed gases such as oxygen and air. For nitrous oxide the gauge pressure stays constant while liquid remains, so it does not track the amount left until the liquid is gone.
  • D. Correct. While any liquid remains, more nitrous oxide vaporizes as gas leaves, holding the pressure steady. Comparing the cylinder's current weight with its empty weight shows how much is left.

Oxygen and air are stored as compressed gases, so the amount left can be calculated from the gauge pressure. Nitrous oxide is stored as both liquid and compressed gas: as gas is drawn off, more liquid vaporizes to replace it, and the pressure stays constant for as long as liquid remains. In an E cylinder the pressure starts to fall only when about 400 L of nitrous oxide is left, so a steady gauge says little about how much has been used. The contents are tracked by weight instead, by weighing the cylinder before and after use or comparing its weight with the empty weight (about 5.9 kg for an E cylinder without its valve).

Sources: OpenAnesthesia: Cylinder Content, Weight vs. Pressure; Kraybill et al., Nitrous Oxide Delivery at Stanford (2025)

Question 7 · Devices and Patient Safety

During a home visit, a therapist reviews oxygen safety with a 71-year-old who uses a concentrator at 3 L/min by nasal cannula. Which of her practices should the therapist correct?

  1. Reheating meals in the microwave while wearing the cannula
  2. Petroleum jelly she applies inside her nostrils at bedtime for dryness
  3. Keeping a spare cylinder under the bed, with open air space beneath
  4. Running an electric space heater about 8 feet from her chair

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Cooking near the stovetop or oven is a hazard, so kitchen use sounds risky, but microwave cooking is usually fine for a patient on oxygen.
  • B. Correct. Petroleum-based products on the face or upper body are avoided unless a therapist or provider approves them, because they are flammable in oxygen-enriched air. Water-based lubricants or aloe vera are safer for dry nostrils.
  • C. Storage under a bed is acceptable when air can move freely underneath; the warning is against enclosed spaces such as trunks, boxes and small closets.
  • D. Space heaters are on the list of heat sources, which makes this tempting, but the advice is to keep oxygen about 6 feet away from them, and 8 feet meets it.

MedlinePlus home oxygen guidance covers fire, heat and storage. Nobody smokes where oxygen is used, no-smoking signs are posted, and the home has working smoke detectors and a fire extinguisher. Oxygen is kept about 6 feet from open flames and from heat or spark sources such as space heaters, wood stoves, candles, electric blankets, hair dryers and electric razors. Oil-, grease- or alcohol-based cleaners are kept away, and petroleum products such as petroleum jelly are not used on the face or upper body unless approved, because they burn readily; water-based products or aloe vera are used instead. Cylinders are not stored in trunks, boxes or small closets, though under a bed is acceptable if air moves freely, and microwave cooking is usually safe.

Sources: MedlinePlus: Oxygen safety

Question 8 · Patient Data

A patient ventilated for pneumonia is on pressure support of 10 cm H₂O with PEEP 5 cm H₂O. Over 2 hours his respiratory rate has risen from 22 to 31/min. Data are: airway occlusion pressure (P0.1) 1.6 → 4.8 cm H₂O; maximal inspiratory pressure −58 → −57 cm H₂O; compliance and airway resistance unchanged. What does the change in P0.1 BEST indicate?

  1. Declining strength of the inspiratory muscles
  2. Pressure support set higher than the patient needs
  3. Rising airway resistance from secretions or bronchospasm
  4. Increased central respiratory drive

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Strength is shown by the maximal inspiratory pressure, which is unchanged; the occlusion pressure measures how hard the respiratory center is driving the muscles, not their maximum.
  • B. Excess support unloads the muscles and tends to lower drive and effort, which would bring the occlusion pressure down rather than triple it.
  • C. The occlusion is too brief for airflow or the patient's mechanics to shape the reading, and resistance here is unchanged; a higher resistance would show as a wider peak-to-plateau gap.
  • D. Correct. The occlusion pressure is read in the first 100 ms of an effort against a closed airway, before the patient can react, so it reflects the drive from the respiratory center.

P0.1 is the fall in airway pressure 0.1 second after the start of inspiration against an occluded airway. Because the occlusion is imperceptible and does not change the breathing pattern, it is taken as an index of central respiratory drive that is largely independent of respiratory mechanics, and it correlates with inspiratory effort. Most modern ventilators measure it automatically. A tripling while strength and mechanics hold steady means something is driving breathing harder, such as fever, pain, anxiety, acidosis, hypoxemia or worsening pneumonia, and that the patient may be making excessive effort on the current support. Studies disagree on how well P0.1 alone predicts extubation outcome, so it is one piece of the assessment.

Sources: Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.

Question 9 · Patient Data

A patient in cardiogenic shock (cardiac index 1.6 L/min/m²) has cold, bluish fingers and toes, but the tongue and inner lips are pink. An arterial sample analyzed by CO-oximetry shows an SaO₂ of 95% and a methemoglobin of 1%. Which explanation BEST fits the blue extremities?

  1. Methemoglobinemia from nitrate infusion
  2. A falsely low reading from poor peripheral perfusion
  3. Central cyanosis from an intrapulmonary shunt
  4. Peripheral cyanosis from slow flow and high oxygen extraction

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Nitrates can cause methemoglobinemia, and it does cause cyanosis, but the CO-oximeter shows a normal methemoglobin and the discoloration would be generalized.
  • B. Poor perfusion can make a finger oximeter unreliable, but the color is a physical finding and the saturation here came from an arterial CO-oximetry sample, not a probe.
  • C. A shunt would lower the arterial saturation and turn the mucous membranes blue; the SaO₂ is 95% and the tongue is pink.
  • D. Correct. With a low cardiac output, blood moves slowly through the extremities and the tissues extract more oxygen, so capillary blood there is desaturated even though arterial blood is not.

Cyanosis needs at least about 5 g/dL of unsaturated hemoglobin, which in central cyanosis corresponds to an arterial saturation below about 85%; central cyanosis affects the whole body, including warm areas such as the tongue and mucous membranes. Peripheral cyanosis of the lips, fingers and toes reflects low blood flow with increased oxygen extraction while the arterial saturation stays normal, as in low cardiac output. If vigorous massage improves nail bed color, the cyanosis is likely peripheral. In this patient the treatment target is perfusion, not more inspired oxygen.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 10 · Devices and Patient Safety

At the 14:00 quality-control run on a blood gas analyzer, the level 2 pH control reads −3.4 SD. The last acceptable run was at 06:00, and 31 patient samples were analyzed and reported in between. The therapist replaces the sensor cartridge, and repeat controls are now within limits. Which further action is required?

  1. Evaluate the patient results reported since 06:00 for error
  2. Double the frequency of quality-control runs on this analyzer
  3. Recall and repeat the samples analyzed in the past 24 hours
  4. Resume patient testing, since the 06:00 run validated earlier results

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Federal laboratory rules require that patient results from the failed run and from the period since the last acceptable run be evaluated to decide whether they were affected, with corrective action and documentation.
  • B. Closer monitoring may be a reasonable addition, but it looks forward only. It does not address the 31 results already in the medical record.
  • C. Samples analyzed before 06:00 were bracketed by an acceptable run, and stored blood gas samples are no longer valid hours later, so blanket repeat analysis is neither required nor meaningful.
  • D. Tempting because the earlier run passed, but the error could have started at any point after 06:00. The passing repeat controls show the analyzer is now in control, not that it was in control all day.

When controls fail, the analyzer's accuracy is unknown back to the last run that passed, because the fault could have begun at any time in that window. The laboratory therefore reviews the results reported since then, for example by comparing them with clinical findings, looking for implausible pH values, or reanalyzing retained specimens on a second analyzer if any remain valid, and notifies prescribers if a result is judged wrong. Every step, from troubleshooting to the review, is documented. More frequent quality control narrows this window, which is one reason blood gas controls are run at least every 8 hours of testing.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.
