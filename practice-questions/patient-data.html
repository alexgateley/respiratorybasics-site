Question 1

After 3 days of nasogastric suction, a patient's arterial blood gas on room air is pH 7.51, PaCO₂ 49 mm Hg, HCO₃⁻ 38 mEq/L and PaO₂ 78 mm Hg. The nurse asks whether the high PaCO₂ means the patient needs noninvasive ventilation. What should the therapist explain?

  1. It reflects chronic CO₂ retention with renal compensation
  2. It reflects a mixed metabolic and respiratory alkalosis
  3. It is expected compensation for metabolic alkalosis
  4. It signals primary hypoventilation that needs BiPAP

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Chronic retention with renal bicarbonate gain leaves the pH acidic or low-normal; a pH of 7.51 means the bicarbonate excess is the primary disorder.
  • B. A coexisting respiratory alkalosis would push the PaCO₂ below the expected range, not above 40.
  • C. Correct. The expected PaCO₂ is 0.7 × 38 + 20, about 47 (±5) mm Hg, so 49 is appropriate respiratory compensation, not ventilatory failure.
  • D. A primary respiratory acidosis would lower the pH; this pH is alkalemic, so the raised PaCO₂ is a response to the alkalosis, and ventilatory support would only remove the compensation.

A pH above 7.45 with a bicarbonate of 38 is a primary metabolic alkalosis, here from loss of gastric hydrochloric acid. The lungs compensate by hypoventilating, and the expected PaCO₂ is about 0.7 × HCO₃⁻ + 20 (±5), roughly 42 to 52 mm Hg, so 49 fits; the mild hypoxemia follows from that hypoventilation. Because the alkalosis is chloride-responsive (urine chloride usually below 10 mEq/L), it is corrected with fluid, chloride and potassium, which lets the PaCO₂ fall on its own.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf)

Question 2

A 66-year-old with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis has been stable for months and reports no new symptoms at a clinic visit. Arterial blood gas on room air: pH 7.44, PaCO₂ 30 mm Hg, HCO₃⁻ 20 mEq/L, PaO₂ 64 mm Hg. Which interpretation is correct?

  1. Acute respiratory alkalosis with a superimposed metabolic acidosis
  2. Compensated respiratory acidosis from chronic CO₂ retention
  3. Primary metabolic acidosis with appropriate respiratory compensation
  4. Chronic respiratory alkalosis with expected renal compensation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. If the alkalosis were acute, the bicarbonate would fall only 1 to 2 mEq/L to about 22 to 23, and 20 would suggest an added acidosis; months of stable disease make the chronic rule the right one.
  • B. Respiratory acidosis requires a high PaCO₂ with a rising bicarbonate; this patient has a low PaCO₂ and a low bicarbonate.
  • C. The low bicarbonate is tempting, but a primary acidosis would leave the pH below 7.40, and Winter's formula would predict a PaCO₂ of about 36 to 40, not 30.
  • D. Correct. The pH is on the alkaline side with a low PaCO₂, and a fall of 10 mm Hg in PaCO₂ should lower the bicarbonate by 4 to 5 mEq/L when chronic, to about 19 to 20; it is 20.

Start with the pH: 7.44 is on the alkaline side of 7.40, and the PaCO₂ of 30 is low, so the primary process is respiratory alkalosis. Then test the compensation. In acute respiratory alkalosis the bicarbonate falls only 1 to 2 mEq/L for each 10 mm Hg drop in PaCO₂, while in chronic respiratory alkalosis the kidneys lower it by 4 to 5 mEq/L for each 10 mm Hg, which predicts about 19 to 20 mEq/L here. The measured 20 fits the chronic rule, so no second disorder is needed to explain it, and the months of stable disease agree. If the problem were a metabolic acidosis, Winter's formula (1.5 × 20 + 8 = 38 ± 2) would predict a PaCO₂ of 36 to 40 with an acidic pH.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Acid-Base Disorders (2025)

Question 3

A patient with bullous emphysema has a total lung capacity of 5.1 L by helium dilution and 7.4 L by body plethysmography. Which statement explains the difference?

  1. A leak during helium dilution lowered the measured volume
  2. Plethysmography overestimates volume whenever the DLCO is reduced
  3. The patient exhaled incompletely inside the plethysmograph
  4. Helium dilution misses poorly ventilated, trapped gas

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A leak loses helium and makes the calculated volume too large.
  • B. The diffusing capacity has no effect on the plethysmographic measurement.
  • C. That would not create a difference of more than 2 L between methods.
  • D. Correct. Dilution methods measure only gas that communicates with the airways during the test. The plethysmograph measures all compressible gas in the thorax, so the 2.3 L difference is trapped gas.

Lung volumes are measured by helium dilution, nitrogen washout or body plethysmography (Boyle’s law). In obstructive disease with air trapping the gas methods underestimate, and plethysmography is preferred. Panting too fast in the box can overestimate volume in severe obstruction.

Sources: Wilkins' Clinical Assessment in RC, 10th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 4

A 70-year-old with heart failure has increasing dyspnea. Over the right lower chest the therapist finds a dull percussion note, diminished breath sounds and reduced tactile fremitus, and egophony is heard just above the upper edge of the dull area. Which condition do these findings suggest?

  1. Fluid in the right pleural space
  2. Right lower lobe consolidation
  3. Bronchospasm in the right lower lobe airways
  4. Right-sided pneumothorax with lung collapse

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Fluid between lung and chest wall dulls the percussion note and damps both breath sounds and fremitus, while the compressed lung at the top of the fluid gives egophony there.
  • B. Consolidation is also dull to percussion, which makes it tempting, but solid lung conducts vibration well, so fremitus increases and breath sounds become bronchial rather than diminished.
  • C. Narrowed airways produce wheezing and prolonged exhalation; they do not make the percussion note dull or remove fremitus over one area.
  • D. Air in the pleural space also lowers breath sounds and fremitus, but the percussion note is hyperresonant, not dull.

Pleural fluid separates the lung from the chest wall and absorbs transmitted vibration, so percussion is dull and breath sounds and tactile fremitus are reduced over it; egophony is loudest at the superior border, where compressed lung sits against the fluid. Large effusions can also fill the intercostal spaces. Consolidation differs because airless lung conducts sound: fremitus rises and breath sounds turn bronchial. Heart failure is a common cause of transudative effusions, and imaging or bedside ultrasound confirms the fluid before any thoracentesis.

Sources: StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 5

A 71-year-old with COPD takes extended-release theophylline, and last month his serum level was 12 mcg/mL. Four days ago ciprofloxacin was started for an exacerbation, and albuterol is given every 4 hours. Today he has nausea, vomiting, a tremor and sinus tachycardia at 128/min. Data are: potassium 3.0 mEq/L, glucose 212 mg/dL, serum theophylline 27 mcg/mL. Which explanation is MOST likely?

  1. Beta-agonist effects from frequent albuterol treatments
  2. Stress hyperglycemia and hypokalemia from systemic corticosteroids
  3. Theophylline toxicity from slowed clearance due to ciprofloxacin
  4. A therapeutic theophylline level, with symptoms from the infection

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Frequent albuterol can add tremor, tachycardia and a lower potassium, which makes it tempting, but it does not explain persistent vomiting or a theophylline level that has more than doubled.
  • B. Steroids for the exacerbation can raise glucose and lower potassium, but they do not cause vomiting and tachycardia of this degree or raise the theophylline level.
  • C. Correct. Ciprofloxacin inhibits the liver enzyme that clears theophylline, raising levels by about 40% on average, and a level of 27 mcg/mL with vomiting, tachycardia, hypokalemia and hyperglycemia is toxicity.
  • D. Bronchodilation occurs from 5 to 20 mcg/mL; at 27 mcg/mL adverse reactions become more frequent and severe, so the level is not therapeutic.

Theophylline is cleared mainly by hepatic cytochrome P450 1A2, and the prescribing information lists ciprofloxacin as inhibiting that enzyme and raising serum levels by about 40% on average, and a new illness or worsening chronic illness can slow clearance further, so a patient stable for months can become toxic within days. Bronchodilation occurs from 5 to 20 mcg/mL, most benefit comes from peaks of 10 to 15 mcg/mL, and above 20 mcg/mL adverse reactions become more frequent and severe: persistent vomiting, arrhythmias and seizures, with sinus tachycardia, hypokalemia and hyperglycemia common in toxic patients. The label calls for a level whenever toxicity is suspected or a drug that alters clearance is added or stopped, so the therapist should report the result for the dose to be held, with ECG and electrolyte monitoring.

Sources: Greene et al., Theophylline Toxicity (Turk J Emerg Med 2018)

Question 6

An asthmatic’s personal best peak expiratory flow is 480 L/min. Today the reading is 210 L/min with chest tightness. Which zone is this and what does it mean?

  1. Yellow zone; add a rescue bronchodilator and step up therapy
  2. Red zone; rescue bronchodilator and emergency care
  3. Green zone; continue usual therapy
  4. The reading is invalid without a spirometer

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Yellow is 50% to 79%; 210 is 44%.
  • B. Correct. 210 ÷ 480 = 44%, in the red zone, indicating a severe exacerbation that needs immediate bronchodilator therapy and medical evaluation per the action plan.
  • C. Green is 80% to 100% of personal best.
  • D. Peak flow meters are validated home monitoring tools.

Asthma action plans use peak flow zones tied to personal best: green (80% to 100%), yellow (50% to 79%), red (below 50%). Peak flow measures large-airway obstruction and is effort-dependent; the best of three attempts is recorded. Technique, device cleaning and a written plan are part of patient education.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Wilkins' Clinical Assessment in RC, 10th ed.

Question 7

A patient with COPD on 2 L/min nasal cannula has a steady SpO₂ of 89% and had a PaCO₂ of 56 mm Hg yesterday. Today's arterial sample reached the laboratory with a large air bubble left in the syringe. It reads pH 7.49, PaCO₂ 34 mm Hg, PaO₂ 112 mm Hg. What is the most likely explanation?

  1. Improved ventilation since yesterday
  2. Air contamination of the sample
  3. Pulse oximeter underreading from poor perfusion
  4. Accidental sampling of venous blood

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A PaO₂ of 112 would give a saturation near 98%, not the steady 89% on the oximeter, and a chronic retainer rarely drops to 34 overnight.
  • B. Correct. Room air has a PO₂ near 155 mm Hg and almost no CO₂, so the bubble raised the PO₂, lowered the PCO₂ and raised the pH.
  • C. Oximeters can misread with poor perfusion, but a steady reading that matches the patient's known baseline is more credible than a gas that contradicts it.
  • D. A venous sample would show a lower PO₂ and a higher PCO₂ than arterial blood, the opposite of these results.

Air exposure is a pre-analytic error: blood with a PO₂ below about 150 mm Hg takes up oxygen from a bubble, while CO₂ diffuses out, so the PCO₂ falls and the pH rises. Results that contradict a stable SpO₂ and known CO₂ retention should be rejected and the sample redrawn: expel air at once with the tip up, cap the syringe, mix by rolling, and analyze promptly. Liquid heparin in an underfilled syringe causes a similar dilution error, which is why lyophilized heparin syringes are preferred.

Sources: StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf); Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 8

A 50-year-old with a body mass index of 33 and intermittent dyspnea completes full pulmonary function tests. Data are: FEV₁/FVC 0.79 (above the lower limit of normal); FVC 68% and FEV₁ 66% of predicted (both below the lower limit); TLC by body plethysmography 88% of predicted (within normal limits); DLCO within normal limits. How should the pattern be classified?

  1. Restrictive pattern from obesity
  2. Non-specific pattern; restriction not confirmed
  3. Obstruction with air trapping (pseudorestriction)
  4. Mixed obstructive and restrictive defect

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Obesity commonly lowers the FVC, so this is tempting, but restriction is defined by a TLC below the lower limit of normal, and this TLC is normal.
  • B. Correct. A low FVC and FEV₁ with a normal ratio raises the question of restriction, but a TLC within normal limits excludes it, and the normal ratio excludes obstruction. That combination is the non-specific pattern.
  • C. Pseudorestriction is a low FVC caused by gas trapped in an obstructed lung, and it comes with a low FEV₁/FVC. The preserved ratio here argues against obstruction.
  • D. A mixed defect needs both a low FEV₁/FVC and a low TLC, and neither is present here.

Spirometry cannot confirm restriction: a low FVC with a normal FEV₁/FVC can come from a small lung, from gas trapped behind narrowed small airways or from a submaximal effort, so lung volumes decide. Under the 2022 ERS/ATS approach each value is read against its lower limit of normal (z-score −1.645). A TLC below that limit (classically under 80% of predicted) defines restriction, a low ratio with a low TLC is a mixed defect, and a low FVC and FEV₁ with a normal ratio and a normal TLC is the non-specific pattern. It is reported as non-specific and interpreted with the clinical picture rather than labeled restrictive. The normal DLCO also argues against parenchymal lung disease.

Sources: ERS/ATS PFT Interpretation Standard (2022); Wilkins' Clinical Assessment in RC, 10th ed.

Question 9

On volume control with a tidal volume of 450 mL and PEEP 10 cm H₂O, a patient’s plateau pressure has risen from 22 to 28 cm H₂O in 24 hours. The difference between peak and plateau pressure is unchanged at 6 cm H₂O. What does the trend show?

  1. Static compliance has improved, so PEEP can be weaned
  2. Static compliance has fallen from about 38 to 25 mL/cm H₂O
  3. Airway resistance has increased by about one half
  4. Auto-PEEP has developed and the rate should be lowered

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A higher plateau for the same volume is worse compliance.
  • B. Correct. Static compliance is tidal volume ÷ (plateau − PEEP): 450 ÷ 12 = 37.5, then 450 ÷ 18 = 25. The lungs or chest wall are stiffer.
  • C. Resistance is reflected in the peak-to-plateau difference, which has not changed.
  • D. Auto-PEEP is found with an expiratory hold, not from these numbers.

Static compliance (normally 60 to 100 mL/cm H₂O, somewhat lower in intubated patients) falls with ARDS, pulmonary edema, atelectasis, pneumothorax, mainstem intubation and abdominal distension. A rising peak pressure with a stable plateau instead means higher airway resistance.

Sources: Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.; Wilkins' Clinical Assessment in RC, 10th ed.

Question 10

A sleep study in a patient with heart failure (ejection fraction 25%) shows repeated cycles of gradually increasing then decreasing tidal volume, followed by 20-second pauses with no chest or abdominal effort. How are the events classified?

  1. Obesity hypoventilation syndrome with sustained hypercapnia
  2. Central sleep apnea with Cheyne-Stokes breathing
  3. Obstructive sleep apnea, because airflow stops for more than 10 seconds
  4. Mixed apnea that is treated by raising CPAP until it resolves

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Cheyne-Stokes breathing is usually associated with a low PaCO₂.
  • B. Correct. Absent effort during the pauses makes the events central, and the crescendo-decrescendo cycle is Cheyne-Stokes breathing, which is common in heart failure.
  • C. Obstructive events show continued or increasing effort against a closed airway.
  • D. Mixed events begin without effort and end with obstructed effort; that is not described.

Scoring respiratory events: an apnea is a drop in airflow of at least 90% for 10 seconds; it is obstructive when effort continues, central when effort is absent. Central apnea with Cheyne-Stokes breathing is managed first by optimizing heart failure therapy; after SERVE-HF found higher mortality with adaptive servo-ventilation at an ejection fraction of 45% or less, the AASM 2025 guideline limits ASV in that group to some device types and experienced centers.

Sources: Kaplan et al., AASM 2025 Central Sleep Apnea Guideline and HF (2026); Hess, Respiratory Care, 4th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.
