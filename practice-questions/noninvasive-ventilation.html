Question 1 · Devices and Patient Safety

A 74-year-old with a COPD exacerbation (pH 7.29, PaCO₂ 66 mm Hg) is alert and agrees to bilevel ventilation by oronasal mask. As soon as the therapist straps the mask on, he pulls at it, says he is smothering, and tears it off. He is willing to try again. Which approach BEST improves his acceptance of the mask?

  1. Let him hold the mask to his face before the straps go on
  2. Give a sedating dose of lorazepam before the next attempt
  3. Start at IPAP 20 cm H₂O so his dyspnea eases faster
  4. Fasten the straps snugly before the pressure is turned on

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Having the patient hold the mask against his own face before the straps are secured gives him control and improves comfort and acceptance; the headgear is fastened once he is breathing with the device.
  • B. His anxiety is real, but sedating a hypercapnic patient before NIV is running risks hypoventilation and a lower level of alertness, which NIV needs to be safe; coaching and a hands-on start come first.
  • C. A high start sounds like quick relief, but typical initial settings are about IPAP 10 to 12 and EPAP 5 to 7 cm H₂O, raised as needed; starting high is less comfortable, leaks more and sits at the pressure that inflates the stomach.
  • D. Tempting because a sealed mask seems to deliver support at once, but a strapped mask before he accepts it is what he just fought, and tight straps add discomfort and skin pressure; one or two fingers should fit under them.

Mask intolerance is a common, correctable reason that NIV fails, and the first minutes set the tone. The Merck procedure for noninvasive positive pressure ventilation suggests having patients hold the mask against their own face before the straps are secured, starting at about IPAP 10 to 12 and EPAP 5 to 7 cm H₂O, leaving one or two finger widths under the straps, and reassessing after about 30 minutes. Alertness and cooperation are part of what makes NIV safe, so sedation is not the first answer, and IPAP stays below about 20 cm H₂O to avoid gastric insufflation.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: How To Do Noninvasive Positive Pressure Ventilation (2025); Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 2 · Devices and Patient Safety

A patient with a mild COPD exacerbation (pH 7.33, PaCO₂ 58 mm Hg) is started on bilevel ventilation with an oronasal mask. He is alert and cooperative but keeps pulling the mask off, saying he feels smothered. He breathes comfortably with his mouth closed and has no nausea. Which interface is MOST appropriate to try?

  1. A nasal mask with a forehead support
  2. A helmet
  3. A larger oronasal mask with looser straps
  4. A total face mask

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A nasal mask is suggested for mild acute respiratory failure when a patient is claustrophobic or does not tolerate a facial mask, and it works when the mouth stays closed.
  • B. A helmet encloses the whole head, is noisy, and is less effective at clearing carbon dioxide and synchronizing with the patient's efforts, which matters in hypercapnic failure.
  • C. A larger, loosely strapped mask still covers the mouth and nose, and an oversized cushion leaks, often toward the eyes, which adds discomfort without solving the problem.
  • D. Tempting because it seals well and spares the nasal bridge, but it covers even more of the face, so it is likely to make the feeling of being smothered worse.

Interface intolerance is a leading cause of noninvasive ventilation failure, so the interface is matched to the patient. Oronasal masks are preferred for moderate to severe acute failure because mouth leak is less, but they cause more claustrophobia and carry more aspiration risk. Nasal masks cause less claustrophobia, let the patient talk, cough and drink, and lower the aspiration risk; their weakness is mouth leak, which is why they suit an alert patient with mild failure who keeps the mouth closed. If leak or PaCO₂ worsens, the team returns to a facial interface.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.; ERS/ATS Noninvasive Ventilation (2017)

Question 3 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 57-year-old with obesity hypoventilation syndrome (body mass index 51) and severe obstructive sleep apnea (AHI 58 events/h) started CPAP at 14 cm H₂O 10 weeks ago after an in-laboratory titration. His download shows use of 7.2 h/night, a residual AHI of 3 events/h and a normal leak. He still wakes with headaches and dozes during the day. Data are (awake, room air): pH 7.36, PaCO₂ 55 mm Hg (56 mm Hg before CPAP), HCO₃⁻ 30 mEq/L. Overnight oximetry on CPAP shows an SpO₂ below 88% for 38% of the night without cyclic dips. What should the respiratory therapist recommend?

  1. Change to bilevel NIV and titrate it
  2. Continue CPAP and repeat the ABG in 3 months
  3. Raise the CPAP to 17 cm H₂O
  4. Add oxygen at 2 L/min into the CPAP circuit

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. CPAP has controlled his obstructive events, but his hypercapnia, symptoms and sleep desaturation persist despite good adherence. That is an inadequate response, and NIV adds pressure support to raise his ventilation during sleep.
  • B. His adherence is excellent, and some patients improve slowly, which makes waiting tempting. After 10 weeks of good use his PaCO₂ and symptoms have not changed, and the ATS approach moves patients with an inadequate response to CPAP on to NIV.
  • C. More pressure is the response to residual obstructive events, which makes this tempting. His residual AHI is 3 events/h, so the airway is already open, and more CPAP does not raise his tidal volume or lower his PaCO₂.
  • D. Oxygen is bled into PAP for desaturation that persists once events are controlled, which fits part of the picture. Here the desaturation comes with an unchanged PaCO₂, so oxygen would raise the saturation without treating the hypoventilation behind it.

CPAP keeps the upper airway open, and in most patients with obesity hypoventilation and severe OSA that is enough, which is why the 2019 ATS guideline suggests CPAP first for stable ambulatory patients. CPAP does not add alveolar ventilation, though. When symptoms persist or gas exchange during wakefulness or sleep does not improve on CPAP, the ATS approach changes to NIV. His events are controlled and his adherence is good, yet his PaCO₂ is unchanged and he has sustained desaturation without cyclic events, which points to sleep hypoventilation. Bilevel settings are set during an attended titration, where transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring can be added to oximetry.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 4 · Devices and Patient Safety

A patient on helmet CPAP has a large gas leak at the neck, and the pressure will not stay at the set 10 cm H₂O. Each time a staff member has tightened the underarm straps, the leak eased briefly; he now reports aching and tingling in both arms. What should the therapist do?

  1. Raise the fresh gas flow to make up the leak
  2. Lower the PEEP to 6 cm H₂O to ease the seal
  3. Loosen the straps and refit or resize the collar
  4. Tighten the underarm straps one more notch

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. More flow can partly hold the pressure against a leak, but it does not fix the seal, adds noise and cold gas at the neck, and leaves the tight straps in place.
  • B. A lower pressure leaks less, but the PEEP is part of the treatment and is also needed to keep the helmet inflated, so cutting it trades the leak for less support.
  • C. Correct. Loosening the straps relieves the pressure in his armpits, and a large neck leak is fixed at its source by adjusting the helmet's position or changing to a better-fitting hood or collar; the straps are then pulled downward and secured to the bed, or a restraint over the top holds the helmet down.
  • D. Tightening the straps has helped briefly, which makes it tempting, but the new aching and tingling point to excessive pressure in the armpits, which can compromise blood flow to the arms and break down the skin there.

A helmet seals with a soft collar around the neck rather than on the face, which is why it avoids facial pressure injuries, and it is kept from rising by braces under the arms. The 2026 review of helmet NIV advises that substantial leaks be handled by adjusting the helmet position or choosing another hood or collar size, that excessive pressure on the axillae be avoided, and that the braces be pulled downward and secured to the bed. PEEP of about 10 to 14 cm H₂O is both clinically useful and needed to pressurize the interface, and continuous-flow helmet CPAP needs a washout flow of at least 50 L/min.

Sources: Raoof 2020: High-Flow, NIV and Awake Proning (Chest); Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.

Question 5 · Devices and Patient Safety

A 59-year-old with pneumonia and hypoxemic respiratory failure is on helmet CPAP at 10 cm H₂O from a flow generator delivering 30 L/min at an FiO₂ of 0.6. Over 2 hours his respiratory rate has risen from 24 to 30/min, he reports a headache, and the PaCO₂ has risen from 36 to 47 mm Hg. The neck collar is sealed. Which change is MOST appropriate?

  1. Raise the PEEP valve to 12.5 cm H₂O
  2. Add an HME at the gas inlet
  3. Loosen the neck collar to vent exhaled gas
  4. Increase the fresh gas flow to about 60 L/min

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because more PEEP improves oxygenation in pneumonia, but it does nothing to wash exhaled carbon dioxide out of the hood; the rising PaCO₂ and headache point to rebreathing.
  • B. Humidity matters with dry gas, but a heat and moisture exchanger at the inlet of a continuous-flow system adds resistance and does not remove the exhaled carbon dioxide behind his symptoms.
  • C. A loose collar would let gas escape but would also drop the CPAP level and the FiO₂. The helmet is meant to seal at the neck, and washout comes from the gas flow.
  • D. Correct. The helmet's large internal volume fills with exhaled gas unless fresh gas washes it out; flows below about 40 L/min cause significant carbon dioxide rebreathing, and helmet CPAP is usually run at about 60 L/min.

A helmet encloses the whole head, so its dead space is large and carbon dioxide rebreathing is its main technical problem. In continuous-flow helmet CPAP, the fresh gas flow is what clears exhaled gas: studies found significant rebreathing at 30 L/min and at flows below 40 L/min, and about 60 L/min is used in practice. A rising respiratory rate, headache and a climbing PaCO₂ in a sealed helmet at low flow fit rebreathing. Gas from a Venturi flow generator is dry and loud, so a heated humidifier and ear protection are considered as well.

Sources: Raoof 2020: High-Flow, NIV and Awake Proning (Chest); Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 6 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 55-year-old has five right rib fractures and a pulmonary contusion after a fall. A right chest tube is draining, the lung has re-expanded, and a thoracic epidural controls his pain. He is alert, cooperative and protecting his airway. Data are: respiratory rate 28/min, SpO₂ 90% on 4 L/min nasal cannula, PaO₂ 62 mm Hg, PaCO₂ 38 mm Hg, pH 7.42. Which support should the therapist recommend?

  1. A trial of noninvasive ventilation by oronasal mask
  2. Intubation and ventilation to splint the chest wall internally
  3. A nonrebreather mask and a repeat blood gas in an hour
  4. Hourly incentive spirometry with a 50% aerosol mask

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. He has acute respiratory failure after chest trauma with his pain controlled, a drained pneumothorax and only moderate hypoxemia, which is the setting where the ERS/ATS guideline suggests a cautious trial of NIV.
  • B. Internal splinting with invasive ventilation was once standard for multiple rib fractures, but in a trial comparing CPAP with invasive ventilation the CPAP group had shorter ICU stays and less nosocomial pneumonia.
  • C. More oxygen may raise the saturation, but it gives no support to the work of breathing; in the chest trauma trials NIV lowered the need for intubation compared with oxygen therapy.
  • D. Incentive spirometry is part of routine rib fracture care, but a rate of 28/min with a PaO₂ of 62 mm Hg is respiratory failure, which needs continuous support rather than a self-directed exercise.

The 2017 ERS/ATS guideline suggests NIV for chest trauma patients with acute respiratory failure, a conditional recommendation based on moderate-certainty evidence. Pooled trials showed fewer intubations, less nosocomial pneumonia and shorter ICU stays, and of the two trials that compared CPAP with invasive ventilation for rib fractures, one found shorter ICU stays and less pneumonia with CPAP and the other found no difference. The panel advises a cautious trial when pain is controlled and hypoxemia is not severe, so analgesia comes first. Because positive pressure can turn an undrained pneumothorax into a tension pneumothorax, the working chest tube matters before any mask is applied.

Sources: ERS/ATS Noninvasive Ventilation (2017); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 7 · Devices and Patient Safety

A 58-year-old with obesity hypoventilation syndrome uses nocturnal bilevel ventilation through a nasal mask. He has a head cold with a blocked nose. For 3 nights the device download has shown a high leak, and he wakes breathing through his mouth with a dry throat. He can take a mask off by himself. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Add a chin strap to keep his mouth closed while he sleeps
  2. Stop the bilevel ventilation until the cold has resolved
  3. Raise the IPAP so the device makes up for the leak
  4. Use an oronasal mask until his nasal congestion clears

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A chin strap is the usual fix for mouth leak with a nasal mask, which makes it tempting, but with a blocked nose it forces him to breathe through an obstructed airway; nasal interfaces are unsuitable while the nose is congested.
  • B. Pausing avoids the discomfort, but he uses the device to treat nocturnal hypoventilation, and several nights without it leave that untreated when a simple interface change would work.
  • C. More pressure through a blocked nose pushes more gas out of the open mouth, which raises the leak and dries the throat further without improving ventilation.
  • D. Correct. A nasal mask needs an open nose and a closed mouth, and congestion defeats both. A temporary change to a mask covering the nose and mouth restores support during the cold, and it is safe because he can remove it himself.

Nasal masks are often the first choice for home noninvasive ventilation but are unsuitable for patients with nasal congestion or who breathe through the mouth, because an open mouth causes unintentional leak that lowers the delivered pressure and dries the airway. A temporary switch to an oronasal mask during a cold is a recognized solution. Masks that cover the mouth should not be used in patients who cannot remove them themselves, so the stem's detail about his hands matters. Chin straps help when the nose is clear and the mouth falls open. After the change, the leak on the download and his symptoms are rechecked.

Sources: Hess, Respiratory Care, 4th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 8 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 71-year-old with metastatic lung cancer has a do-not-intubate order and is receiving comfort-focused care. He is alert but breathless at rest on 4 L/min of nasal oxygen and a low-dose morphine infusion, with an SpO₂ of 93%, and rates his dyspnea 8 out of 10. His daughter arrives tomorrow, and he says he wants to breathe more easily while staying awake enough to talk with her. Which therapy should the therapist suggest to the team?

  1. A nonrebreather mask at 15 L/min to raise his oxygen level
  2. Doubling the morphine infusion until he is resting quietly
  3. A trial of noninvasive ventilation for comfort
  4. Heliox by mask to lower the work of breathing from the tumor

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. His SpO₂ is already 93%, so more oxygen adds little, a high-concentration mask gives no support to the work of breathing, and in the larger trial in advanced cancer NIV relieved breathlessness more than oxygen therapy did.
  • B. Opioids are a foundation of dyspnea palliation, but titrating to quiet rest risks the sedation he wants to avoid; NIV relieved dyspnea while reducing the morphine requirement.
  • C. Correct. The ERS/ATS guideline suggests offering NIV to relieve dyspnea in terminal cancer; in trials it lowered dyspnea scores and the morphine needed, which fits his wish to stay alert.
  • D. Heliox lowers turbulent-flow resistance in narrowed large airways, but nothing here points to central airway obstruction, and it gives no ventilatory support.

The 2017 ERS/ATS guideline suggests offering NIV to dyspneic patients for palliation in terminal cancer or other terminal conditions, a conditional recommendation with moderate-certainty evidence. Pooled trials showed lower dyspnea scores and lower morphine requirements, so NIV can ease breathing without the sedation that higher opioid doses bring, which matters for a patient whose goal is to stay awake. The guideline describes patients whose goal is comfort, some of whom want to stay lucid to communicate while they await relatives, and the panel stresses careful patient selection and staff training. NIV here is a ceiling of care consistent with a do-not-intubate order, and its use should be guided by his comfort.

Sources: ERS/ATS Noninvasive Ventilation (2017)

Question 9 · Devices and Patient Safety

A 58-year-old with pneumonia has received helmet NIV from an ICU ventilator for 2 hours at PEEP 12 and pressure support 12 cm H₂O. Data are: RR 32/min falling to 24/min, SpO₂ 95% on FiO₂ 0.5 (was 89% on 0.6), less accessory muscle use, less dyspnea, displayed tidal volume 1,150 mL (16 mL/kg predicted body weight). A resident says the large tidal volume shows the helmet is failing and asks to intubate. What should the therapist tell the resident?

  1. Change to an oronasal mask to measure the volume, then decide
  2. Agree, since large tidal volumes predict NIV failure
  3. Lower the pressure support to reach 6 to 8 mL/kg
  4. The volume includes gas that inflates the helmet itself

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A mask would give a more meaningful tidal volume, which is tempting, but changing the interface of a patient who is improving risks derecruitment and intolerance just to obtain a number.
  • B. Large exhaled tidal volumes do predict failure with facemask NIV, which is why the resident is worried, but with a helmet the displayed volume does not reflect what the lungs receive, so it cannot be used that way.
  • C. Targeting 6 to 8 mL/kg is the habit from invasive and facemask ventilation, but chasing an unreliable number would cut support in a patient whose effort and gas exchange are improving.
  • D. Correct. The ventilator counts the gas that distends the helmet as well as the gas entering the lungs, so the displayed volume overstates the tidal volume; failure is judged by gas exchange, effort and dyspnea, which are all improving.

A helmet is a large, compliant reservoir around the head. Part of each delivered breath stretches the helmet rather than entering the lungs, so the large values the ventilator displays reflect both, and the 2026 review of helmet NIV advises that tidal volume should not be used to predict failure as it is with facemask NIV. Support is instead matched to inspiratory effort, with typical PEEP of 10 to 14 and pressure support of 8 to 14 cm H₂O, and escalation to intubation is made promptly when helmet NIV fails to improve gas exchange, respiratory effort or dyspnea. Here all three are improving, so the plan continues with close monitoring.

Sources: Raoof 2020: High-Flow, NIV and Awake Proning (Chest); ERS/ATS Noninvasive Ventilation (2017)

Question 10 · Devices and Patient Safety

A therapist is fitting an oronasal mask for a patient who is about to start overnight bilevel ventilation. The patient is sitting on the edge of the bed. Which fitting practice BEST balances the seal against the risk of skin injury?

  1. Tightening the straps until the leak reads zero
  2. Tightening the upper straps more to stop leak at the eyes
  3. Even strap tension, rechecked supine with pressure on
  4. Choosing the next larger size to spread the pressure

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Chasing zero leak leads to overtightening, which drives pressure injury on the nasal bridge; bilevel devices are built to compensate for a modest leak.
  • B. Leak toward the eyes is common and tempting to fix from the top, but uneven tension concentrates force on the nasal bridge, the most vulnerable site; straps are tightened symmetrically and moderately, and a leak at the eyes usually means the size or position needs correcting.
  • C. Correct. The mask settles differently once the patient lies down and the pressure inflates the cushion, so the final adjustment is made supine with the ventilator running, using symmetrical, moderate tension.
  • D. A mask that is too large leaks, often toward the eyes, which invites more tightening; the size is chosen with the manufacturer's gauge instead.

Mask-related pressure injury is one of the most common complications of noninvasive ventilation, and the nasal bridge is the most vulnerable site. Tightening the straps reduces leak but raises the pressure on the skin, so the goal is the least tension that gives an acceptable seal, with the rest of the leak left to the ventilator's compensation. Correct sizing, symmetrical moderate strap tension rather than extra pull on one pair of straps, a final check lying down with the pressure on, and routine skin checks together protect both the seal and the face.

Sources: Hess, Respiratory Care, 4th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.
