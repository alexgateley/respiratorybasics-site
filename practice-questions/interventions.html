Question 1

A therapist arrives on a surgical ward to give a scheduled treatment to a 66-year-old on day 2 after a bowel resection. Since the morning she has started using her neck muscles to breathe and now speaks in short phrases, though she is alert and oriented. Data are (this morning in parentheses): RR 26/min (18), HR 122/min (94), BP 104/62 mm Hg (128/74), SpO₂ 91% on 4 L/min nasal cannula (95% on 2 L/min). The nurse agrees she looks worse but says no value meets the hospital's calling criteria, so she will report it at the handoff in 2 hours. What should the therapist do?

  1. Raise the oxygen to 6 L/min and recheck at the handoff
  2. Call the rapid response team now because of the trend
  3. Give the treatment and document the trend for the handoff
  4. Wait until one of the values crosses a calling criterion

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. More oxygen may lift the saturation, but a rising oxygen need with a faster rate and pulse is a sign of deterioration that calls for a bedside assessment now, not a number to treat and recheck in 2 hours.
  • B. Correct. A staff member's significant concern is a calling criterion in its own right, and any staff member may call; a rising rate, pulse and oxygen need with new accessory muscle use justify it before any single value crosses a threshold.
  • C. Finishing scheduled care and documenting it is routine, which makes it feel safe, but a note brings no one to assess her, and the warning signs before ward arrests often build over hours.
  • D. Numeric thresholds make calling easier, which makes this feel correct, but the criteria also include a staff member's significant concern, and waiting for a number to cross a line lets a clear decline run on.

Rapid response systems exist because ward patients often show signs of decline for hours before an arrest or an unplanned ICU transfer. Typical calling criteria include a heart rate over 140 or under 40, a respiratory rate over 28 or under 8, a systolic pressure over 180 or under 90 mm Hg, an SpO₂ under 90% despite supplemental oxygen, an acute change in mental status and low urine output, and the list also includes any staff member's significant concern. A trend matters as much as a single value: her rate, pulse and oxygen requirement have all risen, her blood pressure has fallen and she has new accessory muscle use after abdominal surgery, which may signal sepsis, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism or fluid overload. While the team comes, the therapist stays with her and supports her oxygenation.

Sources: AHRQ PSNet Primer: Rapid Response Systems

Question 2

A 34-year-old with a severe traumatic brain injury is on day 4 of ventilation, sedated with propofol and fentanyl to a RASS of −4. Overnight his intracranial pressure rose to 28 mm Hg and needed hypertonic saline; this morning it is 24 mm Hg. Data are: FiO₂ 0.40, PEEP 5 cm H₂O, SpO₂ 97%, no vasopressor. At 0700 the unit protocol prompts the nurse to stop the propofol for a spontaneous awakening trial. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Keep the sedation running and defer both trials today
  2. Keep the propofol running and start a breathing trial on PS 5 cm H₂O
  3. Stop the propofol for the awakening trial, since his gas exchange passes
  4. Halve the propofol for a partial awakening, then reassess at noon

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Raised intracranial pressure excludes him from the awakening trial, because sedation is part of its treatment, and it also excludes him from the breathing trial, so both screens are repeated tomorrow.
  • B. Breathing trials under ongoing sedation were the comparison arm in ABC, so this sounds acceptable, but raised ICP also excluded patients from the breathing trial, and at RASS −4 the trial would test little.
  • C. His FiO₂, PEEP and lack of a vasopressor would pass the breathing-trial screen, which makes this tempting, but raised ICP is a separate exclusion from interrupting sedation.
  • D. A partial reduction sounds like a cautious compromise, but any lightening can let the ICP surge again, and the screen bars interrupting sedation at all while the ICP is raised.

In the ABC trial protocol the awakening trial had its own safety screen: sedation was not interrupted when there was an indication for deep sedation (active seizures, alcohol withdrawal or ongoing agitation), neuromuscular blockade, raised intracranial pressure, or a myocardial infarction in the previous 24 hours. His ICP of 24 mm Hg is still above the treatment threshold of 22 mm Hg in the Brain Trauma Foundation guideline, and his sedation is part of controlling it. Raised ICP was also an exclusion on the breathing-trial screen, so passing the oxygenation criteria (FiO₂ 0.50 or less, PEEP 8 or less, no significant vasopressor) does not make him eligible. Both screens are repeated each morning, and the paired trials begin once his ICP is controlled without deep sedation; pairing them gave 14.7 versus 11.6 ventilator-free days in ABC (Girard 2008).

Sources: Girard, ABC Trial (2008); BTF Severe TBI Guidelines, 4th ed. (2016)

Question 3

A 58-year-old woman intubated for 8 days for pneumonia passed a spontaneous breathing trial yesterday afternoon, but her cuff leak test failed, so IV methylprednisolone 20 mg every 4 hours was started 13 hours ago. At 0700 she passes another breathing trial, is alert, coughs strongly and has few secretions. The resident asks for a second cuff leak test and wants to keep her intubated until a leak is present. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Continue the steroid for 24 more hours, then repeat the test
  2. Repeat the leak test and extubate her once a leak returns
  3. Nebulize racemic epinephrine in line, then repeat the leak test
  4. Extubate her now without repeating the leak test

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A longer course sounds more protective, but the guideline's threshold is steroids at least 4 hours before extubation, which she has passed, and each extra day intubated adds ventilator risk.
  • B. Waiting for a leak feels safest, but the guideline does not require a repeat test after steroids, and because a failed test is often a false positive, she could stay intubated with no benefit.
  • C. Nebulized epinephrine treats stridor that appears after extubation; given through the tube, which bypasses the larynx, it does not reach the swollen tissue, and no repeat test is required.
  • D. Correct. She failed the leak test and has had steroids for well over 4 hours; ATS/CHEST, restated by AARC 2024, says a repeat leak test is not required, so she is extubated with close watching for stridor.

An absent or small cuff leak raises the risk of post-extubation stridor and reintubation, and moderate-certainty evidence shows that systemic steroids given before extubation reduce both. ATS/CHEST therefore suggests, for a patient who fails a cuff leak test but is otherwise ready, systemic steroids at least 4 hours before extubation, and states that a repeat cuff leak test is not required; the AARC 2024 guideline restates both points. The test is specific but only moderately sensitive and gives many false alarms, so waiting for a leak to appear can keep a ready patient intubated. Her risk factors, female sex and more than 6 days of intubation, still call for close watching for stridor after extubation.

Sources: AARC CPG: Spontaneous Breathing Trials (2024); Kuriyama et al.: Cuff Leak Test Meta-analysis (2020)

Question 4

A 55-year-old with bronchiectasis that is not due to cystic fibrosis produces a cup of sputum daily and has had three exacerbations this year. She asks whether dornase alfa, which a relative uses for cystic fibrosis, would help. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Dornase alfa twice daily, as in cystic fibrosis
  2. Daily airway clearance, possibly with hypertonic saline, and no dornase alfa
  3. Inhaled corticosteroids as the only treatment
  4. A cough suppressant at night and during the day

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. It is harmful in this group.
  • B. Correct. Regular airway clearance is the foundation of care, and hypertonic saline can help. Dornase alfa worsened lung function and exacerbations in bronchiectasis not caused by cystic fibrosis.
  • C. They do not clear secretions and raise the risk of infection.
  • D. Suppressing the cough retains infected secretions.

Bronchiectasis care includes daily airway clearance with a technique the patient will use, exercise, vaccination, sputum cultures, prompt antibiotics for exacerbations, and long-term macrolide or inhaled antibiotics for frequent exacerbations. Therapies proven in cystic fibrosis do not always transfer to other causes.

Sources: Rau's Resp Care Pharmacology, 11th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 5

A 62-year-old ventilated for 4 days for pneumonia is down to FiO₂ 0.35 and PEEP 5 cm H₂O. Each time his propofol is lightened he becomes delirious on CAM-ICU, climbs toward the bed rail and reaches for the tube, so the team has not been able to start a breathing trial. His pain scores are controlled on low-dose fentanyl. Which sedation change should the therapist suggest at rounds?

  1. Switch to a midazolam infusion to calm the agitation
  2. Add scheduled haloperidol and keep the propofol dose
  3. Start dexmedetomidine in place of propofol
  4. Deepen the propofol to RASS −3 until the delirium clears

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Benzodiazepines quiet agitation but carry a higher delirium risk and slow the return to light sedation and extubation, so PADIS prefers propofol or dexmedetomidine.
  • B. PADIS advises against routine antipsychotics to treat delirium; short-term use may help distressing symptoms, but it does not change the sedative strategy that is blocking the trial.
  • C. Correct. PADIS 2018 suggests dexmedetomidine for delirium in ventilated adults when agitation is what keeps them from weaning or extubation; it gives rousable sedation with little respiratory depression, so it can continue through the breathing trial.
  • D. Deep sedation hides the agitation but stops the awakening and breathing trials that liberation depends on, and the guideline aims for light sedation.

Agitation that returns whenever sedation lightens blocks the awakening and breathing trials that shorten ventilation. PADIS 2018 treats pain first, prefers propofol or dexmedetomidine to benzodiazepines, and suggests dexmedetomidine for ventilated adults whose delirium with agitation precludes weaning or extubation. The 2025 focused update also suggests dexmedetomidine over propofol when light sedation or less delirium is the priority. Because dexmedetomidine causes little respiratory depression, the infusion can continue during the trial and after extubation, with bradycardia and hypotension as the main effects to watch. Antipsychotics are not routine, and deeper sedation works against liberation.

Sources: SCCM PADIS Guideline (2018); SCCM PADIS Focused Update (2025)

Question 6

A 34-year-old was struck in the face with a baseball bat. He is awake and breathing on his own but spitting blood, and pain and swelling limit his mouth opening, so the emergency physician is considering awake nasotracheal intubation. On the therapist's examination, which finding should be raised as a contraindication to the nasal route?

  1. A tender, swollen mandible with the bite out of alignment
  2. Blood pooling in the mouth from a deep tongue laceration
  3. Mouth opening limited to about one finger-breadth by pain
  4. The upper teeth and hard palate move together when grasped

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A mandible fracture is a serious facial injury and tempting to treat as a contraindication, but the lower jaw is not on the nasal path, and these injuries are a common reason for nasal intubation.
  • B. Oral bleeding soils the view for oral laryngoscopy and needs suction, but it does not block a nasal tube, which avoids the mouth altogether.
  • C. Limited mouth opening makes oral laryngoscopy difficult, which is the usual reason a nasal route is considered, so it argues for the nasal route rather than against it.
  • D. Correct. A maxilla that moves as a unit when the upper teeth are rocked signals a midface fracture, and Merck lists midface fractures as an absolute contraindication to nasotracheal intubation because the tube can pass through the fractured skull base or sinuses.

Merck describes nasotracheal intubation as an infrequently used technique for patients who are awake and breathing spontaneously, useful when the mouth cannot be opened well. It is absolutely contraindicated in patients with midface fractures, and known or suspected basal skull fractures are a further contraindication, because a tube passed through the nose can enter the cranial vault or the disrupted sinuses. The therapist checks for midface instability, periorbital bruising and clear nasal drainage before the nasal route is chosen; if any is present, the team plans an oral approach with a video laryngoscope or a surgical airway instead.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Tracheal Intubation (2026); Merck Manual Professional: Airway Establishment and Control (2026)

Question 7

The pressure-volume loop of a patient on volume control shows a flattened upper portion: pressure keeps rising while little volume is added at the end of inspiration. What does this mean, and what should be done?

  1. Increased airway resistance; give a bronchodilator
  2. Active exhalation; increase the sedation
  3. A circuit leak; replace the circuit
  4. Overdistension; reduce the tidal volume

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Resistance widens the loop without flattening its top.
  • B. Active exhalation distorts the expiratory limb.
  • C. A leak makes a loop that does not close on exhalation.
  • D. Correct. The beaked upper end of the loop shows that the lung has reached the flat part of its compliance curve. Extra pressure adds stress, not volume.

Pressure-volume loops show compliance (the slope), resistance (the width), overdistension (a beak at the top), the level at which alveoli open (a lower inflection point) and leaks (a loop that does not close). Overdistension is corrected with a smaller tidal volume or lower pressure.

Sources: Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 8

After failed intubation, a supraglottic airway cannot be seated and mask ventilation produces no chest rise. The SpO₂ is 58% and falling in a patient with massive facial swelling. What is the next step?

  1. A fourth laryngoscopy attempt by the same operator
  2. Emergency cricothyrotomy
  3. A nasopharyngeal airway and high-flow nasal oxygen
  4. Needle decompression of both sides of the chest

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Further attempts consume the remaining time and worsen the swelling.
  • B. Correct. When a patient can be neither intubated nor oxygenated, front-of-neck access is the only remaining way to deliver oxygen and must not be delayed.
  • C. These cannot bypass a completely obstructed upper airway.
  • D. The problem is the upper airway, not the pleural space.

The therapist’s role in a failed airway is to call the situation aloud, hand over the cricothyrotomy kit (scalpel, bougie and a 6.0-mm cuffed tube), and be ready to ventilate and confirm placement with capnography once the airway is in.

Sources: Walls Manual of Emergency Airway, 6th ed.

Question 9

During an in-hospital cardiac arrest, two-person bag-mask ventilation with an oral airway is producing good chest rise at 30:2. A resident asks for compressions to stop so she can intubate, and after 15 seconds of laryngoscopy the tube is still not through the cords. What should the therapist do?

  1. Ask to resume compressions and go back to bag-mask ventilation
  2. Hand the resident a bougie and allow another 15-second attempt
  3. Hold compressions until the tube passes so the view is not lost
  4. Insert a supraglottic airway now while compressions stay paused

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Compressions should not stop for more than 10 seconds, intubation included. Because bag-mask ventilation is working, compressions resume at once, and any further attempt is made without halting them.
  • B. A bougie may help the next attempt, but another 15-second pause on top of the first doubles the interruption; the attempt waits until compressions are running again.
  • C. Losing the view is frustrating, but every second without compressions costs coronary and cerebral perfusion, and effective bag-mask ventilation makes the tube less urgent than the compressions.
  • D. A supraglottic airway is a reasonable rescue device, but placing it during the ongoing pause still prolongs the interruption, and mask ventilation is already effective.

Merck's CPR guidance states that chest compressions must not be interrupted for more than 10 seconds at any time, including for intubation, defibrillation or rhythm analysis, and that compressions and defibrillation take precedence over endotracheal intubation. When bag-mask ventilation produces visible chest rise, the airway can wait, because the breaths are already being delivered and a long pause costs perfusion. An advanced airway, endotracheal or supraglottic, is then placed without stopping compressions, its position is confirmed with waveform capnography, and ventilation switches to 1 breath every 6 seconds with continuous compressions.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: CPR in Adults; AHA 2025 Part 9: Adult ALS

Question 10

A patient with a lung mass and enlarged mediastinal lymph nodes on CT is scheduled for endobronchial ultrasound. What is the main purpose of this procedure?

  1. Lavage of the alveoli for culture
  2. Removal of a foreign body from a main bronchus
  3. Needle sampling of lymph nodes for diagnosis and staging
  4. Measurement of airway resistance

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Lavage is done through a standard bronchoscope.
  • B. Foreign bodies are removed with rigid or flexible bronchoscopy.
  • C. Correct. An ultrasound probe at the tip of the bronchoscope shows nodes beside the airway so that a needle can sample them under direct vision.
  • D. Resistance is measured by pulmonary function testing.

Specialized bronchoscopy: endobronchial ultrasound samples mediastinal and hilar nodes; navigational and robotic bronchoscopy reach peripheral nodules. The therapist prepares equipment, supports the airway (often a supraglottic airway or tube), monitors sedation and labels specimens correctly.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.
