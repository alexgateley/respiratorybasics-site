Question 1 · Patient Data

A 46-year-old with granulomatosis with polyangiitis has new dyspnea, scant hemoptysis and bilateral alveolar opacities. Her hemoglobin has fallen from 11.8 to 9.1 g/dL over 2 days. A single-breath DLCO, with the reference value adjusted for the current hemoglobin, is 138% of predicted. Which process does the high DLCO MOST strongly suggest?

  1. Bleeding into the alveolar spaces
  2. Alveolar edema from fluid overload
  3. Erythrocytosis from chronic hypoxemia
  4. Interstitial fibrosis from the vasculitis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Free hemoglobin in the air spaces takes up the test carbon monoxide, so alveolar hemorrhage raises the measured DLCO even while the blood hemoglobin falls.
  • B. Edema also fills alveoli and causes opacities, but fluid in the air spaces and interstitium impedes gas transfer; it does not raise the DLCO above normal.
  • C. Increased red cell mass does raise the DLCO, but her hemoglobin is falling, not rising, and the reference value was already adjusted for it.
  • D. Fibrosis thickens the alveolar-capillary membrane and shrinks lung volume, which lowers the DLCO rather than raising it.

The 2022 ERS/ATS standard notes that a high DLCO is almost always due to increased pulmonary blood volume (as in a left-to-right shunt), increased hemoglobin (erythrocytosis), or free hemoglobin in any part of the airway, as in alveolar hemorrhage. Reference values must be adjusted for measured hemoglobin, which is why anemia alone would push the uncorrected value down. In a patient with a small-vessel vasculitis, new alveolar opacities, a falling hemoglobin and a high or rising DLCO point to diffuse alveolar hemorrhage. The diagnosis is supported by bronchoalveolar lavage, whose fluid typically becomes more bloody with each sequential aliquot.

Sources: ERS/ATS PFT Interpretation Standard (2022); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 2 · Patient Data

A patient with COPD is booked for spirometry with bronchodilator responsiveness testing at 9:00 AM on Thursday to help confirm the diagnosis. He inhales tiotropium every morning at 8:00 AM, uses fluticasone twice daily and takes albuterol as needed. Which instruction follows the 2019 ATS/ERS withholding times?

  1. Take the last tiotropium dose on Wednesday morning
  2. Take the last tiotropium dose on Tuesday morning
  3. Take Thursday's tiotropium as usual and hold the albuterol
  4. Hold the fluticasone for 24 hours

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Twenty-five hours fits the 24-hour rule for long-acting beta agonists such as salmeterol or formoterol, not the 36 to 48 hours a long-acting muscarinic antagonist needs.
  • B. Correct. Tiotropium is a long-acting muscarinic antagonist, withheld for 36 to 48 hours. A Tuesday 8:00 AM dose leaves 49 hours before the test, while Wednesday's dose would leave 25.
  • C. Continuing maintenance inhalers fits a referral that asks whether lung function can improve beyond current treatment. This test is to help confirm the diagnosis, so the long-acting muscarinic antagonist must be held for 36 to 48 hours or its bronchodilation blunts the measured response.
  • D. Inhaled corticosteroids and leukotriene modifiers need not be withheld; they do not produce the acute bronchodilation that the test measures.

Bronchodilator responsiveness testing measures how much FEV₁ and FVC improve after a bronchodilator, so drugs taken earlier must have worn off or they hide the response. The 2019 ATS/ERS withholding times are SABA 4 to 6 hours, SAMA 12 hours, LABA 24 hours, ultra-LABA (indacaterol, vilanterol, olodaterol) 36 hours and LAMA (tiotropium, umeclidinium, aclidinium, glycopyrronium) 36 to 48 hours. Albuterol is also held for 4 to 6 hours before the appointment. When the referral instead asks whether lung function can improve beyond current treatment, regular medicines may be continued, which is why the ordering clinician states the purpose. Patients are reminded at booking and withholding is confirmed at check-in.

Sources: ATS/ERS Spirometry Standardization (2019)

Question 3 · Devices and Patient Safety

A ventilated patient with copious thick secretions and a core temperature of 31.5 °C is using a heat and moisture exchanger. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Continue the exchanger and increase suctioning
  2. Instill saline through the tube every hour
  3. Add a second exchanger in series
  4. Change to a heated humidifier

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The exchanger is contraindicated with thick or copious secretions and with a body temperature below 32 °C. More suctioning does not fix a device that cannot humidify this patient.
  • B. Routine saline instillation is not recommended and does not replace humidification.
  • C. Stacking exchangers increases dead space and resistance.
  • D. Correct. Heat and moisture exchangers are passive and depend on the patient’s exhaled heat; below a body temperature of 32 °C they deliver inadequate humidity, and thick secretions occlude them. Active heated humidification is indicated.

Device selection by patient condition is a recurring exam theme: heat and moisture exchanger versus heated humidifier, closed versus open suction, small-volume nebulizer versus metered-dose inhaler with spacer versus dry powder inhaler (needs adequate inspiratory flow), and interfaces for noninvasive ventilation (oronasal, nasal, full-face, helmet) chosen for leak, comfort and skin protection.

Sources: Al Ashry: Humidification during mechanical ventilation (2014); Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 4 · Devices and Patient Safety

A patient must be transported to radiology on 4 L/min by nasal cannula using an E cylinder that reads 1,800 psi. Allowing a safe residual of 200 psi, about how long will the cylinder last?

  1. About 64 minutes
  2. About 20 minutes
  3. About 112 minutes
  4. About 4 hours, since E cylinders hold nearly 700 liters

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. This applies the D-cylinder factor (0.16 L/psi) instead of the E factor (0.28 L/psi).
  • B. This drastically underestimates the supply.
  • C. Correct. Duration = (1,800 − 200) × 0.28 ÷ 4 = 112 minutes; the E cylinder factor is 0.28 L/psi.
  • D. That would require a much larger cylinder or a lower flow.

Cylinder factors: D 0.16, E 0.28, G 2.41, H and K 3.14 L/psi. A full E cylinder holds about 625 L at 2,200 psi. Transport planning adds a margin for delays, and liquid oxygen systems use a different calculation based on weight (1 lb of liquid oxygen yields about 344 L of gas).

Sources: Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 5 · Devices and Patient Safety

A patient reports that her dry powder inhaler no longer seems to work. She stores it in the bathroom and exhales into the mouthpiece before each dose. What should the therapist teach?

  1. Keep it dry, exhale away from it, then inhale fast and deep
  2. Attach a valved holding chamber to slow the powder and improve delivery
  3. Rinse the mouthpiece with water after every dose
  4. Shake it well and inhale slowly over 5 seconds

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Moisture from the bathroom and from exhaled breath clumps the powder. A dry powder inhaler also needs a rapid, forceful inhalation to break up and carry the dose.
  • B. Chambers are for pressurized inhalers and do not work with powder devices.
  • C. Water in the device ruins the powder; the mouthpiece is wiped dry.
  • D. That is the technique for a pressurized metered-dose inhaler.

Dry powder inhalers are breath-actuated and need an inspiratory flow of roughly 30 to 60 L/min or more, depending on the device. Teaching points: load the dose, exhale away from the device, seal the lips, inhale fast and deep, hold the breath for up to 10 seconds, and rinse the mouth after an inhaled steroid.

Sources: Rau's Resp Care Pharmacology, 11th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 6 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 58-year-old with community-acquired pneumonia and no chronic lung disease has been on heated high-flow nasal cannula at 60 L/min and FiO₂ 0.60 for 3 hours. He is alert, speaks in full sentences and is breathing more easily. Data are: RR 24/min (was 32/min), SpO₂ 93%, PaO₂ 78 mm Hg, PaCO₂ 34 mm Hg, pH 7.45. The resident asks to change him to bilevel NIV by oronasal mask because NIV oxygenates better. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Intubate now, as a P/F ratio under 150 predicts failure on high flow
  2. Keep the high-flow settings and track the ROX index
  3. Lower the flow to 30 L/min now that his breathing has eased
  4. Change to bilevel NIV by oronasal mask at IPAP 12/EPAP 8 cm H₂O

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A P/F ratio of about 130 is severe, which makes intubation tempting, but no P/F cutoff decides high-flow failure; he is alert, his breathing is easing and his ROX index predicts success. Intubation is for a falling ROX, rising work of breathing or worsening mental status.
  • B. Correct. In hypoxemic failure without hypercapnia the ERS guideline suggests high flow over NIV, and he is responding: his rate has fallen and his ROX index is (93 ÷ 0.60) ÷ 24, about 6.5, above the 4.88 that predicts success.
  • C. Easier breathing shows the support is working, not that it can be withdrawn; at an FiO₂ of 0.60 he is not ready to wean, and protocols usually lower the FiO₂ toward 0.40 before reducing the flow.
  • D. NIV gives a higher mean airway pressure and oxygenates better than high flow, which is the resident's point, but in de novo hypoxemic failure it can drive excessive tidal volumes, and it was linked to higher ICU mortality when the P/F ratio was under 150.

Heated high flow delivers up to 60 L/min of warmed, humidified gas, which meets inspiratory demand so the set FiO₂ is what the patient breathes, washes CO₂ out of the nasopharyngeal dead space and adds an expiratory pressure of roughly 1 cm H₂O per 10 L/min. The 2022 ERS guideline suggests high flow over both conventional oxygen and NIV in hypoxemic respiratory failure. NIV oxygenates better, but in de novo hypoxemia it can generate excessive tidal volumes, and in FLORALI 90-day mortality was lower with high flow than with NIV or standard oxygen. Here the P/F ratio is 78 ÷ 0.60 = 130 and the ROX index is about 6.5; a rising ROX with less work of breathing supports continuing, with reassessment at 6 and 12 hours.

Sources: ERS High-Flow Nasal Cannula Guideline (2022); Raoof 2020: High-Flow, NIV and Awake Proning (Chest)

Question 7 · Patient Data

A 59-year-old has chronic cough and exertional dyspnea that have not improved with inhaled bronchodilators. Data are: FEV₁/FVC above the lower limit of normal; peak expiratory flow 45% of predicted; flow-volume loop with a flat plateau across the expiratory limb and a normal, rounded inspiratory limb; mid-vital-capacity expiratory-to-inspiratory flow ratio (MEF₅₀/MIF₅₀) 0.25. Which lesion do these findings suggest?

  1. Variable intrathoracic obstruction, such as tracheomalacia
  2. Diffuse small-airway obstruction from COPD
  3. Variable extrathoracic obstruction, such as vocal cord paralysis
  4. Fixed obstruction, such as tracheal stenosis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Inside the chest, pleural pressure exceeds airway pressure during forced exhalation and squeezes a floppy segment, capping expiratory flow while inspiration stays normal. An MEF₅₀/MIF₅₀ below about 0.3 fits this lesion.
  • B. COPD scoops the expiratory limb into a concave curve after an early peak and lowers the FEV₁/FVC. It does not cut a flat plateau, and the ratio here is preserved.
  • C. A floppy lesion above the thoracic inlet collapses on forced inspiration, when airway pressure falls below atmospheric, so it flattens the inspiratory limb and gives an MEF₅₀/MIF₅₀ above 1.
  • D. A rigid narrowing limits flow in both directions, flattening both limbs into a box with a ratio near 1. Here the inspiratory limb is normal.

Central-airway lesions flatten one limb of the flow-volume loop into a plateau, and the pressure around the lesion decides which. Outside the chest a variable lesion narrows on forced inspiration, inside the chest it narrows on forced expiration, and a fixed lesion limits both, so the shape localizes the problem. Suspect it when dyspnea or noisy breathing ignores bronchodilators, especially after prolonged intubation or tracheostomy. A peak flow that is low compared with the FEV₁ (Empey index, FEV₁ in mL divided by PEF in L/min, above 10) also points to central obstruction. Loops are insensitive, detecting only about 69% of endoscopically proven obstructions in one series of 475 loops, so CT or bronchoscopy confirms the lesion.

Sources: ERS/ATS PFT Interpretation Standard (2022); Wilkins' Clinical Assessment in RC, 10th ed.

Question 8 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 19-year-old with sickle cell disease is admitted with a pain crisis involving the chest and back. He is taking shallow breaths because of pain. Which therapy helps prevent acute chest syndrome?

  1. Oxygen at 15 L/min whatever the saturation
  2. Withholding opioids to preserve respiratory drive
  3. Incentive spirometry with adequate pain control
  4. Bed rest with breathing kept as shallow as possible to limit pain

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Oxygen is given for hypoxemia, not routinely.
  • B. Uncontrolled pain causes splinting; analgesia is titrated carefully.
  • C. Correct. Splinting leads to atelectasis and hypoxemia, which promote sickling in the lung. Regular deep breathing, every 2 hours while awake, reduces the incidence of acute chest syndrome.
  • D. Shallow breathing is the mechanism of the complication.

Acute chest syndrome is a new infiltrate with fever, chest pain or respiratory symptoms in sickle cell disease. Prevention is incentive spirometry, pain control and mobilization. Treatment adds oxygen for hypoxemia, antibiotics, careful fluids, bronchodilators and transfusion.

Sources: Goldman-Cecil Medicine, 27th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 9 · Patient Data

A 58-year-old man with limb-onset amyotrophic lateral sclerosis has weak lip closure. During maximal inspiratory pressure testing with a flanged mouthpiece, air hisses from the corners of his mouth, and his best effort reads −38 cm H₂O. He is alert and follows instructions well. Which measurement would BEST assess his inspiratory muscle strength?

  1. Maximal inspiratory pressure repeated from functional residual capacity
  2. Sniff nasal inspiratory pressure with one nostril plugged
  3. Maximal expiratory pressure through the same flanged mouthpiece
  4. Vital capacity through the same flanged mouthpiece

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Starting from functional residual capacity is more reproducible, but values are about 30% lower than from residual volume, and the same lip leak remains, so the reading would look even weaker.
  • B. Correct. The sniff is a short, natural maneuver made with the mouth closed and measured at a plugged nostril, so weak lips cannot leak, and the result tracks esophageal pressure.
  • C. Expiratory pressure measures the expiratory muscles, not inspiratory strength, and leaks around the mouthpiece with facial weakness are a known cause of falsely low expiratory values.
  • D. Vital capacity is a useful trend, but lung volume falls only after strength is substantially lost, so it detects weakness later than pressure tests, and the lip leak would still lower it.

Maximal inspiratory pressure is not intuitive and depends on cooperation and a tight seal, so a low value may reflect the method rather than weakness. Sniff nasal inspiratory pressure is measured through a plug in one nostril while the other stays open; the patient sniffs sharply from functional residual capacity with the mouth closed. It is easy to learn, reproducible and reflects esophageal pressure, with lower limits of normal of about 70 cm H₂O in men and 60 cm H₂O in women. It does not replace inspiratory pressure: using both lowers the rate of false-positive weakness by nearly 20%. It underestimates strength with marked airway obstruction or nasal blockage and cannot be used in ventilated patients.

Sources: Caruso et al., Respiratory Muscle Strength Methods (J Bras Pneumol 2015)

Question 10 · Patient Data

A patient performs three forced vital capacity maneuvers with good starts and a plateau at the end of each. The two largest FVC values are 3.10 L and 2.72 L, and the two largest FEV₁ values differ by 0.31 L. What should the therapist do?

  1. Report the largest values, because three acceptable efforts are enough
  2. Coach more maneuvers, up to eight, until repeatability is met
  3. Stop testing and report a restrictive pattern
  4. Average the three efforts and report the mean FVC and FEV₁

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Three acceptable efforts are required and so is repeatability.
  • B. Correct. The two largest FVC and FEV₁ values must agree within 0.150 L. These differ by 0.38 and 0.31 L, so testing continues with coaching, usually to a limit of eight efforts.
  • C. A pattern cannot be reported from data that are not repeatable.
  • D. The largest acceptable FVC and FEV₁ are reported, never averages.

Spirometry quality: at least three acceptable maneuvers (a fast start, no cough in the first second, no early stop, and an end-of-forced-expiration plateau or 15 seconds), with the two largest FVC and FEV₁ within 0.150 L (0.100 L for children 6 and under). The largest FVC and largest FEV₁ are reported even if they come from different efforts.

Sources: ATS/ERS Spirometry Standardization (2019)
