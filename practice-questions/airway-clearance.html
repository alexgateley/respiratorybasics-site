Question 1 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A patient with pneumonia on volume control (FiO₂ 0.50, PEEP 8 cm H₂O, SpO₂ 92%) needs open suctioning. A colleague plans to disconnect the circuit and hyperinflate with a manual resuscitator on 100% oxygen for 1 minute first. What should the therapist recommend instead?

  1. Instill saline down the tube first to loosen the secretions
  2. Preoxygenate through the ventilator by raising its FiO₂
  3. Skip preoxygenation, since the SpO₂ is above 90%
  4. Use the manual resuscitator but fit a PEEP valve set at 8 cm H₂O

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Saline instillation lowers oxygenation and is generally avoided, so it worsens the very problem preoxygenation is meant to prevent.
  • B. Correct. The guideline recommends preoxygenating through the ventilator, either by raising the FiO₂ or with its preoxygenation program, and does not recommend manual ventilation for this purpose.
  • C. A saturation of 92% on FiO₂ 0.5 and PEEP leaves little reserve; the guideline recommends preoxygenation for adults because suctioning without it lowers SpO₂ and PaO₂.
  • D. A PEEP valve limits derecruitment, but manual bagging still gives variable volumes and higher peak pressures, and the circuit is still opened.

The 2022 AARC suctioning guideline found that preoxygenation limits the fall in SpO₂ and PaO₂ that suctioning causes, and recommends it before suctioning adults and children. It is given through the ventilator by increasing the FiO₂ or by using the temporary oxygen-enrichment program that most ventilators provide; an FiO₂ 0.2 above baseline may be enough rather than 1.0. Manual ventilation for preoxygenation is not recommended, because a resuscitation bag produced higher peak airway pressures with no gain in oxygenation, and disconnecting a patient on PEEP lets the lungs derecruit. A closed suction catheter is a further option for patients who desaturate when the circuit is opened.

Sources: AARC CPG: Artificial Airway Suctioning (2022)

Question 2 · Devices and Patient Safety

A patient with bronchiectasis says that his pipe-shaped oscillating PEP device (Flutter) no longer vibrates in his chest when he uses it lying on his side. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Blow harder and faster through the device while lying down
  2. Replace it with an incentive spirometer
  3. Use it sitting upright, with the stem angled to maximize the vibration
  4. Remove the steel ball so that air flows more freely

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Forceful exhalation collapses airways and does not restore the oscillation.
  • B. An incentive spirometer expands the lungs but does not mobilize secretions.
  • C. Correct. This device depends on gravity acting on its steel ball, so it must be held near level and tilted slightly to tune the oscillation.
  • D. The ball is what creates the pressure and the oscillation.

Oscillating PEP combines expiratory pressure of about 10 to 20 cm H₂O with airflow oscillations. The pipe-style device is position-dependent; lever-style devices work in any position. Technique: a larger than normal breath, a 2- to 3-second hold, an active but not forced exhalation for 10 to 20 breaths, then huff coughing.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Hess, Respiratory Care, 4th ed.

Question 3 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A patient with C6 tetraplegia has a weak cough and rattling secretions. He has no abdominal injury, no rib fractures and no vena cava filter. How should the therapist perform a manually assisted cough?

  1. Compress the lateral rib cage at the start of each inspiration
  2. Vibrate the upper chest wall during each exhalation
  3. Push inward and upward below the ribs in time with each cough
  4. Press firmly on the lower sternum while he takes a deep breath in

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Rib compression at the start of inspiration works against lung filling; the assist is timed with expiration and applied to the abdomen.
  • B. Vibration may help loosen secretions, but it adds no expulsive force, which is what a patient without abdominal muscles lacks.
  • C. Correct. The palm goes between the xiphoid and the navel and presses upward and inward in time with his expiratory effort, replacing the force his paralyzed abdominal muscles cannot produce.
  • D. Compression during inspiration limits the breath he needs for an effective cough, and the sternum is not where expulsive force is added.

Patients with cervical cord injury lose the abdominal and intercostal muscles that generate an effective cough, so secretions are retained and atelectasis and pneumonia follow. In a manually assisted cough the clinician places a palm below the rib cage, between the xiphoid and the navel, and pushes upward and inward in time with the patient’s expiration or cough. A deep breath first, from stacked breaths or a resuscitation bag, improves the expiratory flow, and nebulized saline beforehand may thin the secretions. The review lists contraindications: an unstable spine in traction, abdominal complications, rib fractures and a recently placed vena cava filter.

Sources: Respiratory Management in SCI (2013)

Question 4 · Devices and Patient Safety

A 68-year-old with COPD is admitted with an exacerbation. He coughs forcefully but brings up little of the thick sputum he can feel in his chest, and he says the secretions are what make him most short of breath. Data are: SpO₂ 90% on 2 L/min, RR 24/min, alert and cooperative. At home he used an oscillating PEP device and found it helpful. Which plan should the therapist recommend?

  1. Mechanical insufflation-exsufflation four times daily
  2. No airway clearance, as it is not advised in COPD
  3. Resume his own oscillating PEP device
  4. Percussion and postural drainage with a head-down tilt

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Cough augmentation replaces a weak cough, as in neuromuscular disease with a peak cough flow under 270 L/min. This patient coughs forcefully; his problem is moving thick secretions toward the central airways, which an oscillating device addresses.
  • B. Tempting because the guideline does not recommend airway clearance for routine use in COPD, but a trial may be considered when retained secretions cause symptoms. This patient's sputum is his main complaint, which is the situation in which a trial is reasonable.
  • C. Correct. Airway clearance may be considered in COPD with symptomatic secretion retention, with the choice guided by the patient's preference, tolerance and the effect of therapy. A device he already uses well meets all three, and it is stopped if it does not help.
  • D. Chest physiotherapy needs a caregiver and a head-down position that a dyspneic patient may not tolerate. It is not his preference, and it has no better evidence than the device he already uses with benefit.

The AARC guideline on nonpharmacologic airway clearance in hospitalized patients without cystic fibrosis does not recommend airway clearance therapy for routine use in COPD, because the evidence does not show benefit for most patients. It does allow a trial when retained secretions cause symptoms, choosing the therapy by the patient's preference, tolerance and response, and some guidelines it cites suggest oscillating PEP in COPD. Oscillating PEP combines a positive expiratory pressure with airflow oscillations during exhalation, and each session ends with huff coughing to clear what has been loosened.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Hess, Respiratory Care, 4th ed.

Question 5 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 24-year-old with cystic fibrosis coughs up about 300 mL of blood at home and is brought to the hospital. Which change to the respiratory regimen is appropriate?

  1. Stop airway clearance and hypertonic saline for now
  2. Start positive expiratory pressure therapy every 2 hours
  3. Double the vest sessions to clear the blood
  4. Add dornase alfa and nebulized acetylcysteine

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. With massive hemoptysis, vigorous clearance and irritant aerosols can dislodge clot and restart bleeding, so they are withheld until the bleeding is controlled.
  • B. Positive-pressure clearance is also withheld.
  • C. Oscillation and coughing can restart the bleeding.
  • D. Irritant and mucolytic aerosols are held during major bleeding.

Hemoptysis in cystic fibrosis: with scant bleeding (under 5 mL) usual therapy continues; with massive bleeding (over 240 mL in a day) the patient is admitted, given antibiotics, taken off drugs that impair clotting, and airway clearance is held. Bronchial artery embolization is used for unstable or recurrent bleeding.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Des Jardins, Clinical Manifestations, 9th ed.

Question 6 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A drowsy postoperative patient with bibasilar atelectasis has a vital capacity of 8 mL/kg and cannot perform incentive spirometry despite coaching. Which therapy is appropriate?

  1. Incentive spirometry continued at a lower target volume each hour
  2. A nonrebreathing mask at 15 L/min
  3. Positive-pressure lung expansion, such as IPPB or CPAP
  4. Postural drainage with percussion

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. The patient cannot perform the maneuver at any target.
  • B. Oxygen treats the hypoxemia but does not re-expand lung and can promote absorption atelectasis.
  • C. Correct. Incentive spirometry needs cooperation and a vital capacity above about 10 mL/kg. When the patient cannot take a deep breath, positive pressure provides it.
  • D. This treats retained secretions, not low lung volumes.

Lung expansion therapy is matched to the patient: deep breathing and early mobilization for everyone; incentive spirometry for alert patients with an adequate vital capacity; and positive-pressure methods (IPPB, CPAP or positive expiratory pressure) when volumes are too small or the patient cannot cooperate.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 7 · Devices and Patient Safety

A 24-year-old with Duchenne muscular dystrophy uses mechanical insufflation-exsufflation at +40/−40 cm H₂O four times a day. A chest radiograph taken this morning for new chest pain shows a small right apical pneumothorax, and the team has not yet decided whether to drain it. He is due for a cough-assist session. What should the therapist do?

  1. Give the usual session with continuous SpO₂ and heart rate monitoring
  2. Switch on the oscillation setting at a lower amplitude
  3. Hold the session and discuss the plan with the physician
  4. Give the session at +20/−20 cm H₂O to limit the risk

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Monitoring is advised when cardiac instability is a concern, but it does not make the device safe with air already in the pleural space; the risk is enlargement of the pneumothorax, not a drop in saturation alone.
  • B. Adding oscillations still applies positive pressure to the lungs, and studies have not shown that oscillations add benefit to mechanical insufflation-exsufflation.
  • C. Correct. An undrained pneumothorax is listed as a contraindication to mechanical insufflation-exsufflation. The session is withheld and the plan for drainage and for secretion clearance is agreed with the team.
  • D. Tempting because lower pressures seem safer, but any positive-pressure insufflation can enlarge an undrained pneumothorax, and pressures this low also produce an ineffective cough.

Mechanical insufflation-exsufflation applies a deep positive-pressure breath followed by rapid negative pressure. Recognized contraindications include bullous emphysema, undrained pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, recent barotrauma and tracheoesophageal fistula, and a previous pneumothorax or a pulmonary air leak calls for caution. Pneumothorax is also one of the reported complications of the therapy. Once the pneumothorax is drained or resolved, cough assistance is usually resumed, often starting at lower pressures and titrating back up, because these patients depend on it to clear secretions.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Hess, Respiratory Care, 4th ed.

Question 8 · Devices and Patient Safety

A 34-year-old with limb-girdle muscular dystrophy has had two chest infections this winter. Data are: FVC 1.4 L (38% of predicted), peak cough flow 210 L/min, intact bulbar function, and no bullae on a recent CT scan. His family has bought a high-frequency chest wall oscillation vest after reading about it online. Which airway clearance device should the therapist recommend?

  1. The vest twice a day, as his family has planned
  2. A mechanical insufflation-exsufflation device
  3. Intrapulmonary percussive ventilation by mouthpiece
  4. An oscillating PEP device used four times a day

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The family's purchase and the vest's use in cystic fibrosis make it tempting, but the AARC guideline found too little evidence to recommend chest wall compression for neuromuscular weakness, and it does not supply the cough flow he lacks.
  • B. Correct. Cough assist is recommended for neuromuscular disease, particularly when peak cough flow is below 270 L/min. The device gives a positive-pressure breath and then negative pressure at the airway opening, producing the expiratory flow his weak muscles cannot.
  • C. Percussive bursts can loosen secretions, but the trials in neuromuscular disease were small and the evidence is insufficient to recommend it; loosened mucus still has to be coughed out.
  • D. PEP depends on active exhalation against a resistor, and no major medical society recommends it for patients with neuromuscular disease; his expiratory muscles are too weak to use it well.

The 2013 AARC airway clearance guideline makes two recommendations for neuromuscular disease, respiratory muscle weakness or impaired cough: cough assist techniques should be used, particularly when peak cough flow is below 270 L/min, while chest physiotherapy, PEP, intrapulmonary percussive ventilation and high-frequency chest wall compression cannot be recommended because the evidence is insufficient. Expiratory muscle weakness prevents the intrathoracic pressure needed for an effective cough, so mechanical insufflation-exsufflation, which inflates the lungs and then applies negative pressure, addresses the actual problem. Bullae and recent barotrauma are reasons to avoid the device, which is why the CT finding matters.

Sources: CHEST 2023: Respiratory Management in Neuromuscular Weakness; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Hess, Respiratory Care, 4th ed.

Question 9 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 44-year-old with two nondisplaced left rib fractures from a fall is being discharged from the emergency department. His SpO₂ is 97% on room air, and his pain is 4 out of 10 after acetaminophen and an NSAID. He asks for an elastic rib belt so that it will hurt less when he breathes. What should the therapist advise?

  1. Wear the rib belt day and night until the pain settles
  2. Breathe shallowly for a few days to let the ribs knit
  3. Hourly incentive spirometry, starting in 3 days when the pain eases
  4. No rib belt; deep breaths and supported coughs each hour

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A belt does reduce pain from rib movement, which is why patients ask for it, but it constricts breathing and so promotes the atelectasis and pneumonia that rib fracture care aims to prevent.
  • B. Shallow breathing protects against pain, but splinting is exactly how rib fractures lead to atelectasis and pneumonia, especially in older patients.
  • C. Incentive spirometry is part of the plan, but delaying lung expansion for 3 days leaves the period of worst pain and splinting untreated.
  • D. Correct. Care aims at lung expansion: analgesia that allows deep breathing, deep breathing exercises or incentive spirometry, supported coughing and early activity, without strapping.

Pain from rib fractures makes patients splint their breathing, and shallow breaths and a weak cough lead to atelectasis and pneumonia, especially in older patients. Treatment therefore aims at lung expansion: NSAIDs with acetaminophen first, opioids only if those fail, and an epidural or intercostal block for patients who still cannot breathe deeply or cough. Respiratory care includes deep breathing exercises, incentive spirometry, supported coughing, early mobilization and frequent position changes. Immobilizing the ribs in a way that constricts respiration, as strapping or a tight belt does, should be avoided. Patients with three or more fractures or underlying cardiopulmonary disease are admitted.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Rib Fracture (2026)

Question 10 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 23-year-old with cystic fibrosis is in the hospital for a pulmonary exacerbation and uses a chest wall oscillation vest twice a day. This morning she coughed up sputum with a few streaks of bright red blood, less than a teaspoon in all. Her vital signs are stable, and her SpO₂ is 95% on room air. She asks whether she should skip her vest sessions. What should the therapist advise?

  1. Keep her usual vest sessions and report any increase in bleeding
  2. Stop airway clearance until she has gone 24 hours without blood
  3. Change to postural drainage with percussion, which is gentler on the airways
  4. Hold the vest and ask the team about bronchial artery embolization

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Scant hemoptysis, under about 5 mL, is common during cystic fibrosis exacerbations; usual airway clearance continues, and larger or rising volumes are reported at once.
  • B. Stopping seems cautious, but with scant bleeding usual therapy continues, because retained secretions sustain the infection and inflammation that drive the bleeding.
  • C. Clapping on the chest wall is not gentler than a vest, and no change of technique is needed for scant bleeding.
  • D. Embolization is considered for massive hemoptysis, over about 240 mL in 24 hours, together with stopping airway clearance and hypertonic saline; streaks of blood do not call for it.

Chronic airway infection in cystic fibrosis enlarges the bronchial arteries, which bleed easily, so hemoptysis is common, especially during exacerbations. The CF Foundation consensus grades it by volume: scant bleeding, under about 5 mL, is managed by continuing usual therapy, including airway clearance, while massive hemoptysis, over about 240 mL in 24 hours, calls for admission, stopping airway clearance and hypertonic saline, and consideration of bronchial artery embolization. Airway clearance is recommended regularly for every person with cystic fibrosis because retained mucus perpetuates infection and inflammation. She should report bleeding that increases, clots or new breathlessness.

Sources: CFF Airway Clearance Guideline (2009)
