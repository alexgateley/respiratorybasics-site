Question 1 · Devices and Patient Safety

A 24-year-old with cystic fibrosis is admitted with an exacerbation. At home she inhales dornase alfa 2.5 mg once daily from a nebulizer she left at home. The unit stocks ultrasonic nebulizers and one of the jet nebulizer and compressor pairs listed in the dornase label. She asks to mix the dornase with her albuterol to save time. Which plan is BEST?

  1. Dornase and albuterol mixed in one jet cup
  2. Dornase in the ultrasonic nebulizer for a faster treatment
  3. Dornase alone, undiluted, in the listed jet nebulizer
  4. Dornase diluted in 3 mL of saline in the listed jet nebulizer

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Mixing saves time, which is why patients ask, but the label says not to dilute or mix dornase with other drugs in the nebulizer because mixing can change the drug physically or functionally.
  • B. Ultrasonic nebulizers can warm and degrade protein drugs, and the label states there are no data supporting dornase in nebulizer systems other than those it lists. Speed does not justify an untested device.
  • C. Correct. The label calls for one 2.5 mg ampule by a jet nebulizer connected to an adequate air compressor (or a listed mesh system), not diluted and not mixed with other drugs. Albuterol is given in its own treatment.
  • D. The device is right but the preparation is not: the label says not to dilute dornase. The ampule is already the full dose in a volume suited to the listed nebulizers.

Dornase alfa is a recombinant enzyme that cleaves the DNA released by neutrophils in purulent cystic fibrosis sputum, which lowers its viscosity. Because it is a protein, how it is aerosolized matters: the Pulmozyme label lists the jet nebulizer and compressor combinations and mesh systems that were tested and says there are no data for others, and AARC's aerosol device guide advises against ultrasonic nebulizers for proteins and suspensions because the vibrating crystal can heat and degrade them. The label also forbids diluting dornase or mixing it with other drugs in the cup. Albuterol is usually given first in the cystic fibrosis sequence, before hypertonic saline, dornase and airway clearance, and inhaled antibiotics come last.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 2 · Devices and Patient Safety

Thirty minutes after a ventilator circuit change, a heated humidifier's low airway-temperature alarm sounds. Data are: chamber water level normal; chamber outlet temperature 37 °C; airway temperature display 24 °C; inspiratory limb warm to the touch. The airway temperature probe is hanging in room air beside the Y-piece rather than sitting in its port. What should the therapist do FIRST?

  1. Replace the humidifier chamber, which is not heating
  2. Raise the airway temperature set point by 3 °C
  3. Seat the probe back in its airway port at the Y
  4. Switch to a heat and moisture exchanger for the time being

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A chamber that is not heating would show a low outlet temperature. Here the outlet reads 37 °C, which shows the heater plate and chamber are working.
  • B. Raising the set point answers a false reading. Once the probe is reseated, the higher setting would deliver hotter gas than intended, and the controller would already be driving the heater wire hard against the room-air reading.
  • C. Correct. The chamber is heating to 37 °C and the limb is warm, so the 24 °C display is room air read by a displaced probe. Reseating it restores accurate control and clears the alarm once the reading settles.
  • D. An exchanger is a reasonable backup if the humidifier truly fails, but nothing here has failed. Changing humidification type for a misplaced probe adds dead space and another circuit break.

Servo-controlled heated humidifiers use two probes: one at the chamber outlet that controls the heater plate, and one near the Y-piece that controls the heated wire so the gas reaches the patient at the set temperature. Low-temperature alarms after a circuit change usually come from an empty chamber, a probe that is off or out of its port, or a new circuit still warming up. A probe reading room air makes the controller heat the wire harder, so gas can reach the patient warmer than intended until the probe is reseated; conversely, a probe left inside an incubator or under a radiant warmer reads falsely high and the heater turns down. Checking the probe positions is part of every circuit change.

Sources: Al Ashry: Humidification during mechanical ventilation (2014); Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.

Question 3 · Patient Data

A patient newly prescribed a controller inhaler nods and says he understands the instructions. He has trouble reading the printed handout. What is the best way to confirm that he has learned the technique?

  1. Ask whether he has any questions before he leaves today
  2. Have him sign a form stating that teaching was done
  3. Have him show the technique and teach it back
  4. Provide a more detailed handout to read at home

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Patients with low literacy often say they have none.
  • B. A signature documents the session, not the learning.
  • C. Correct. Teach-back with a return demonstration shows what the patient can do, and it does not depend on reading ability.
  • D. More text does not help someone who struggles to read.

Assess learning needs before teaching: literacy, preferred language (using a qualified interpreter and not a family member), learning style, and cultural or social factors. Use plain language, pictures and demonstration, limit each session to a few key points, and confirm with teach-back at every visit.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 4 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

Five minutes into a nebulized albuterol treatment, a patient’s heart rate rises from 96 to 148/min with frequent premature ventricular contractions and tremor. What should the therapist do?

  1. Add ipratropium to the nebulizer to slow the heart rate
  2. Switch the driving gas to oxygen at a higher flow rate
  3. Finish the treatment, since most of the dose has already been delivered
  4. Stop the treatment, monitor and notify the provider

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Ipratropium does not slow the heart.
  • B. This does not address the drug effect.
  • C. The remaining dose adds to the adverse effect.
  • D. Correct. A marked rise in heart rate with ectopy is an adverse reaction to the beta agonist. The treatment ends, and the prescriber decides on the dose, interval or drug.

A treatment is stopped for a severe adverse response: a heart rate increase of more than about 20%, arrhythmia, chest pain, severe tremor, bronchospasm, desaturation or a change in consciousness. The therapist stays with the patient, documents the event and recommends a change in the care plan.

Sources: Rau's Resp Care Pharmacology, 11th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 5 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 52-year-old admitted with an asthma exacerbation was comfortable after her last albuterol treatment 4 hours ago, with scattered wheezes and an SpO₂ of 95% on room air. One minute into the next nebulized treatment, using the first vial from a newly opened box, she becomes tight-chested and coughs, her wheezing becomes louder and more widespread, and the SpO₂ falls to 89%. What should the therapist do?

  1. Add a second albuterol vial to the cup to give a larger dose
  2. Stop the albuterol and get an alternative treatment ordered
  3. Finish the treatment, as wheezing often grows louder as airways open
  4. Switch the rest of the albuterol from nebulizer to MDI with spacer

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. More bronchodilator is the reflex response to bronchospasm, but if albuterol itself is provoking the spasm, a larger dose adds to the harm.
  • B. Correct. Bronchospasm that worsens immediately after a dose, especially with the first use of a new vial or canister, is paradoxical bronchospasm; the label says to stop albuterol at once and start alternative therapy, while oxygen is given and the prescriber is called.
  • C. Louder wheezing can mean better air movement in a silent chest, but she had air movement before, and new chest tightness with a fall in SpO₂ within a minute of the dose points to a reaction to the drug.
  • D. A spacer improves delivery and is often a good choice, but the problem here is the drug, not the device; the label carries the same warning for inhaled albuterol in any form.

The albuterol label warns that the drug can produce paradoxical bronchospasm that may be life threatening and that it frequently occurs with the first use of a new canister or vial. When it happens, albuterol is stopped immediately and alternative therapy is started. The AARC aerosol device guide, summarized on the app's aerosol page, likewise lists paradoxical bronchospasm as a reason to stop a treatment. The clues are timing and direction: she was stable, then worsened within a minute of starting the dose. The therapist should stop the treatment, give oxygen to restore her saturation, stay with her and notify the prescriber so that another bronchodilator can be chosen.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 6 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 67-year-old with COPD uses a long-acting beta agonist and antimuscarinic inhaler and, since last month, ensifentrine 3 mg twice daily by jet nebulizer with a mouthpiece. At a clinic visit she says that when she suddenly becomes breathless she nebulizes an extra ensifentrine ampule, and on busy mornings she pours it into the cup with her albuterol. What should the therapist teach?

  1. Change to a mesh nebulizer so the extra dose works faster
  2. Use albuterol for sudden breathlessness, not extra ensifentrine
  3. Mixing the two in one cup is fine if she nebulizes it right away
  4. She can stop the dual inhaler, since ensifentrine replaces it

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The label directs use with a standard jet nebulizer and a mouthpiece, and a faster device would not make a maintenance drug suitable for rescue.
  • B. Correct. Ensifentrine is labeled for maintenance treatment of COPD and is not to be used for acute symptoms; sudden breathlessness is treated with a short-acting inhaled bronchodilator.
  • C. Some nebulizer solutions may be mixed for immediate use, which makes this sound plausible, but the ensifentrine label says it should not be physically mixed with other drugs or added to solutions containing them.
  • D. Ensifentrine is used as an add-on for patients still breathless despite long-acting bronchodilators; stopping the long-acting beta agonist and antimuscarinic would remove her foundation therapy.

Ensifentrine is an inhibitor of phosphodiesterase 3 and 4 given as a 3-mg nebulized suspension twice daily, morning and evening, by standard jet nebulizer with a mouthpiece. Its label limits it to maintenance treatment of COPD in adults and states that it should not be used for relief of acute symptoms, which are treated with a short-acting inhaled bronchodilator. The label also says not to mix it with other drugs in the nebulizer, so her albuterol belongs in a separate treatment. Teaching should also cover the label's warnings: stop the drug and seek help for paradoxical bronchospasm, and report new low mood or other psychiatric symptoms, which occurred more often with treatment.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 7 · Clinical Judgment: Pediatric and Neonatal

A 9-year-old with cystic fibrosis has Pseudomonas aeruginosa in a routine throat culture for the first time. She has no new symptoms. What should be recommended?

  1. Dornase alfa in place of airway clearance
  2. Lifelong oral ciprofloxacin starting today
  3. No treatment unless she develops a fever or new cough
  4. Inhaled tobramycin for 28 days to eradicate it

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Dornase is used together with airway clearance and does not treat infection.
  • B. Indefinite oral therapy promotes resistance and is not the eradication regimen.
  • C. Waiting allows the organism to become established.
  • D. Correct. Early treatment of a first Pseudomonas isolate can clear it and delay chronic infection, which accelerates the decline in lung function.

Routine cultures every 3 months in cystic fibrosis detect new organisms early. A first isolate of Pseudomonas is treated with inhaled tobramycin, followed by a repeat culture. Chronic infection is managed with alternating months of inhaled antibiotics.

Sources: Walsh, Neonatal/Pediatric Resp Care, 6th ed.; Rau's Resp Care Pharmacology, 11th ed.

Question 8 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

Aerosolized pentamidine is ordered for a patient with HIV who cannot take oral prophylaxis. Which precautions apply to the treatment?

  1. A negative-pressure room or booth, a filtered nebulizer and a bronchodilator at hand
  2. A standard nebulizer in the patient’s own room with the door closed
  3. An open ward with a standard jet nebulizer and no filter
  4. A heated humidifier in line to increase the particle size

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The drug provokes coughing and bronchospasm and exposes staff to both the aerosol and any tuberculosis the patient may carry. Containment, an expiratory filter and a bronchodilator address each risk.
  • B. A closed door does not contain the aerosol or any tuberculosis the patient coughs out; containment needs negative pressure and an expiratory filter.
  • C. This exposes staff and other patients.
  • D. Larger particles deposit in the upper airway; the target is the alveoli.

Aerosolized antimicrobials (pentamidine, tobramycin, aztreonam, colistin) are given with nebulizers that have one-way valves and expiratory filters. Staff protection includes containment and a respirator when indicated, and patients are screened for tuberculosis before cough-inducing treatments.

Sources: Rau's Resp Care Pharmacology, 11th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 9 · Clinical Judgment: Pediatric and Neonatal

A 3-year-old with asthma is prescribed an inhaled corticosteroid by metered-dose inhaler. Which delivery method should the therapist teach the parents?

  1. A valved holding chamber with a face mask
  2. A holding chamber with a mouthpiece and a 10-second breath hold
  3. The inhaler alone, held in front of the open mouth
  4. A dry powder inhaler with a rapid deep breath

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A young child cannot coordinate actuation or seal on a mouthpiece. A chamber with a well-fitting mask lets the child breathe the dose in over several tidal breaths.
  • B. A 3-year-old cannot reliably seal on a mouthpiece or hold a breath.
  • C. Most of the dose is lost without a chamber.
  • D. Children under about 5 or 6 cannot generate the required flow.

Device selection by age: under about 4 years, a metered-dose inhaler with a valved chamber and mask (5 to 6 tidal breaths per puff); from about 4 to 6 years, a chamber with a mouthpiece; older children can use dry powder or breath-actuated devices. The face is wiped and the mouth rinsed after steroids.

Sources: GINA Asthma Strategy Report (2026); Rau's Resp Care Pharmacology, 11th ed.; Walsh, Neonatal/Pediatric Resp Care, 6th ed.

Question 10 · Devices and Patient Safety

A patient with a right-sided bronchopleural fistula is ventilated in volume control with a heat and moisture exchanger at the Y-piece. Data are: set tidal volume 500 mL, exhaled tidal volume 310 mL, continuous bubbling in the chest drain's air-leak chamber, and secretions that have become thicker over the past 24 hours. Which change in humidification is MOST appropriate?

  1. Replace the exchanger with a heated humidifier
  2. Change the exchanger at 8-hour intervals to restore output
  3. Use an exchanger with a larger internal volume
  4. Add a heated-wire circuit and keep the exchanger at the Y

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Exhaled volume is 62% of the delivered volume, below the 70% threshold for passive humidification, so the exchanger cannot capture enough moisture. A heated humidifier supplies heat and water independently of exhaled gas.
  • B. Frequent changes keep the exchanger clean but do not fix the problem: it can only return the heat and water that reach it in exhaled gas, and more than a third of each breath is escaping elsewhere.
  • C. A larger exchanger adds dead space and resistance, and it still depends on exhaled gas passing back through it. With so much of each breath leaving through the chest tube, a bigger device recovers little more moisture.
  • D. Heated wires keep the gas in the limb warm but add no water vapor of their own, so the exchanger would still be the only source of moisture, and it would still be starved by the leak.

A heat and moisture exchanger is a passive device: it traps heat and water from the patient's exhaled gas and returns them on the next breath. When exhaled volume falls below about 70% of the delivered volume, as with a bronchopleural fistula, a large cuff leak or an uncuffed tube, too little exhaled gas crosses the exchanger, inspired humidity drops and secretions thicken. Other reasons to move to a heated humidifier include thick, copious or bloody secretions, body temperature below 32 °C, minute ventilation above 10 L/min, low-tidal-volume ventilation and noninvasive ventilation. A heated humidifier delivers 33 to 44 mg H₂O/L at 34 to 41 °C at the Y-piece whatever the leak, and the thick secretions here are the expected result of the humidity deficit.

Sources: Al Ashry: Humidification during mechanical ventilation (2014)
