Question 1 · Clinical Judgment: Pediatric and Neonatal

A term infant with a right congenital diaphragmatic hernia is 14 hours old on pressure-control ventilation, and the PIP has been raised from 22 to 28 cm H₂O over 6 hours. Data are: PEEP 4 cm H₂O, rate 60/min, FiO₂ 0.60, preductal SpO₂ 86%, postductal SpO₂ 74%, pH 7.19, PaCO₂ 76 mm Hg. The echocardiogram shows a structurally normal heart. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Raise the PIP to 32 cm H₂O to clear the CO₂
  2. Change to high-frequency oscillatory ventilation
  3. Raise the PEEP to 8 cm H₂O to recruit the lung
  4. Give a neuromuscular blocker to improve compliance

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. More pressure would lower the CO₂ briefly, but a PIP above 28 cm H₂O in a hypoplastic lung invites pneumothorax, and needing it is itself a reason to change strategy.
  • B. Correct. Conventional ventilation has failed: the PaCO₂ is above 70 mm Hg at the pressure ceiling. Oscillation is the recommended rescue mode, and ECMO criteria are not yet met.
  • C. Recruitment helps atelectatic lungs, but these lungs are small rather than collapsed; PEEP is kept at 3 to 5 cm H₂O to avoid overdistension and impaired venous return.
  • D. Paralysis sounds like a way to gain control of ventilation, but in these infants compliance fell after neuromuscular blockade, and the consensus advises avoiding it in initial care.

In congenital diaphragmatic hernia, conventional ventilation is the preferred first mode, with high-frequency oscillation kept as rescue when it fails. Failure means CO₂ and saturation targets cannot be met within a PIP of about 25 to 28 cm H₂O. ECMO is considered when preductal saturation cannot be kept above 85%, pH falls below 7.15 despite optimized ventilation, more than 28 cm H₂O or a mean airway pressure above 17 is needed, or the oxygenation index stays at 40 or more for at least 3 hours.

Sources: Walsh, Neonatal/Pediatric Resp Care, 6th ed.; Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 2 · Clinical Judgment: Pediatric and Neonatal

A 5-week-old born at term is brought to the emergency department after two episodes at home in which she stopped breathing and turned blue, each ending with stimulation. She has had a runny nose for a day, and her brother has a cold. In the department her RR is 44/min, HR 150/min and SpO₂ 96% on room air, with clear breath sounds and no retractions. A nasal swab is positive for RSV. Which plan should the therapist recommend?

  1. Admission with continuous cardiorespiratory and SpO₂ monitoring
  2. Discharge with a home apnea monitor and a clinic visit tomorrow
  3. A caffeine citrate load, then 4 hours of observation
  4. Discharge home, since her lungs are clear and her SpO₂ is normal

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Apnea by history and age under 2 months are both reasons to admit an infant with bronchiolitis, and continuous monitoring is advised when apnea has occurred or the risk is high.
  • B. A home monitor sounds like a safe middle path, but monitors have not been shown to prevent sudden death, and an infant who has already had apnea needs observation in hospital while the illness evolves.
  • C. Caffeine treats apnea of prematurity, which makes it tempting. Its labeled use is short-term treatment in infants born at 28 to under 33 weeks, and it does not replace admission and monitoring for a term infant with viral apnea.
  • D. A reassuring examination makes discharge tempting, but young infants can present with apnea before lower airway signs appear, and the typical illness develops over the next 24 to 48 hours.

Infants under 2 months and those born preterm can present with recurrent apneic spells as the first sign of RSV infection, with tachypnea, retractions, wheeze and crackles following over 24 to 48 hours. Apnea by history, age under 2 months, hypoxemia, poor feeding and chronic heart or lung disease are criteria for hospital care. While she is admitted, continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring and oximetry catch further events early; apnea is uncommon overall in bronchiolitis, so the 2025 Australasian guideline reserves continuous oximetry for infants with apnea or risk factors for it. Recurrent severe apnea is one of the indications for intubation if it continues.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Bronchiolitis (2026); AAP Bronchiolitis Guideline (2014)

Question 3 · Patient Data

Amniotic fluid from a woman in preterm labor at 33 weeks shows a lecithin-to-sphingomyelin ratio of 1.4 with no phosphatidylglycerol. What does this predict for the newborn?

  1. Mature lungs, because any ratio above 1.0 indicates adequate surfactant
  2. A high risk of meconium aspiration at delivery
  3. Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn
  4. Immature lungs with a high risk of neonatal RDS

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Maturity is indicated by a ratio of 2:1 or more.
  • B. Meconium aspiration is a problem of term and post-term infants and is not predicted by this test.
  • C. The ratio measures surfactant maturity, not pulmonary vascular tone.
  • D. Correct. A ratio below 2:1 and absent phosphatidylglycerol indicate too little surfactant and a high risk of respiratory distress syndrome (RDS). Antenatal corticosteroids are given, and the team prepares for CPAP and possible surfactant.

Fetal lung maturity: a lecithin-to-sphingomyelin ratio of 2:1 or more and the presence of phosphatidylglycerol indicate mature surfactant, usually by 35 weeks. Maternal diabetes delays maturation, so phosphatidylglycerol is the more reliable marker in those pregnancies. Antenatal steroids between 24 and 34 weeks reduce respiratory distress syndrome.

Sources: Walsh, Neonatal/Pediatric Resp Care, 6th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 4 · Patient Data

A term newborn on conventional ventilation has a mean airway pressure of 18 cm H₂O, an FiO₂ of 1.0 and a postductal PaO₂ of 45 mm Hg. What is the oxygenation index, and what does it indicate?

  1. 4; normal oxygenation, so the FiO₂ can be weaned
  2. 25; moderate failure that warrants a trial of inhaled nitric oxide
  3. 40; an error, because the index cannot exceed 30
  4. 40; severe failure that meets the usual threshold for ECMO referral

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The FiO₂ is a fraction and the product is multiplied by 100.
  • B. The calculation gives 40.
  • C. The index has no upper limit.
  • D. Correct. OI = mean airway pressure × FiO₂ × 100 ÷ PaO₂ = 18 × 1.0 × 100 ÷ 45 = 40. An index of 40 or more despite maximal therapy is the conventional criterion for ECMO evaluation.

The oxygenation index includes the pressure cost of oxygenation, so it is preferred in neonates and children. In term newborns an index above about 15 to 25 prompts inhaled nitric oxide or high-frequency ventilation, and 40 or more prompts ECMO referral. In pediatric ARDS an index of 16 or more is severe.

Sources: Walsh, Neonatal/Pediatric Resp Care, 6th ed.; ELSO Red Book, 6th ed.

Question 5 · Clinical Judgment: Pediatric and Neonatal

A 4-day-old born at home becomes gray, mottled and tachypneic with weak femoral pulses and a saturation of 80% that does not rise with oxygen. Which diagnosis should the therapist consider, and what should be recommended?

  1. Bronchiolitis; suction, high-flow nasal cannula and observation
  2. Respiratory distress syndrome; surfactant
  3. Pneumonia; antibiotics alone
  4. A ductal-dependent heart defect; prostaglandin E1 and transfer

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Bronchiolitis does not cause absent pulses and refractory cyanosis.
  • B. Surfactant deficiency causes distress from birth, mainly in preterm infants. It does not appear on day 4 with shock and weak femoral pulses.
  • C. Sepsis is treated concurrently, but hypoxemia unresponsive to oxygen with poor pulses in the first week points to a cardiac cause.
  • D. Correct. Cyanosis unresponsive to oxygen (a failed hyperoxia test) and shock in the first days of life indicate a ductal-dependent lesion; prostaglandin is life-saving, and excessive oxygen may promote ductal closure and pulmonary overcirculation.

Congenital heart disease and the respiratory therapist: the hyperoxia test, pre- and postductal saturations, oxygen as a pulmonary vasodilator that can worsen single-ventricle physiology, ventilation strategies that balance pulmonary and systemic flow (mild hypercapnia, lower FiO₂ for lesions with pulmonary overcirculation), and post-surgical care.

Sources: Walsh, Neonatal/Pediatric Resp Care, 6th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 6 · Patient Data

A neonate born at 29 weeks by emergency cesarean for maternal preeclampsia has APGAR scores of 4 and 7, grunting, nasal flaring and retractions at 20 minutes of life. Which factor in the history most raises the risk of respiratory distress syndrome?

  1. Gestational age of 29 weeks
  2. Maternal preeclampsia in this pregnancy
  3. The 1-minute APGAR score of 4
  4. The emergency cesarean delivery

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Prematurity means surfactant deficiency: production is inadequate before about 34 to 36 weeks, and an infant at 29 weeks with grunting and retractions has respiratory distress syndrome until proven otherwise; CPAP and early surfactant are indicated.
  • B. Preeclampsia prompted early delivery but does not itself cause surfactant deficiency.
  • C. The APGAR score reflects the transition, not surfactant status.
  • D. Cesarean delivery increases transient tachypnea, but gestational age is the dominant factor for surfactant deficiency.

Perinatal data: gestational age and weight, maternal history (diabetes increases RDS risk; hypertension and steroids may accelerate maturity), antenatal steroids, lecithin/sphingomyelin ratio above 2 and phosphatidylglycerol presence indicating maturity, meconium, Apgar scores at 1 and 5 minutes (appearance, pulse, grimace, activity, respiration), and Silverman-Andersen scoring of distress.

Sources: Walsh, Neonatal/Pediatric Resp Care, 6th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 7 · Clinical Judgment: Pediatric and Neonatal

A 3.6-kg term infant with meconium aspiration and pulmonary hypertension has received two doses of surfactant and is on high-frequency oscillatory ventilation with inhaled nitric oxide at 20 ppm for 6 hours. The radiograph shows the diaphragms at the ninth posterior rib. Data are: mean airway pressure 20 cm H₂O, FiO₂ 1.0, preductal PaO₂ 42 mm Hg, pH 7.28, mean arterial pressure 48 mm Hg on dopamine. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Raise the mean airway pressure to 26 cm H₂O
  2. Induce alkalosis with bicarbonate to a pH of 7.50
  3. Referral to an ECMO center for evaluation
  4. Increase the nitric oxide to 40 ppm

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. More pressure helps when the lungs are underinflated, but diaphragms at the ninth rib show adequate inflation; more pressure would overdistend the lung, raise pulmonary vascular resistance and impair venous return.
  • B. Alkalosis was once used to dilate the pulmonary vessels, but it is not a current therapy; the aim is to correct acidosis, not to create alkalosis, and it does not treat refractory hypoxemia.
  • C. Correct. The oxygenation index is 20 × 100 ÷ 42, or about 48. An index above 35 to 40 despite maximal support, which here includes surfactant, oscillation and nitric oxide, is the usual threshold for extracorporeal support.
  • D. Tempting because the infant has not responded, but the labeled dose is 20 ppm, doses above 20 ppm are not recommended, and higher doses add methemoglobin and nitrogen dioxide without more benefit.

The oxygenation index (mean airway pressure × FiO₂ × 100 ÷ PaO₂) tracks how much support it takes to oxygenate. Persistent pulmonary hypertension after meconium aspiration is treated with oxygen, lung recruitment, surfactant, oscillation and inhaled nitric oxide, and an index that stays near or above 40 despite all of these is the accepted threshold for extracorporeal support. That is why early referral matters: the infant must reach an ECMO center before shock or intracranial bleeding rules cannulation out.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); ELSO Red Book, 6th ed.

Question 8 · Clinical Judgment: Pediatric and Neonatal

In October, a 14-month-old former 26-week infant with bronchopulmonary dysplasia is seen in pulmonary clinic. She came off home oxygen at 7 months of age and stopped chlorothiazide 4 months ago. She received nirsevimab before her first RSV season. Her parents ask what she needs for the coming season. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Monthly palivizumab injections through the season
  2. Nirsevimab 200 mg before the season begins
  3. No antibody this season, since she is off oxygen and diuretics
  4. Clesrovimab 105 mg as a single injection

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Palivizumab was the monthly product for high-risk children for many years, but CDC reports it is no longer available after December 31, 2025.
  • B. Correct. CDC recommends nirsevimab for children aged 8 to 19 months with chronic lung disease of prematurity who needed medical support (steroids, diuretics or oxygen) at any time in the 6 months before their second season. The label dose is 200 mg as two 100-mg IM injections, regardless of weight.
  • C. Tempting because she is well now, but eligibility looks back 6 months. She was on a diuretic 4 months ago, so she qualifies.
  • D. Clesrovimab is a single 105-mg dose for infants in their first season. CDC does not recommend it after 8 months of age, and it is not approved for a second season.

Prevention is part of respiratory care in BPD because these children have a higher risk of severe RSV lower respiratory tract disease and can decompensate with lung infections. Nirsevimab is a long-acting monoclonal antibody, not a vaccine. Its label covers infants in their first season and children up to 24 months who remain vulnerable through a second season. For the second season it is given shortly before the season starts. Infants older than 6 months with BPD should also receive the influenza vaccine.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 9 · Clinical Judgment: Pediatric and Neonatal

A 3-year-old has had four episodes of wheezing in his life, each starting with a cold, and no symptoms between colds. Each episode was treated with albuterol by spacer, and two also needed oral steroids. His examination today is normal. Which plan should the therapist recommend?

  1. A short ICS course at each cold's onset, with albuterol as needed
  2. Daily low-dose ICS through the year, with albuterol for quick relief
  3. Budesonide-formoterol as needed in place of albuterol
  4. Albuterol as needed alone, because he is symptom-free between colds

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. For children aged 0 to 4 years with recurrent wheezing triggered by respiratory infections and no symptoms between them, NAEPP 2020 conditionally recommends a 7- to 10-day daily ICS course from the first sign of infection, with SABA as needed (high certainty evidence).
  • B. Daily controller therapy is used for persistent asthma, but this child has no symptoms between colds. The panel made no recommendation on daily versus intermittent ICS for this pattern, and the short course limits steroid exposure.
  • C. Single-inhaler ICS-formoterol is recommended for moderate to severe persistent asthma from age 4 years. It is not the regimen for a preschool child with wheezing only during infections.
  • D. This was the comparison in the NAEPP 2020 recommendation. Adding the short ICS course reduced exacerbations needing systemic steroids by about a third, and he has already needed steroids twice.

NAEPP 2020 applies this recommendation to children aged 0 to 4 years who have had three or more wheezing episodes with respiratory infections in their lifetime, or two in the past year, and no symptoms between them. One studied regimen is budesonide inhalation suspension 1 mg twice daily for 7 days. Caregivers can start it at home with clear instructions. Because studies disagree on its effect on growth, the child's length or height should be monitored.

Sources: NAEPP Asthma Focused Updates (2020)

Question 10 · Clinical Judgment: Pediatric and Neonatal

A 39-week infant with a left congenital diaphragmatic hernia is 10 hours old on pressure-control ventilation. Data are: PIP 23 cm H₂O, PEEP 4 cm H₂O, rate 50/min, FiO₂ 0.40, preductal SpO₂ 89%, postductal SpO₂ 76%, right radial pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 64 mm Hg, lactate 1.8 mmol/L. A resident asks for a normal PaCO₂. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Increase the rate to 70/min for a PaCO₂ near 45
  2. Add 4 cm H₂O of PIP to lift the postductal SpO₂
  3. Titrate the FiO₂ to 0.70 for a preductal SpO₂ of 97%
  4. Keep the settings and accept these gases

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Normal CO₂ looks like the right goal, but permissive hypercapnia is deliberate here; the extra ventilation needed to normalize it injures the small, hypoplastic lungs.
  • B. A postductal saturation of 76% already meets the target above 70%; pushing the PIP above 25 cm H₂O risks barotrauma and pneumothorax in the hypoplastic lung.
  • C. More oxygen seems protective against pulmonary hypertension, but the target is 80% to 95% and the FiO₂ is lowered when preductal saturation exceeds 95% to avoid hyperoxia.
  • D. Correct. Every value sits inside the gentle-ventilation targets for a hernia: preductal saturation 80% to 95%, postductal above 70%, PaCO₂ 50 to 70 mm Hg with pH above 7.2, PIP at or below 25 cm H₂O and PEEP 3 to 5.

Congenital diaphragmatic hernia leaves both lungs small and the pulmonary vessels reactive, so survival improves with gentle ventilation and permissive hypercapnia rather than normal gases. The CDH EURO consensus targets a preductal SpO₂ of 80% to 95%, postductal above 70% and PaCO₂ of 50 to 70 mm Hg, using a PIP of 25 cm H₂O or less and PEEP of 3 to 5. Chasing normal values leads to ventilator-induced lung injury, air leak and worse pulmonary hypertension.

Sources: Walsh, Neonatal/Pediatric Resp Care, 6th ed.; Merck Manual, Professional Version
