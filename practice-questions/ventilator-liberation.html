Question 1 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 66-year-old with a tracheostomy has been off the ventilator for 5 days after a long ICU stay for pneumonia. He is alert and coughs effectively. Over the last 24 hours on continuous high-flow oxygen through the tracheostomy he has needed suctioning once in each 8-hour shift. With the cuff deflated he breathes comfortably for 5 minutes with the tube occluded, and a swallow assessment found no severe dysphagia. Which plan has the BEST evidence for shortening his time to decannulation?

  1. Decannulate him now, without first doing a capping trial
  2. Downsizing to a smaller cuffless tube for 48 hours before removal
  3. A fenestrated tube, capped for 72 hours to prove upper airway patency
  4. A 24-hour capping trial, then decannulation if he tolerates it

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. He meets a suctioning-frequency pathway: no more than two suctions per 8 hours over 24 hours, a tolerated 5-minute occlusion test with the cuff down and no severe dysphagia; in a trial this approach halved time to decannulation without more failures than capping.
  • B. Stepwise downsizing feels cautious, but a protocol that left out routine downsizing and capping decannulated patients sooner, and he already meets the removal criteria.
  • C. A fenestrated tube is one way to test the upper airway, but his tolerated occlusion test with the cuff down has already shown that he can breathe around the tube.
  • D. Capping is a common readiness test and is reasonable, but in the randomized comparison it delayed decannulation by about a week compared with deciding on suctioning needs, without lowering the failure rate.

The AARC guideline on adult tracheostomy care found that weaning and decannulation protocols shorten time to decannulation, and that one protocol without capping and routine downsizing worked faster than one with them. In a randomized trial of 330 patients, deciding decannulation by suctioning frequency on continuous high flow, instead of a 24-hour capping trial, cut the time to decannulation from 13 to 6 days without more failures, and the 2026 French guideline adopted the pathway: no more than two suctions per 8 hours over 24 hours, a 5-minute occlusion test with the cuff down and no severe dysphagia. Fewer tube days restore normal cough, speech and swallowing sooner.

Sources: AARC CPG: Adult Tracheostomy (2021)

Question 2 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 46-year-old without heart or lung disease was ventilated for 2 days after a drug overdose. He passes a spontaneous breathing trial with a strong cough and few secretions, and his PaCO₂ is 39 mm Hg. Which post-extubation therapy has the BEST guideline support?

  1. Conventional nasal cannula oxygen at 2 L/min
  2. CPAP at 5 cm H₂O by full face mask until the next morning
  3. Heated high-flow nasal cannula after extubation
  4. Preventive bilevel NIV for the first 24 hours after extubation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Low-flow oxygen is common practice after extubation, but for low-risk nonsurgical patients the guideline suggests high flow over conventional oxygen to reduce reintubation.
  • B. Mask CPAP after extubation is a form of preventive noninvasive support, which the ERS/ATS guideline suggests against in patients who are not at high risk of extubation failure; it also adds mask discomfort.
  • C. Correct. For nonsurgical patients at low risk of extubation failure, the ERS guideline suggests high-flow nasal cannula over conventional oxygen.
  • D. Preventive NIV helps patients at high risk, such as older patients with heart or lung disease or hypercapnia; it is suggested against for those not at high risk.

The 2022 ERS high-flow nasal cannula guideline stratifies post-extubation support by risk. For nonsurgical patients at low risk of extubation failure it suggests high-flow nasal cannula over conventional oxygen. For patients at high risk it suggests NIV over high flow unless NIV is contraindicated. The 2017 ERS/ATS NIV guideline likewise suggests preventive NIV only for high-risk patients, and not for others. High flow delivers heated, humidified gas at flows that meet inspiratory demand, washes CO₂ out of the upper airway dead space, adds a small amount of positive pressure and is well tolerated, which explains its advantage over a low-flow cannula.

Sources: ERS High-Flow Nasal Cannula Guideline (2022); ERS/ATS Noninvasive Ventilation (2017)

Question 3 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 72-year-old with severe COPD who uses home oxygen was intubated 4 days ago for hypercapnic respiratory failure from pneumonia. Today he completes a 30-minute spontaneous breathing trial on pressure support 5 cm H₂O and PEEP 5 cm H₂O: RR 24/min, VT 380 mL, HR 98/min, SpO₂ 92% on FiO₂ 0.35, pH 7.35, PaCO₂ 58 torr (baseline about 55 torr). He is alert, with a strong cough and scant secretions. Which plan should the respiratory therapist recommend?

  1. Extubate to a nasal cannula, with NIV on standby for distress
  2. Keep him intubated and repeat a 2-hour trial tomorrow
  3. Extubate directly to bilevel noninvasive ventilation
  4. Extubate to high-flow nasal cannula at 50 L/min and FiO₂ 0.35

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. This is a wait-and-see plan. Waiting until distress develops gives up the lower reintubation rate that NIV started at extubation offers high-risk patients, and NIV begun for established post-extubation failure has not reduced reintubation and can delay a needed one.
  • B. He has passed the trial and has a good cough. A longer trial does not predict success better than 30 minutes, and another day intubated adds risk of pneumonia, weakness and delirium.
  • C. Correct. Age over 65, COPD and hypercapnia at the end of the trial make him high risk for extubation failure. NIV applied right after extubation lowers the reintubation rate in this group.
  • D. High-flow nasal cannula is a reasonable choice after extubation in lower-risk patients or when NIV cannot be used. For a high-risk, hypercapnic patient, the ERS guideline suggests NIV over high flow.

Patients with chronic hypercapnic lung disease are at high risk of failing extubation. Bilevel NIV started immediately after extubation (preventive NIV) reduces reintubation in these patients, and NIV is preferred over high-flow nasal cannula for high-risk extubations when there is no contraindication. NIV started only after respiratory failure has developed (rescue NIV) does not reduce reintubation and may delay a needed reintubation.

Sources: ERS/ATS Noninvasive Ventilation (2017); ERS High-Flow Nasal Cannula Guideline (2022)

Question 4 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

An ICU is revising its ventilator liberation protocol to follow the 2024 AARC guideline. At present, sedation pauses and spontaneous breathing trials are started whenever staff have time, often late in the afternoon or evening, and patients who pass are often left intubated until the next day's rounds. Which change should the therapist propose?

  1. A standard morning screen, with each trial started before noon
  2. Screening at 0200, with overnight extubation of anyone who passes
  3. Trials after afternoon rounds, once the day's results are back
  4. Screens at 0700 and 1500, with a trial after each one passed

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. AARC 2024 suggests a standardized readiness assessment and, when appropriate, a breathing trial before noon each day, so that a passed trial meets the morning team and leads to a same-day extubation decision.
  • B. Screening earlier sounds faster, but the guideline suggests trials before noon, when the team that decides on extubation and watches the patient afterward is present, not a night-time process.
  • C. Waiting for the day's results keeps the trials late in the day, which is when passed patients were being left intubated overnight; the guideline suggests trials before noon.
  • D. Twice-daily screening is being tested in the FAST trial, but it is not yet recommended, and Esteban 1995 found no faster liberation with several trials a day than with one daily trial.

The 2024 AARC guideline suggests a standardized approach to assessing readiness and, if appropriate, completing a spontaneous breathing trial before noon each day (conditional recommendation, very low certainty). No trial has compared times of day, so the advice follows the trials that built current practice: in Ely 1996, therapists screened patients at 0630 to 0700 and started a trial in those who passed, which shortened ventilation compared with physician-directed weaning. A morning trial lines up with rounds, and the nurses' sedation pause has to be coordinated with it. Esteban 1995 found that once-daily trials liberated patients faster than SIMV or pressure support weaning and as fast as several trials a day.

Sources: AARC CPG: Spontaneous Breathing Trials (2024)

Question 5 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 70-year-old with COPD and ischemic cardiomyopathy (LVEF 30%) has been ventilated for 4 days for an exacerbation with pneumonia and is 6 L net positive. Twenty-five minutes into a T-piece spontaneous breathing trial he becomes diaphoretic. Data are: BP 188/100 mm Hg (132/74 before the trial), HR 122/min, RR 34/min, SpO₂ 88% (96% before), new bibasilar crackles, and hemoglobin 10.9 g/dL (10.1 g/dL before the trial). Which recommendation is MOST appropriate?

  1. Extubate to preventive NIV, given his high risk
  2. Resume full support, diurese, then repeat the trial
  3. Increase sedation, since anxiety is driving his blood pressure
  4. Repeat the trial tomorrow on pressure support instead of a T-piece

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Preventive NIV right after extubation is advised for high-risk patients, which fits his COPD and heart disease, but it is for patients who pass the trial. He failed it, so extubation now invites reintubation.
  • B. Correct. The findings fit weaning-induced pulmonary edema in a fluid-overloaded heart; full support stops it now, and removing the excess preload before the next trial targets the cause.
  • C. Anxiety can raise blood pressure during a trial, but new crackles, desaturation and a rise in hemoglobin of more than 5% point to cardiogenic edema, and deeper sedation would slow weaning further.
  • D. A pressure support trial does load the heart less than a T-piece, which is why it is tempting, but it would hide the problem: the same preload and afterload rise returns when support is removed at extubation, and the fluid overload is left untreated.

Switching from positive pressure to spontaneous breathing makes intrathoracic pressure negative, which raises venous return and LV afterload, while adrenergic tone rises. A weak or stiff left ventricle cannot handle the load, filling pressures rise and the lungs flood. Known or suspected heart disease and COPD are the main risk factors. Clues during a failed trial are a sharp rise in blood pressure, desaturation, new crackles and hemoconcentration (plasma protein or hemoglobin up more than 5%) or a BNP rise of more than 12%. Diuretics are for excess preload, as in this net-positive patient, and nitrates for excess afterload or ischemia. A T-piece is the harder test for the heart, so passing one after treatment is reassuring before extubation.

Sources: Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.; AARC CPG: Spontaneous Breathing Trials (2024)

Question 6 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A therapist is changing the ties on a tracheostomy tube for a patient who coughs forcefully. Which technique prevents accidental decannulation?

  1. The ties are left loose enough to admit a whole hand
  2. A second person holds the tube until the new ties are secured
  3. The old ties are cut away first so that the whole neck can be cleaned
  4. The cuff is deflated so that the tube moves freely

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Ties should admit one finger; looser ties allow the tube to come out.
  • B. Correct. A cough can expel an unsecured tube. One person stabilizes the flange while the other changes the ties.
  • C. This leaves the tube unsecured.
  • D. Cuff deflation is unrelated to tie changes and loosens the tube further.

Tracheostomy care includes cleaning or replacing the inner cannula, cleaning the stoma with sterile saline, inspecting the skin, changing dressings and ties, and checking cuff pressure. A spare tube of the same size and one size smaller, an obturator, suction and a resuscitator stay at the bedside.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 7 · Patient Data

A patient recovering from pneumonia has these spontaneous measurements: maximal inspiratory pressure −14 cm H₂O, vital capacity 7 mL/kg, minute ventilation 14 L/min and a rapid shallow breathing index of 128. What do they indicate?

  1. Inadequate strength and reserve; continue ventilatory support
  2. Readiness to extubate, because the minute ventilation is high
  3. Adequate strength, since the pressure is more negative than −10
  4. Readiness for a 2-hour T-piece trial followed by extubation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Every value misses its threshold: the inspiratory pressure is weaker than −20, the vital capacity is under 10 mL/kg, the resting minute ventilation exceeds 10 L/min and the index is above 105.
  • B. A high resting minute ventilation means a high demand the patient may not sustain.
  • C. The usual threshold is −20 to −30 cm H₂O.
  • D. These values predict failure; the cause of the weakness is addressed first.

Liberation parameters: maximal inspiratory pressure more negative than −20 to −30 cm H₂O, vital capacity above 10 to 15 mL/kg, spontaneous minute ventilation under 10 L/min, respiratory rate under 30 to 35 and a rapid shallow breathing index under 105. No single number decides; a spontaneous breathing trial is the best test once screening criteria are met.

Sources: Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.; AARC CPG: Spontaneous Breathing Trials (2024)

Question 8 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

Which statement describes the effect of therapist-driven ventilator liberation protocols?

  1. They replace the need for daily sedation assessment
  2. They apply only to patients ventilated for less than 24 hours
  3. They shorten ventilation without raising reintubation rates
  4. They prolong ventilation, because physicians are no longer involved in decisions

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Liberation protocols work best when paired with sedation protocols.
  • B. They apply to every ventilated patient who meets the screening criteria.
  • C. Correct. Trials show that daily screening and breathing trials run by protocol reduce ventilator days and intensive care stay compared with physician-directed weaning.
  • D. Protocols shorten ventilation, and physicians remain responsible for the plan.

Evidence-based liberation bundles pair a daily spontaneous awakening trial with a spontaneous breathing trial, and add early mobility and delirium monitoring. Protocols make the right step the default, reduce variation and give the therapist authority to act.

Sources: Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 9 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A patient passes a 30-minute spontaneous breathing trial. He is alert, but his cough is barely audible (peak flow 45 L/min) and he needs suctioning every hour for thick secretions. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. Extubate now, because the breathing trial was passed
  2. Repeat the breathing trial for 2 hours on a T-piece
  3. Delay extubation and work on secretion clearance
  4. Give a sedative so that he tolerates the tube better

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Airway protection is a separate requirement.
  • B. A longer trial does not test cough or secretion clearance.
  • C. Correct. Passing a breathing trial shows that he can breathe without the ventilator. It does not show that he can protect and clear his airway, and a weak cough with heavy secretions predicts reintubation.
  • D. Sedation weakens the cough further.

Extubation readiness has two parts: passing the breathing trial, and being able to protect the airway (adequate mental status, a cough peak flow of roughly 60 L/min or more, and secretions that need suctioning no more often than every 2 hours). Patients at risk of stridor also have a cuff leak test.

Sources: Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 10 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

During a spontaneous breathing trial on pressure support of 5 cm H₂O, a patient’s respiratory rate rises from 18 to 38, the SpO₂ falls to 87%, and he becomes diaphoretic and agitated after 15 minutes. What should be done?

  1. Increase the pressure support to 15 and call it a pass
  2. Continue the trial for the full 30 minutes before deciding
  3. Extubate to high-flow nasal cannula
  4. Stop the trial, rest on full support, find the cause

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. That is not a spontaneous breathing trial.
  • B. Objective failure criteria have been met; continuing causes fatigue and harm.
  • C. A failed trial predicts extubation failure.
  • D. Correct. Failure criteria (rate above 35, SpO₂ below 90%, heart rate or blood pressure changes over 20%, agitation, diaphoresis, paradoxical breathing) end the trial; the patient rests on full support and the cause (fluid, cardiac ischemia, weakness, secretions, sedation) is addressed.

Spontaneous breathing trials are performed daily once readiness criteria are met, using a T-piece, CPAP or low pressure support for 30 to 120 minutes. Success leads to extubation planning; failure leads to rest, reassessment and treatment of causes. Prolonged weaning may use gradual pressure support reduction or tracheostomy for patients requiring weeks of support.

Sources: AARC CPG: Spontaneous Breathing Trials (2024); Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.
