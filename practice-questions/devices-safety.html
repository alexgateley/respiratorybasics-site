Question 1

A respiratory therapist who has grown a full beard for religious reasons is assigned to perform sputum induction on a patient with suspected pulmonary tuberculosis. He passed an N95 fit test 10 months ago, before growing the beard. Which respiratory protection should he use?

  1. His fit-tested N95 after a careful user seal check
  2. A tight-fitting full-facepiece elastomeric respirator
  3. An N95 with a surgical mask worn over it to close gaps
  4. A loose-fitting powered air-purifying respirator with a hood

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A user seal check is required each time a tight-fitting respirator is put on, but it cannot overcome facial hair under the seal; OSHA prohibits tight-fitting facepieces when hair lies between the seal and the face.
  • B. A full facepiece gives a higher protection factor, but it still seals against the face, so the facial-hair prohibition applies to it as much as to an N95.
  • C. A surgical mask over an N95 does not create a seal where the beard breaks it; air still leaks around the respirator's edge, so the protection is unreliable.
  • D. Correct. A loose-fitting hooded PAPR does not depend on a face seal, so it protects a wearer with a beard, and fit testing is not required for it.

Sputum induction in suspected tuberculosis is a high-risk aerosol-generating procedure done in an airborne infection isolation room with an N95 or higher respirator. Under the OSHA respiratory protection standard (29 CFR 1910.134), employers may not allow tight-fitting facepieces on workers with facial hair that comes between the sealing surface and the face, workers must perform a user seal check each time they put one on, and fit testing is done before first use, at least annually and after changes in physical condition that could affect fit. The standard defines a loose-fitting facepiece as one that forms only a partial seal with the face, and its fit-testing requirement covers tight-fitting facepieces, so a loose-fitting hooded powered air-purifying respirator is the option that does not depend on a seal the beard would break.

Sources: CDC Isolation Precautions Guideline (2007); OSHA Respiratory Protection Standard, 29 CFR 1910.134

Question 2

A ventilator is being removed from the room of a patient colonized with Candida auris so that it can return to the equipment pool. The department's routine wipe contains a quaternary ammonium compound as its sole active ingredient. Which reprocessing step is MOST important before the ventilator is used for another patient?

  1. Clean it, then disinfect with a List P product for its contact time
  2. Hold it out of service in the storeroom for several weeks
  3. Run it through an ultraviolet room cycle instead of wiping it
  4. Clean it, then use the routine wipe with twice the stated contact time

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. CDC recommends an EPA-registered hospital-grade disinfectant with a Candida auris claim (List P), used at its stated contact time after cleaning.
  • B. Waiting may seem safer than trusting a wipe, but Candida auris persists on surfaces, and CDC's approach for shared equipment is cleaning and disinfection after each use with an effective product, not storage time.
  • C. No-touch methods such as ultraviolet light or vaporized hydrogen peroxide may supplement cleaning, but CDC does not accept them as a replacement for cleaning and disinfection.
  • D. A longer contact time seems like extra safety, but CDC states that products relying solely on quaternary ammonium compounds are not effective against Candida auris, whatever the wet time.

Candida auris is a multidrug-resistant yeast that persists on surfaces and spreads on shared equipment such as ventilators, glucometers and blood pressure cuffs. CDC recommends Contact Precautions in acute care for the entire duration of every inpatient stay, without routine reassessment for colonization, because patients have stayed colonized for more than 4 years. Reusable equipment is cleaned and disinfected after each use, then labeled and kept apart as clean. The disinfectant must be an EPA-registered hospital-grade product with a Candida auris claim (EPA List P); products that rely solely on quaternary ammonium compounds are not effective, and ultraviolet or hydrogen peroxide vapor systems only supplement manual cleaning.

Sources: CDC: Infection Control Guidance for Candida auris

Question 3

A 70-year-old with a COPD exacerbation is on noninvasive ventilation through a non-vented oronasal mask from an ICU ventilator with a dual-limb circuit, IPAP 16 and EPAP 5 cm H₂O. To limit condensation, a heat and moisture exchanger was placed between the mask and the Y-piece overnight. Data are: leak small, good synchrony on the waveform, tidal volume about 480 mL, RR 26/min with mouth breathing, PaCO₂ 68 mm Hg (62 mm Hg on admission), pH 7.30. Which change is MOST likely to lower his PaCO₂?

  1. Replace the HME with a heated humidifier on the inspiratory limb
  2. Lower the EPAP to 3 cm H₂O so that he can exhale more easily
  3. Change to a total face mask so the seal is spread more evenly
  4. Change to a nasal mask, which holds less dead space than his mask

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. An HME between the mask and the Y-piece adds its volume to the dead space he rebreathes with each breath. Removing it and humidifying with a heated humidifier cuts that dead space, and heated humidification is the method recommended during noninvasive ventilation.
  • B. Exhalation feels easier at a lower pressure, but with a dual-limb circuit exhaled gas leaves through the ventilator's expiratory valve, so EPAP is not what clears CO₂, and in COPD the EPAP helps offset intrinsic PEEP and makes triggering easier.
  • C. A total face mask helps with pressure injury or a leak that will not seal, but his leak is small, and the larger mask does nothing about the HME's dead space.
  • D. A nasal mask does have less internal volume than an oronasal mask in bench testing, which makes this tempting, but he breathes through his mouth, nasal masks promote mouth leak in acute failure, and the HME would still be in place.

A heat and moisture exchanger placed between the mask and the circuit adds its internal volume to the dead space the patient rebreathes with every breath. During noninvasive ventilation, studies have shown higher minute ventilation and work of breathing with an HME than with a heated humidifier, and Jaber and colleagues reported a higher PaCO₂, probably from the greater dead space. The AARC humidification guideline advises against HMEs during noninvasive ventilation, and a heated humidifier is used instead. Interface volume matters too: in vitro, an oronasal mask holds about 205 mL and a nasal mask about 120 mL, but a nasal mask suits a patient who keeps the mouth closed.

Sources: Hess, Respiratory Care, 4th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 4

A patient on pressure support ventilation receives a dose of intravenous opioid. Two minutes later the apnea alarm sounds and the ventilator begins delivering mandatory breaths. What has happened, and what is the appropriate response?

  1. The ventilator has failed; replace it immediately
  2. The circuit has become disconnected; reattach it and silence the alarm
  3. Auto-PEEP has developed; lengthen the expiratory time
  4. Backup ventilation has started; change to a mode with a set rate

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The ventilator is doing what it was designed to do.
  • B. A disconnection triggers low-pressure and low-volume alarms.
  • C. Auto-PEEP causes missed triggers, not an absence of effort after an opioid.
  • D. Correct. Pressure support has no mandatory rate. When no effort is detected for the apnea interval, the ventilator alarms and switches to its backup settings.

Spontaneous modes need a reliable respiratory drive. The apnea interval is usually set at 20 seconds, with backup settings that would ventilate the patient adequately. After a backup event the therapist assesses the cause, such as sedation, a neurologic change or a low PaCO₂, and selects a mode that guarantees a rate.

Sources: Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 5

A patient on a high-flow nasal cannula at 40 L/min and FiO₂ 0.6 complains that the gas feels cold and is coughing. The delivered temperature reads 31 °C. What adjustment is appropriate?

  1. Turn the humidifier off to stop the coughing
  2. Replace the cannula with a non-rebreather mask
  3. Raise the temperature toward 37 °C
  4. Reduce the flow to 10 L/min so the gas warms

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Dry high-flow gas is intolerable and injures the mucosa.
  • B. The problem is the temperature setting, not the device choice.
  • C. Correct. High-flow therapy depends on gas heated to near body temperature and fully saturated with water; a low delivered temperature causes discomfort, cough and secretion drying. The device settings, wire and prongs are checked.
  • D. Reducing flow below the patient’s inspiratory demand loses the benefits of the therapy; the low temperature is corrected at the heater, not by lowering the flow.

High-flow nasal cannula: flows of 20 to 60 L/min (up to 2 L/kg/min in infants) exceed inspiratory demand so the FiO₂ is reliable, wash out nasopharyngeal dead space and provide a small positive pressure. Prongs should occupy no more than half the nares. Indications include hypoxemic failure, post-extubation support and bronchiolitis; failure signs prompt escalation to noninvasive or invasive ventilation.

Sources: Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 6

Before use, the therapist calibrates a galvanic oxygen analyzer. Which procedure is correct?

  1. No calibration is needed for galvanic cells
  2. Two-point calibration at 21% and 100%
  3. A single-point calibration in room air
  4. Calibration with a 50% oxygen mixture only

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. All analyzers require calibration.
  • B. Correct. Two-point calibration verifies the analyzer across its range; a sensor that drifts or cannot reach 100% is depleted and replaced. Moisture and pressure changes also affect readings.
  • C. Single-point calibration does not verify accuracy at the concentrations used.
  • D. Mid-range calibration alone does not check the endpoints.

Oxygen delivery device checks: analyzer calibration, blender alarm test (loss of one gas pressure triggers the alarm), flowmeter function (a back-pressure-compensated Thorpe tube reads accurately with downstream restriction, an uncompensated tube reads lower than actual flow, and a Bourdon gauge reads higher than actual flow when obstructed), regulator and cylinder integrity, and concentration verification at the patient.

Sources: Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 7

A ventilated patient is ordered albuterol by metered-dose inhaler. Which technique gives the best delivery?

  1. Inline adapter between the heat and moisture exchanger and the Y
  2. Chamber spacer in the inspiratory limb, actuated at the start of inspiration
  3. Chamber spacer in the expiratory limb, actuated at any time
  4. Elbow adapter at the tube, actuated during exhalation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The exchanger filters out the drug; it must be removed or bypassed.
  • B. Correct. A chamber lets the plume slow and stay suspended, and firing at the start of the breath carries it to the lungs. Delivery is several times that of an elbow adapter.
  • C. Medication in the expiratory limb is carried away from the patient.
  • D. The plume impacts on the adapter and tube and is then exhaled.

Technique for a metered-dose inhaler in a ventilator circuit: shake the canister, place a chamber spacer in the inspiratory limb about 15 cm from the Y, actuate at the onset of inspiration, wait 15 seconds or more between puffs, and remove the heat and moisture exchanger. Doses are often doubled compared with spontaneous breathing.

Sources: Rau's Resp Care Pharmacology, 11th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Question 8

A ventilated patient’s total rate is 34 with a set rate of 14, yet no chest or abdominal effort precedes the extra breaths. Water is sloshing in the inspiratory limb, and the flow trigger is set at 1 L/min. What should the therapist do?

  1. Change to pressure support so that every breath is patient-cycled
  2. Raise the set rate to 34 to match the patient’s demand
  3. Drain the circuit, check for leaks and make the trigger less sensitive
  4. Increase sedation to suppress the patient’s respiratory drive

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Auto-triggering continues in any mode that is patient-triggered.
  • B. There is no demand; this would lock in the hyperventilation.
  • C. Correct. Breaths with no patient effort are auto-triggered. Moving condensate, leaks and a very sensitive trigger all create flow changes the ventilator reads as effort.
  • D. The patient is not making the efforts, so sedation does not help.

Auto-triggering causes respiratory alkalosis, air trapping and discomfort. Its causes are circuit condensate, leaks around a cuff or through a chest tube, cardiac oscillations and an oversensitive trigger. Correct the cause first, then adjust the sensitivity to the least sensitive setting that the patient can still trigger easily.

Sources: Pilbeam's Mechanical Ventilation, 8th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.

Question 9

A patient is receiving 40% oxygen by air-entrainment mask at 8 L/min. A blanket is covering the entrainment ports. What happens to the delivered gas?

  1. The FiO₂ and the total flow both fall
  2. The FiO₂ rises and the total flow falls
  3. The FiO₂ falls, and the total flow at the mask rises
  4. Nothing changes, because the jet size fixes the FiO₂

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. With less air mixed in, the concentration cannot fall.
  • B. Correct. Blocking the ports reduces the room air drawn in. Less dilution raises the oxygen concentration, and less entrained air lowers the total output.
  • C. That would require more air entrainment, not less.
  • D. The jet fixes the ratio only while the ports are open.

Air-entrainment devices mix source oxygen with room air in a fixed ratio: 24% is 25:1, 28% is 10:1, 35% is 5:1, 40% is 3:1 and 60% is 1:1. Total flow is the oxygen flow multiplied by the sum of the ratio parts, so 40% at 8 L/min gives 32 L/min. Obstruction downstream or at the ports raises the FiO₂ and lowers the flow.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.

Question 10

A ventilated patient has been receiving continuous nebulized epoprostenol for 6 hours. The baseline pressure now sits above the set PEEP, expiratory flow returns to zero slowly, and the expiratory filter looks wet and coated. What should be done?

  1. Remove the expiratory filter for the remainder of the infusion
  2. Increase the set PEEP to match the higher baseline
  3. Replace the expiratory filter and change it on a schedule
  4. Stop the epoprostenol abruptly and observe the patient

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Without a filter the drug fouls the exhalation valve and flow sensor.
  • B. This accepts the obstruction and adds pressure.
  • C. Correct. The drug’s diluent is sticky and loads the expiratory filter, which raises expiratory resistance and traps gas. Filters are changed on a set schedule, every few hours by local protocol, and sooner if the baseline pressure rises.
  • D. Sudden withdrawal can cause rebound pulmonary hypertension.

Inhaled epoprostenol is delivered continuously, usually by a mesh nebulizer fed from a syringe pump, to lower pulmonary vascular resistance and improve matching in ventilated lung. Risks include filter obstruction, hypotension and bleeding from platelet inhibition. It is weaned gradually, with a backup delivery system ready.

Sources: Rau's Resp Care Pharmacology, 11th ed.; Mosby's Respiratory Care Equipment, 12th ed.
