Question 1 · Patient Data

A polysomnography report shows an apnea-hypopnea index of 22 events per hour, nearly all obstructive (effort continues during the events), with oxygen desaturations to 82% and excessive daytime sleepiness. How is this classified and what is the first-line treatment?

  1. Central sleep apnea; oxygen only
  2. Severe obstructive sleep apnea; tracheostomy
  3. Mild obstructive sleep apnea; positional therapy
  4. Moderate obstructive sleep apnea; CPAP

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The report describes obstructive events, and oxygen alone does not treat obstruction.
  • B. Severe is 30 or more, and tracheostomy is a last resort.
  • C. Mild is 5 to 14 events per hour.
  • D. Correct. An index of 15 to 29 is moderate, and CPAP titrated to eliminate events is the first-line treatment, with adherence monitoring and mask fitting by the therapist.

Sleep study data: apnea-hypopnea index (mild 5 to 14, moderate 15 to 29, severe 30 or more), oxygen desaturation index, sleep architecture and arousal index. Central apnea (absent effort) suggests heart failure, opioids or neurologic disease and may need CPAP, bilevel support or adaptive servo-ventilation, but adaptive servo-ventilation is contraindicated in heart failure with an ejection fraction of 45% or less and moderate to severe central apnea because it raised cardiac mortality. Untreated apnea worsens hypertension, arrhythmia and pulmonary hypertension.

Sources: Hess, Respiratory Care, 4th ed.; Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; AASM ASV Update for Central Sleep Apnea (2016)

Question 2 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

During an attended CPAP titration, a 50-year-old with severe obstructive sleep apnea has his obstructive apneas eliminated at 12 cm H₂O. Hypopneas and loud snoring continue as the pressure is raised to 15 cm H₂O in supine REM sleep, and he wakes twice saying he cannot breathe out against the mask. Mask leak is within the expected range. What should the respiratory therapist do?

  1. Keep raising CPAP by 1 cm H₂O toward 20 cm H₂O
  2. End the study and prescribe CPAP at 12 cm H₂O
  3. Add 2 L/min of oxygen and hold CPAP at 15 cm H₂O
  4. Change to bilevel, EPAP 12 and IPAP 16 cm H₂O

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The recommended maximum CPAP for adults is 20 cm H₂O, so room remains, which is why this is tempting. He is already intolerant of 15 cm H₂O, and higher fixed pressure will make exhaling harder.
  • B. That pressure stopped the apneas, but hypopneas and snoring remain. An optimal titration brings the respiratory disturbance index under 5 per hour for at least 15 minutes, including supine REM sleep.
  • C. Oxygen raises the saturation during events but does not keep the airway open, and holding a pressure he cannot tolerate leaves the hypopneas and his discomfort in place.
  • D. Correct. Bilevel pressure is considered when a patient cannot tolerate high CPAP or obstructive events continue at 15 cm H₂O, and both apply. EPAP starts at the pressure that eliminated the apneas, and IPAP sits at least 4 cm H₂O above it.

The AASM titration guideline raises CPAP by at least 1 cm H₂O no more often than every 5 minutes for at least 2 obstructive apneas, 3 hypopneas, 5 respiratory effort-related arousals or 3 minutes of loud snoring, up to 20 cm H₂O in adults. Bilevel pressure is considered if the patient is uncomfortable or intolerant of high CPAP or obstructive events continue at 15 cm H₂O. On switching, EPAP starts at 4 cm H₂O or the CPAP that eliminated the apneas, the IPAP-EPAP difference is 4 to 10 cm H₂O, and IPAP may go to 30 cm H₂O. Titration then continues on both pressures to an RDI under 5.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 3 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 62-year-old with COPD has had two exacerbations requiring steroids in the past year and uses only an as-needed albuterol inhaler. Blood eosinophils are 350 cells/µL. Which maintenance regimen should the therapist recommend?

  1. LAMA plus LABA with an inhaled corticosteroid
  2. An inhaled corticosteroid alone, stepped up to a high dose if needed
  3. Oral prednisone 10 mg daily as long-term maintenance
  4. Continue albuterol alone

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Current COPD guidance for patients with frequent exacerbations recommends dual long-acting bronchodilation, adding an inhaled corticosteroid when eosinophils are 300 or higher, along with rehabilitation, vaccines and smoking cessation.
  • B. Monotherapy with an inhaled steroid is not recommended in COPD.
  • C. Chronic oral steroids cause harm without benefit in stable COPD.
  • D. Frequent exacerbations require maintenance therapy.

Respiratory pharmacology: short-acting beta agonists and antimuscarinics for rescue, long-acting beta agonists and muscarinic antagonists for maintenance, inhaled corticosteroids in asthma and selected COPD, biologics for severe asthma, mucoactive agents (dornase alfa, hypertonic saline, N-acetylcysteine), antimicrobials (inhaled tobramycin, aztreonam, pentamidine), and systemic steroids for exacerbations.

Sources: GOLD COPD Report (2026); Rau's Resp Care Pharmacology, 11th ed.

Question 4 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 58-year-old who takes methadone 60 mg daily for chronic back pain is referred to a sleep clinic. His wife reports loud snoring and pauses in his breathing, and his Epworth Sleepiness Scale score is 15. He has no known heart or lung disease. Which diagnostic study should the respiratory therapist recommend?

  1. A home sleep apnea test with a type 3 device
  2. A STOP-Bang score to confirm the diagnosis
  3. Overnight pulse oximetry at home
  4. Attended polysomnography in a sleep lab

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A home test is acceptable for uncomplicated adults at high risk of moderate to severe OSA, which makes it tempting here. Chronic opioid therapy removes him from that group, since a home device records no sleep stages and cannot reliably separate central from obstructive events.
  • B. Screening questionnaires estimate risk and help decide who needs testing. The AASM recommends against using questionnaires or prediction tools to diagnose OSA in the absence of polysomnography or home sleep apnea testing.
  • C. Oximetry shows desaturation clusters but cannot count apneas and hypopneas, classify them as central or obstructive, or detect hypoventilation directly, so it does not establish the diagnosis or guide the type of PAP therapy.
  • D. Correct. Chronic opioid use is one of the conditions in which the AASM calls for polysomnography rather than a home test, because opioids cause central apneas and sleep-related hypoventilation that need full sleep, airflow and effort channels to score.

The 2017 AASM diagnostic guideline accepts either polysomnography or a technically adequate home sleep apnea test for uncomplicated adults with signs of moderate to severe OSA. It recommends in-laboratory polysomnography instead when the patient has significant cardiorespiratory disease, possible respiratory muscle weakness, awake or suspected sleep-related hypoventilation, chronic opioid use, a history of stroke, or severe insomnia. Opioids depress the respiratory drive, so central apneas and hypoventilation are common; polysomnography measures sleep, airflow, effort and oxygenation, which allows those events to be scored and the right PAP mode chosen.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 5 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 46-year-old office worker with a body mass index of 31 has severe obstructive sleep apnea on a technically adequate home sleep apnea test (AHI 38 events/h, nadir SpO₂ 81%) and an Epworth Sleepiness Scale score of 14. He has no heart, lung or neuromuscular disease, takes no opioids, and his awake room-air SpO₂ is 97%. The sleep laboratory's next titration night is 9 weeks away, and he wants to start treatment sooner. Which recommendation should the respiratory therapist make?

  1. Repeat the diagnosis with a split-night polysomnogram first
  2. Wait for the attended titration night to set a fixed CPAP pressure
  3. Start auto-CPAP at home now, with a download review in 2 weeks
  4. Start bilevel at 10/5 cm H₂O at home and adjust it by download

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A split-night study diagnoses and titrates in one night, but his technically adequate home test has already shown severe OSA, so another diagnostic study adds delay and cost without changing the treatment.
  • B. An in-laboratory titration is an equally accepted way to start PAP, which makes it tempting, but it is not required in an uncomplicated patient and would leave a sleepy patient untreated for 9 more weeks.
  • C. Correct. In an adult with OSA and no significant comorbidities, the AASM recommends starting PAP either with auto-CPAP at home or with an attended titration; home auto-CPAP starts treatment now, and an early download of use, leak and residual AHI confirms that it works.
  • D. Bilevel looks gentler, but the AASM suggests CPAP or auto-CPAP over bilevel for routine OSA; bilevel is kept for patients who need very high pressures or cannot tolerate CPAP despite pressure relief.

The 2019 AASM PAP guideline strongly recommends PAP for OSA with excessive sleepiness and recommends starting it either with auto-CPAP at home or with an in-laboratory titration in adults without significant comorbidities. The studies behind that recommendation excluded heart failure, chronic opioid use, COPD and other significant lung disease, neuromuscular disease, sleep-related oxygen needs, hypoventilation syndromes and central sleep apnea, so those patients are usually titrated in the laboratory. This patient has none of them, so home auto-CPAP is appropriate. Education at the start and early troubleshooting of the download improve adherence.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 6 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 19-year-old with mild asthma uses only albuterol, about three times a week, and has no controller. According to current international asthma guidance, which regimen is preferred?

  1. Albuterol alone, increased to four times daily
  2. Oral prednisone at the start of each episode
  3. As-needed low-dose inhaled corticosteroid with formoterol
  4. A long-acting beta agonist alone at bedtime

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Regular or frequent short-acting beta agonist use is associated with worse outcomes.
  • B. Repeated oral steroid courses carry cumulative harm.
  • C. Correct. Treating with a short-acting beta agonist alone is no longer recommended because it leaves inflammation untreated. A steroid-formoterol reliever lowers the risk of severe exacerbations.
  • D. A long-acting beta agonist is never used without a steroid in asthma.

Current asthma guidance has two tracks. The preferred one uses low-dose inhaled corticosteroid with formoterol as the reliever at every step, with the same inhaler as maintenance from step 3. The alternative uses a short-acting reliever only when an inhaled corticosteroid is also taken.

Sources: GINA Asthma Strategy Report (2026)

Question 7 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 49-year-old truck driver with a body mass index of 36, a neck circumference of 44 cm, hypertension, loud snoring and witnessed apneas completes a home sleep apnea test. The report shows an AHI of 4 events/h over 5.1 hours of recording, with the nasal pressure signal lost for part of the night. He still nods off at red lights. What should the respiratory therapist recommend NEXT?

  1. Repeat the home test with a new nasal sensor
  2. Start auto-titrating CPAP based on his symptoms
  3. Reassure him that sleep apnea has been ruled out
  4. Attended polysomnography in a sleep laboratory

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. This seems efficient because a sensor failed, but the AASM recommends polysomnography, not a second home test, after a negative, inconclusive or technically inadequate study; a repeat is more likely to fail again and patients drop out of the diagnostic process.
  • B. PAP therapy follows a confirmed diagnosis. Symptoms and risk factors raise the pretest probability, but treating without a study leaves the severity unknown and commits him to therapy that may not be needed.
  • C. A negative home test does not exclude OSA in a symptomatic, high-risk patient. Home devices divide events by recording time rather than sleep time and miss hypopneas that end in arousal, so they underestimate the AHI.
  • D. Correct. Polysomnography is the reference test; it measures actual sleep time and scores arousals, and it is the recommended next step whenever a single home test is negative, inconclusive or technically inadequate.

Home sleep apnea testing has no EEG, so its index is calculated over recording time, including time awake, and it cannot score hypopneas that end in an arousal without a desaturation. Both effects lower the reported AHI, which is why a negative home test in a symptomatic patient with a high pretest probability is not reassuring. The AASM recommends in-laboratory polysomnography when a single home test is negative, inconclusive or technically inadequate, rather than repeating the home test. For a commercial driver with sleepiness, an accurate diagnosis also matters for safety and for fitness-to-drive decisions.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 8 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 44-year-old with a body mass index of 27 was diagnosed with moderate obstructive sleep apnea (AHI 19 events/h, not position-dependent) 4 months ago. Despite trials of a nasal mask, nasal pillows and a full-face mask with heated humidification, he removes his CPAP within an hour every night, and he asks for another option. He has healthy teeth and normal jaw movement. What should the respiratory therapist recommend?

  1. A positional device to keep him off his back
  2. Nocturnal oxygen at 2 L/min by nasal cannula
  3. A ready-made mouthguard device from a pharmacy
  4. A custom, titratable oral appliance made by a dentist

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Positional therapy is simple and noninvasive, which makes it tempting, but it helps patients whose apneas occur mainly when they lie supine. His events are not position-dependent, so keeping him off his back would leave most of them untreated.
  • B. Oxygen raises the saturation during events but does not stop the airway from closing, so the apneas, arousals and sleepiness continue. It is not a treatment for OSA on its own.
  • C. Over-the-counter devices are cheaper and quicker, but custom, titratable appliances fitted by a qualified dentist are preferred because they can be advanced until the airway stays open and are tolerated better.
  • D. Correct. For adults with OSA who cannot tolerate CPAP, oral appliance therapy is the recommended alternative, made by a qualified dentist as a custom, titratable device and followed by a sleep test to confirm it works.

CPAP remains first-line therapy for OSA, but a patient who has tried several interfaces with humidification and still cannot keep it on needs an effective alternative rather than repeated mask changes. The AASM and AADSM guideline recommends considering oral appliances for adults with OSA who are intolerant of CPAP or prefer another therapy, suggests a custom, titratable device made by a qualified dentist over non-custom devices, and suggests follow-up sleep testing to confirm efficacy, because symptom relief does not prove the AHI has fallen. Mandibular advancement keeps the tongue and jaw forward during sleep, and it works best in mild to moderate OSA, which fits his AHI and body mass index.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 9 · Initiation and Modification of Interventions

A 26-year-old with asthma on a low-dose inhaled corticosteroid reports daytime symptoms 4 days a week, waking at night twice a week and using albuterol every day. Her inhaler technique and adherence are confirmed to be good. How is her asthma classified, and what follows?

  1. Well controlled; continue the current therapy
  2. Partly controlled; step down after 3 months
  3. Uncontrolled; step up the controller therapy
  4. Intermittent; stop the inhaled corticosteroid

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Well-controlled asthma has none of these markers.
  • B. Three markers is uncontrolled, and stepping down requires good control.
  • C. Correct. She has three of the four markers of poor control: daytime symptoms and reliever use more than twice a week, and night waking. With technique and adherence confirmed, treatment is stepped up.
  • D. Her symptoms are frequent and need more controller therapy.

Asthma control over the past 4 weeks is judged by daytime symptoms more than twice a week, any night waking, reliever use more than twice a week and activity limitation: none is well controlled, one or two is partly controlled, and three or four is uncontrolled. Before stepping up, check technique, adherence, triggers and comorbidities.

Sources: GINA Asthma Strategy Report (2026)

Question 10 · Clinical Judgment: Adult Patients

A 56-year-old man is referred by his primary care physician for fatigue and morning headaches; his body mass index is 45. He has no lung, neuromuscular or chest wall disease and has never been admitted for respiratory failure. Data are: awake room-air ABG pH 7.37, PaCO₂ 53 mm Hg, PaO₂ 64 mm Hg, HCO₃⁻ 30 mEq/L; polysomnogram AHI 12 events/h; SpO₂ below 88% for 46% of sleep time, with long stretches of steady desaturation in REM sleep without scored events. Which therapy should the respiratory therapist recommend?

  1. CPAP titration, as used for most patients with OHS
  2. Bilevel NIV titration with a backup rate during sleep
  3. A weight-loss program first, with a repeat ABG in 6 months
  4. Nocturnal oxygen at 2 L/min to correct the desaturation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Most patients with obesity hypoventilation also have severe OSA, and for them the ATS suggests CPAP first, which makes this tempting; his AHI of 12 is not severe, and CPAP splints the airway without adding ventilation.
  • B. Correct. He has obesity hypoventilation without severe OSA; the ATS panel judged that NIV may benefit this group more than CPAP, and its algorithm sends OHS with no OSA or mild to moderate OSA to NIV titration.
  • C. Sustained weight loss of about 25 to 30% can resolve obesity hypoventilation, but it takes far longer than 6 months for most patients, and he is hypercapnic now and needs nocturnal support in the meantime.
  • D. Oxygen would raise the saturation, but it does not treat the hypoventilation that causes it, and in a hypercapnic patient oxygen without ventilatory support can raise the PaCO₂ further.

Obesity hypoventilation syndrome is awake hypercapnia (PaCO₂ 45 mm Hg or more) in a patient with obesity after other causes are excluded; his PaCO₂ of 53 with a compensating bicarbonate of 30 fits a chronic process. The 2019 ATS guideline suggests CPAP rather than NIV first for stable ambulatory patients with OHS and severe OSA (AHI 30 or more), which is about 70% of them. His AHI is 12, and the long REM desaturations without events show hypoventilation rather than obstruction, which pressure support and a backup rate treat and CPAP does not.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version
