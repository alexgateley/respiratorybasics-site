Try one

Aerosol Drugs and Humidity practice question

A 52-year-old admitted with an asthma exacerbation was comfortable after her last albuterol treatment 4 hours ago, with scattered wheezes and an SpO₂ of 95% on room air. One minute into the next nebulized treatment, using the first vial from a newly opened box, she becomes tight-chested and coughs, her wheezing becomes louder and more widespread, and the SpO₂ falls to 89%. What should the therapist do?

  1. Add a second albuterol vial to the cup to give a larger dose
  2. Stop the albuterol and get an alternative treatment ordered
  3. Finish the treatment, as wheezing often grows louder as airways open
  4. Switch the rest of the albuterol from nebulizer to MDI with spacer

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. More bronchodilator is the reflex response to bronchospasm, but if albuterol itself is provoking the spasm, a larger dose adds to the harm.
  • B. Correct. Bronchospasm that worsens immediately after a dose, especially with the first use of a new vial or canister, is paradoxical bronchospasm; the label says to stop albuterol at once and start alternative therapy, while oxygen is given and the prescriber is called.
  • C. Louder wheezing can mean better air movement in a silent chest, but she had air movement before, and new chest tightness with a fall in SpO₂ within a minute of the dose points to a reaction to the drug.
  • D. A spacer improves delivery and is often a good choice, but the problem here is the drug, not the device; the label carries the same warning for inhaled albuterol in any form.

The albuterol label warns that the drug can produce paradoxical bronchospasm that may be life threatening and that it frequently occurs with the first use of a new canister or vial. When it happens, albuterol is stopped immediately and alternative therapy is started. The AARC aerosol device guide, summarized on the app's aerosol page, likewise lists paradoxical bronchospasm as a reason to stop a treatment. The clues are timing and direction: she was stable, then worsened within a minute of starting the dose. The therapist should stop the treatment, give oxygen to restore her saturation, stay with her and notify the prescriber so that another bronchodilator can be chosen.

Sequence, response and stopping

  • CF order: bronchodilator, hypertonic saline, dornase, airway clearance, then inhaled antibiotic or steroid.
  • Judge response: breath sounds, peak flow or FEV₁ before and after, heart rate, SpO₂, sputum. No indication or no response: recommend stopping.
  • Stop for a sharp heart-rate rise, new arrhythmia, chest pain, paradoxical bronchospasm, desaturation or severe tremor.
  • Inhaled steroid thrush or hoarseness: valved chamber, rinse and spit, antifungal; keep the controller.
  • Teach by return demonstration (teach-back), not 'do you understand?'
Safety Between treatments on one patient, rinse the nebulizer with sterile water and air-dry it; fill only with sterile fluids. Contaminated nebulizers have caused pneumonia outbreaks.

Humidity physics and troubleshooting

Absolute humidity is the water vapor in a volume of gas (mg/L); relative humidity is that content as a percentage of what the gas could hold at its temperature. Warming a saturated gas lowers its relative humidity, and cooling it causes rainout. At 37 °C, saturated gas holds 44 mg/L, so gas delivered at 30 mg/L leaves a humidity deficit of 14 mg/L that the airway must make up. The isothermic saturation boundary normally sits about 5 cm below the carina; dry gas through a tube moves it deeper, drying mucosa, slowing cilia and thickening secretions until they plug the tube.

AARC 2012 calls for humidification in every invasively ventilated patient: 33–44 mg/L at 34–41 °C at the Y-piece from a heated humidifier, or at least 30 mg/L from an HME, which suits short-term use and transport. Heated-wire circuits keep the inspiratory limb warmer than the chamber so less water condenses; if the circuit runs much hotter than the chamber, relative humidity falls and secretions dry. A probe left inside an incubator or under a radiant warmer reads falsely high and the heater turns down.

ProblemLikely causeFix
Water pooling, coughing with breathsRainout in a cool limbDrain away from patient; check heated wire

Low-temperature alarmEmpty chamber, probe off, new circuitRefill, reseat probe, allow warm-up

Bland aerosol (AARC 2003) has three indications: cool aerosol for upper-airway edema (post-extubation, subglottic), humidity for a bypassed upper airway, and hypertonic or hypotonic saline for sputum induction. Its only listed contraindication is bronchoconstriction. Aerosol is a poorer humidifier for a tracheostomy than a heated humidifier and carries infection risk. Cool mist for croup is traditional but has shown no benefit in trials.

Aerosol generators in detail

Particles of 1–5 µm reach the lower airways; larger ones impact in the mouth and throat, more so with fast breathing, and smaller ones are often exhaled. Jet nebulizers shear liquid with gas at 6–8 L/min from a 4–5 mL fill and leave 0.5–1 mL in the cup; tapping the cup and treating until it sputters extracts more. Breath-enhanced and breath-actuated designs waste less during exhalation.

Vibrating mesh nebulizers push liquid through a plate with thousands of laser-drilled holes. They are quiet, portable, leave little residue and add no gas, which makes them the usual choice on ventilators and for continuous inhaled epoprostenol; because they are efficient, some doses may need adjusting. Ultrasonic nebulizers vibrate a piezoelectric crystal at 1–3 MHz, can warm and degrade drugs, and should not be used for suspensions such as budesonide or for proteins such as dornase. Large-volume ultrasonic units are used for sputum induction.

Continuous albuterol for severe asthma (EPR-3) is 10–15 mg/h in adults and 0.5 mg/kg/h in children, through a large-volume or infusion-fed mesh nebulizer, with cardiac monitoring and potassium checks. Ipratropium mist in the eyes can dilate the pupil and precipitate angle-closure glaucoma, so use a mouthpiece or a well-fitted mask.

Mucoactive drugs and the evidence

Mucolytics break mucus bonds: acetylcysteine splits disulfide bonds and dornase alfa cleaves the DNA released by neutrophils, which is why dornase works in purulent CF sputum. Hypertonic saline is a hyperosmolar expectorant that draws water into the airway surface liquid and raises mucociliary clearance. Acetylcysteine smells of sulfur, irritates the airway and can cause bronchospasm at any dose, so the label advises a nebulized bronchodilator and stopping if bronchospasm progresses; a vial once opened is refrigerated and used within 96 hours.

The CF Foundation (2013) recommends dornase and 7% hypertonic saline for patients 6 years and older. In idiopathic bronchiectasis, dornase given twice daily for 24 weeks increased exacerbations and FEV₁ decline (O'Donnell 1998, 349 patients), so it is not used outside CF. AARC 2015 found no high-level evidence for any aerosol to aid secretion clearance in hospitalized patients without CF and recommends against routine dornase, acetylcysteine and bronchodilators for that purpose.

For a first Pseudomonas aeruginosa culture in CF, the CF Foundation (2014) recommends inhaled tobramycin for eradication, typically 300 mg twice daily for 28 days. Chronic infection is treated with cycles of inhaled tobramycin or aztreonam, given after airway clearance. Inhaled antibiotics need an expiratory filter in hospital to protect staff, and tobramycin is never mixed with dornase in the cup.

Inhaled tobramycin doses are taken as close to 12 hours apart as possible and never less than 6 hours apart, each over about 15 minutes with the nebulizer and compressor named in the label, undiluted and unmixed. Aminoglycosides as a class can injure the ear and the kidney. Nephrotoxicity was not seen in the inhaled solution's clinical studies, but tinnitus was: the label calls tinnitus a possible sentinel symptom of ototoxicity whose onset warrants further clinical investigation, so new ringing in the ears is reported to the prescriber promptly; audiometry and a serum tobramycin level may follow, and the drug may have to be stopped. Hearing loss has been reported since marketing, some of it in patients also given systemic aminoglycosides, and the label says to avoid concurrent or sequential use with other ototoxic, nephrotoxic or neurotoxic drugs; kidney function and serum levels are checked in patients with renal dysfunction or on other nephrotoxic drugs.

Protecting patients and staff

  • Pentamidine: 300 mg every 4 weeks by Respirgard II at 5–7 L/min until empty (30–45 minutes). Cough (38%) and bronchospasm (15%) are common, so a bronchodilator is given first if needed. Screen for TB, treat in a negative-pressure booth or room, and use an expiratory filter.
  • Ribavirin: SPAG-2 generator only, 20 mg/mL for 12–18 hours a day for 3–7 days. It is contraindicated in anyone who is or may become pregnant, so pregnant staff stay away, a scavenging tent or exhaust is used, and the drug can precipitate in ventilator valves and tubing.
  • Sputum induction: a booth or negative-pressure room, an N95 respirator for staff when TB is possible, and a bronchodilator on hand because hypertonic saline can cause bronchospasm.
  • Nebulizers are single-patient devices: between treatments clean, rinse with sterile water and air-dry; use sterile fluids and unit-dose vials; at home, clean after every use and disinfect per the manufacturer.
  • Valved holding chambers and spacers: wash weekly in warm water with liquid detergent and let them air-dry.

pMDI and holding chamber care: static, priming, counting doses

New plastic valved holding chambers and spacers can carry an electrostatic charge on their inside walls. Aerosol particles from an HFA pMDI cling to the charged plastic, so less drug reaches the patient (electrostatic drug loss). The AARC aerosol device guide (2023) removes the charge with detergent:

  1. Soak the chamber for 5–10 minutes in 3–4 drops of liquid dish detergent in 2–3 cups of lukewarm water.
  2. Rinse the detergent only from the mouthpiece and the outside of the chamber; leave the inside unrinsed.
  3. Let it air-dry, so the dried detergent coats the inside and forms a barrier to clinging particles. Do not towel-dry: that reduces dose delivery because of static charge.

Firing 10–20 puffs into the chamber before a treatment also reduces electrostatic loss, but it is wasteful and expensive; a chamber manufactured to resist static charge (stated on the device or its literature) is the alternative. Routine cleaning is once a week or more often as needed: take the device apart, soak it in warm water with liquid detergent, shake out the excess water and let the parts air-dry upright overnight.

Priming is releasing one or more sprays into the air when a pMDI is new or has not been used for a while. Drug separates from propellant in the canister and metering valve, and shaking mixes the canister but not the metering chamber, so the first unprimed puff may not deliver an adequate dose. Each label gives the number of sprays and the interval: albuterol HFA inhalers need 3 or 4 sprays when new and after about 2 weeks without use.

The only reliable way to know how many doses remain in a pMDI is to track them, with a dose counter or by hand; most pMDIs now have a counter built in. Without one, read the total number of actuations on the label, log every actuation given, priming sprays as well as treatment puffs, subtract them from the total, divide what is left by the puffs used per day, and mark the run-out date on the canister or a calendar. A 200-actuation inhaler primed with 4 sprays and used at 4 puffs a day lasts (200 − 4) ÷ 4 = 49 days, not 50, and fewer if extra puffs are taken. Once the labeled number has been reached, the inhaler is replaced.

Nebulized COPD maintenance drugs: revefenacin and ensifentrine

DrugClass and doseDeviceLabel warnings
RevefenacinLong-acting muscarinic antagonist; 175 mcg (one 3 mL vial) once dailyStandard jet nebulizer with a mouthpiece, on an air compressorNarrow-angle glaucoma, urinary retention; avoid other anticholinergic drugs
EnsifentrinePDE3 and PDE4 inhibitor; 3 mg (one 2.5 mL ampule, shaken vigorously) twice dailyStandard jet nebulizer with a mouthpiece, on an air compressorPsychiatric adverse reactions, including suicidality

Both drugs are labeled for maintenance treatment of COPD and neither is a rescue drug: they are not used for relief of acute symptoms, and extra doses are not given for that purpose. Acute breathlessness is treated with an inhaled short-acting bronchodilator such as albuterol. Revefenacin is also not started while COPD is acutely deteriorating. Either drug can cause paradoxical bronchospasm, which is treated at once with a short-acting bronchodilator, and the drug is then discontinued. Neither is mixed with another medicine in the nebulizer cup, because compatibility has not been established.

Revefenacin's label says to avoid giving it together with other anticholinergic-containing drugs, because the anticholinergic effects add (worsening narrow-angle glaucoma and urinary retention among them). Ipratropium is a short-acting anticholinergic, so a patient who stays on revefenacin has scheduled ipratropium-albuterol changed to albuterol alone; a second long-acting antimuscarinic such as tiotropium duplicates it in the same way.

Inhaled pulmonary vasodilators: treprostinil and nitric oxide

Inhaled treprostinil solution is a prostacyclin mimetic for pulmonary arterial hypertension (WHO Group 1) and for pulmonary hypertension associated with interstitial lung disease (WHO Group 3). It is given only with its own ultrasonic, pulsed inhalation system, undiluted as supplied and never mixed with other drugs; a single breath delivers about 6 mcg.

  • Schedule: 4 separate sessions a day, about 4 hours apart, during waking hours.
  • Start: 3 breaths (18 mcg) per session. If 3 breaths are not tolerated, reduce to 1 or 2 breaths and then increase to 3 as tolerated.
  • Titrate: add 3 breaths per session at about 1- to 2-week intervals toward a target of 9–12 breaths per session; if adverse effects prevent reaching the target, continue at the highest tolerated dose.
  • Expect cough, headache, throat irritation, nausea, flushing and dizziness. Treprostinil can cause symptomatic hypotension in patients with low arterial pressure and bronchospasm in patients with asthma or COPD.

Inhaled nitric oxide is labeled at 20 ppm for term and near-term neonates with hypoxic respiratory failure and pulmonary hypertension; doses above 20 ppm are not recommended, and the label states that it is not indicated in adults, so adult use is off-label. Methemoglobin is measured within 4–8 hours of starting and periodically after that, and nitrogen dioxide is watched in the breathing circuit. It is weaned in several steps and abrupt discontinuation is avoided: rebound pulmonary hypertension shows as hypoxemia, systemic hypotension, bradycardia and a fall in cardiac output, and the treatment is to restart nitric oxide immediately.

Safety Left ventricular dysfunction: nitric oxide can raise the pulmonary capillary wedge pressure and cause pulmonary edema, worsening LV dysfunction, systemic hypotension, bradycardia and cardiac arrest. Here the label says to discontinue nitric oxide while providing symptomatic care; raising the dose is the wrong response.

Inhaled drugs contraindicated in airway disease: loxapine and insulin

Inhaled loxapine (10 mg from a single-use inhaler, for acute agitation in schizophrenia or bipolar I disorder) carries a boxed warning for bronchospasm that can lead to respiratory distress and respiratory arrest. It is contraindicated with a current diagnosis or a history of asthma, COPD or other lung disease associated with bronchospasm, with acute respiratory signs such as wheezing, with current use of medicines for airway disease, and after bronchospasm with an earlier dose. The history is a contraindication in its own right, so a clear chest on the day does not make the dose acceptable. In the label's asthma trial, respiratory adverse reactions occurred in 54% of patients given loxapine and 12% given placebo.

  • Given only in a healthcare setting certified under its REMS program, with a short-acting bronchodilator (inhaler with spacer, or nebulizer) and staff able to manage acute bronchospasm on site.
  • Before the dose: screen for a history of asthma, COPD or other lung disease and listen to the chest for wheezing.
  • After the dose: monitor for signs and symptoms of bronchospasm for at least 1 hour; sedation from the drug can mask them.
  • Only a single dose within any 24-hour period.

Inhaled insulin powder carries a boxed warning for acute bronchospasm in patients with chronic lung disease and is contraindicated in asthma and COPD. Before the first dose every patient needs a medical history, a physical examination and spirometry (FEV₁) to identify lung disease, so baseline spirometry that shows airflow obstruction (COPD is confirmed by a post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC below 0.7) rules the drug out before it is started. In patients who do take it, spirometry is repeated after the first 6 months and yearly after that, and stopping is considered for a decline in FEV₁ of 20% or more from baseline. It is not recommended for patients who smoke or who stopped smoking less than 6 months ago.

NBRC emphasis and exam pearls

  • Thick secretions and an HME: change to a heated humidifier.
  • Mist disappears on inspiration from a trach collar: flow is too low for demand.
  • Acetylcysteine in an asthmatic: give or pair it with a bronchodilator.
  • Dornase: CF only, its own cup, jet or mesh; never ultrasonic.
  • Pentamidine: negative pressure, filter, TB screen; ribavirin: pregnancy precautions and scavenging.
  • Albuterol with no wheeze and no measurable response: recommend discontinuing it.
  • Heart rate jumps with PVCs during albuterol: stop the treatment, stay, monitor, notify.
  • Inhaled steroid with thrush: spacer, rinse and spit; do not stop the controller.
  • New plastic holding chamber: soak in dilute dish detergent, rinse only the mouthpiece and outside, air-dry.
  • pMDI with no counter: count every actuation, priming sprays included, and mark the run-out date.
  • Revefenacin and ensifentrine: maintenance only, nothing else in the cup; albuterol for acute symptoms.
  • Tinnitus on inhaled tobramycin: report it promptly as possible ototoxicity.
  • Pulmonary edema on nitric oxide with a failing left ventricle: discontinue the nitric oxide.
  • Inhaled loxapine with any history of asthma or COPD, and inhaled insulin with chronic lung disease such as asthma or COPD: contraindicated.
