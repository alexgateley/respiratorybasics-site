Postural drainage and percussion

Place the affected segment above the carina for 3–15 minutes, before meals or an hour after; percuss with a cupped hand and vibrate on exhalation, then cough or suction. In the ICU, every 4–6 hours, with the order reviewed every 48 hours.

  • Any position: ICP over 20; unstabilized head or neck injury and bleeding with instability (absolute); recent spine surgery, active hemoptysis, empyema, bronchopleural fistula, CHF edema, large effusion, PE, rib fractures.
  • Head-down: raised ICP risk (neurosurgery, aneurysm, eye surgery), uncontrolled hypertension, distended abdomen, esophageal surgery, aspiration risk.
  • Head-up (reverse Trendelenburg): hypotension or vasoactive drugs.
  • Percussion: subcutaneous emphysema, burns or grafts, new pacemaker, suspected TB, contusion, bronchospasm, osteoporosis, coagulopathy, chest-wall pain.
Safety Desaturation, rising ICP, hypotension or hemoptysis during treatment: stop, return the patient to the resting position, give oxygen and call the physician.

Try one

Airway Clearance and Suctioning practice question

Chest percussion is ordered for a patient with retained secretions who has osteoporosis, three rib fractures and a platelet count of 28,000/µL. What should the therapist recommend?

  1. PEP therapy with huff coughing in its place
  2. Percussion applied only over the sternum and spine
  3. Mechanical percussion at the highest setting
  4. Gentle percussion over the fracture site through a folded towel

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Percussion risks further fractures and bleeding in this patient. Positive expiratory pressure and directed coughing move secretions without striking the chest.
  • B. Bony prominences are never percussed.
  • C. A device does not remove the contraindication.
  • D. Percussion is never applied over fractures.

Percussion and vibration are avoided over rib fractures, osteoporotic bone, surgical sites, burns and tumors, and in patients with a bleeding tendency, bronchospasm or chest pain. Alternatives include PEP devices, the active cycle of breathing, autogenic drainage and mechanical insufflation-exsufflation.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.

Exam pearls

Exam tip Craniotomy patient: no head-down drainage. Rib fractures, osteoporosis or low platelets: no percussion, use PEP. Weak cough in ALS: insufflation–exsufflation. Desaturation with every disconnect: closed suction. Non-CF bronchiectasis: clearance and hypertonic saline, no dornase.

In depth

The one-page summary above is what the PDF prints. These sections go further.

Why secretions are retained

Clearance depends on cilia, a mucus layer of the right thickness, and a cough with enough flow. Cough needs a deep breath, glottic closure, then expiratory muscles that build pressure and release it as a high-velocity jet. Neuromuscular weakness fails at the inspiratory and expiratory steps, bulbar weakness at glottic closure, and CF, bronchiectasis and smoke injury at the mucus itself. An artificial airway prevents glottic closure, so even strong patients cough poorly through a tube.

AARC 2013 found little high-level evidence for nonpharmacologic airway clearance in hospitalized patients without CF. It recommends cough assistance in neuromuscular disease, especially when peak cough flow is under 270 L/min, early mobility after surgery, and no routine chest physiotherapy for pneumonia, COPD or after surgery. CPT, PEP, IPV and chest wall oscillation could not be recommended in neuromuscular disease for lack of evidence. In CF (Flume 2009) airway clearance is recommended for everyone, no technique beats another, and aerobic exercise is a useful adjunct.

Percussion is rhythmic clapping with cupped hands over the segment being drained, through a thin layer of cloth, avoiding bone prominences, breasts and incisions. Vibration is a fine tremor applied in the direction the chest moves on exhalation. Neither has proven superior to the other or to mechanical devices. Monitor SpO₂, heart rate, breath sounds, sputum volume and color, and ICP where measured.

Positive expiratory pressure and devices

PEP keeps small airways open during exhalation so that air gets behind secretions and collateral channels refill, then huffing moves the mucus up. The patient breathes in a slightly larger than normal breath, holds 2–3 seconds, and exhales actively but not forcefully through the resistor for 10–20 breaths, aiming for 10–20 cm H₂O on a manometer, then huffs; 4–6 cycles take 15–20 minutes. Oscillating devices add flow interruptions: the Flutter's steel ball depends on gravity and works only upright, while lever-type devices (Acapella, Aerobika) work in any position.

Chest wall oscillation vests compress the chest at about 5–20 Hz, usually stepping through several frequencies for 20–30 minutes with pauses to huff; nebulized drugs can be given at the same time. Intrapulmonary percussive ventilation delivers small high-rate bursts with aerosol by mouthpiece or into a ventilator circuit. Insufflation–exsufflation gives a deep positive breath then a rapid shift to negative pressure, often around +40 and −40 cm H₂O for 2–3 s and 1–2 s, in sets of 4–5 with rest, followed by suctioning if needed; higher pressures may be needed through a tracheostomy.

Huffing (forced expiratory technique) is one or two open-glottis exhalations from mid to low lung volume. The active cycle of breathing alternates breathing control, 3–4 deep breaths with a hold and huffs. Autogenic drainage moves secretions by breathing at low, then middle, then high lung volumes. Manually assisted cough, an abdominal thrust timed to the cough, helps spinal cord injury but is avoided after abdominal surgery.

PEP sessions: monitoring, stopping and self-use

During PEP, EPAP or mask CPAP for airway clearance, AARC 1993 has the therapist follow the patient's own report, pulse rate and rhythm, breathing pattern and rate, sputum, mental function, skin color, breath sounds and blood pressure, with SpO₂ when hypoxemia has occurred or is suspected and ICP where it matters. Moderate changes in respiratory rate and pulse rate are expected; the cardiovascular findings in the last two rows end the session.

Before a patient uses PEP unsupervised, the guideline expects five things to be demonstrated: proper technique, proper use of the equipment, appropriate breathing patterns and cough techniques, the ability to modify the technique when an adverse reaction occurs, and the ability to change the length or frequency of sessions with the severity of symptoms. Dizziness is one of the feelings the guideline names as a reason to modify or stop, so a patient who becomes dizzy stops or changes the session and does not push on. In one cystic fibrosis trial, dizziness with an oscillating device improved once the patients were shown again how to use it. Session length and frequency follow the symptoms, not a fixed schedule.

Frequency is set by response: commonly two to four times a day in acute and home care, and from once an hour to once every 6 hours in critical care. The order is re-evaluated at least every 24 hours in critical care and every 72 hours in acute care. If PEP does not increase sputum in a patient who already produces more than 30 mL a day, continuing it may not be indicated.

Nebulizer with PEP, and mask PEP in cystic fibrosis

AARC 1993 lists four indications for PEP: to reduce air trapping in asthma and COPD, to mobilize retained secretions in cystic fibrosis and chronic bronchitis, to prevent or reverse atelectasis, and to optimize bronchodilator delivery in patients receiving bronchial hygiene therapy.

Several PEP and oscillating PEP devices accept a jet nebulizer through a port or a valved T-adapter, so the patient inhales the bronchodilator and does PEP in one session instead of two; the Flutter cannot be used in line with a nebulizer. Where the nebulizer sits matters. In one bench study, aerosol that had to pass through an oscillating device lost about three quarters of the albuterol dose, because the larger droplets deposited inside the device. In a deposition study in healthy volunteers, moving the nebulizer to the patient side of the device improved delivery, and a design that keeps the aerosol path clear of the valve delivered nearly the same fine-particle dose as the nebulizer alone. The combination saves time, but it is not dose-neutral for every device and nebulizer pairing.

In cystic fibrosis no technique has proven better for lung function, but the 2019 Cochrane review of PEP (28 studies, 788 participants) found a lower exacerbation rate when PEP was used with a mask for at least 1 year than with the techniques it was compared with, including a significant reduction against the chest wall oscillation vest. The one mouthpiece PEP study that reported exacerbations found no difference. FEV₁ showed little or no difference over treatment periods up to 3 months and longer studies gave conflicting results; in studies lasting at least a month participants preferred PEP. The developers of PEP recommend the mask because it avoids leaks through the upper airway and mouth. The choice is still individualized to age, preference, symptoms and lung function.

Vest settings and problems

Chest wall oscillation settings are titrated to the patient. Frequencies run from 5 to 20 Hz, most often 8–15 Hz; lower frequencies are described as better for proximal secretions and higher frequencies for distal ones, so a session often varies the frequency. Pressure is usually 5–20 cm H₂O: start low, at about 5–10 cm H₂O, and raise it as tolerated until the chest wall visibly oscillates without pain, hemodynamic compromise or desaturation. Pause every few minutes to huff or cough out what has been loosened.

  • Untitrated settings: chest discomfort, musculoskeletal pain, transient desaturation, dyspnea and fatigue are more likely when high frequencies or pressures are applied without titration.
  • Bronchospasm: oscillation can provoke it in patients with hyperreactive airways, so a short-acting beta-2 agonist is given before the session. If wheezing starts during a session, stop, give or increase oxygen and give the ordered bronchodilator while the physician is contacted (AARC 1991's action for bronchospasm during chest physiotherapy); when therapy resumes, start again at a low pressure and titrate up.
  • Contraindications listed for the vest: an unstable chest wall (fractured ribs, flail chest), recent thoracic or abdominal surgery, untreated pneumothorax, and active or massive hemoptysis; severe osteoporosis, hemodynamic instability and raised ICP are relative contraindications.

Cough assist and breath stacking with bulbar weakness

In ALS with bulbar involvement the larynx can close against mechanical insufflation: the vocal folds adduct and the tongue base moves backward, most of all when a high positive pressure is delivered fast, so the lungs do not fill and the treatment fails. Narrowing of the hypopharynx during exsufflation tends to appear later in the disease. Higher insufflation pressures provoke more closure, not less. The changes that reduce it are a lower insufflation pressure, a lower inspiratory flow, a longer inspiratory time (which makes up the volume lost by lowering the pressure) and insufflations triggered by the patient.

Listening over the trachea or the side of the neck while the device cycles is a simple way to hear whether air is moving in each phase. No airflow during insufflation means closure on insufflation, which is more typical of upper motor neuron bulbar disease, and the insufflation pressure and flow are lowered; no airflow during exsufflation means closure on exsufflation, more typical of lower motor neuron disease, and the exsufflation pressure is lowered. The cough peak flow shown by the device can mislead: the first flow peak of exsufflation is gas drawn from the circuit and upper airway, and it is recorded whether the airway is open or closed. A very low insufflation volume also suggests closure. Adding oscillations to insufflation–exsufflation did not raise assisted cough flow or improve outcomes in ALS.

Breath stacking (lung volume recruitment) builds one deep breath from several squeezes of a resuscitation bag through a mouthpiece or oronasal mask, with no exhalation in between, up to the largest volume the patient tolerates; the patient then coughs, with a manual assist if needed, or exhales passively. Without a valve the vocal cords must close after every squeeze to hold the air, so a patient with weak bulbar muscles loses the volume between squeezes. A one-way valve between the bag and the patient holds each breath in, so stacking no longer depends on glottic control. The volume held by the glottis is the maximum insufflation capacity and the volume held by a valve is the lung insufflation capacity; a stacked volume above the patient's own vital capacity is reached in more than 90% of selected patients, and a cough started from it can have a higher peak cough flow.

Reported side effects of assisted inflation include dizziness, syncope and mild chest discomfort, which usually settle with practice or a change of technique; large blood pressure swings, bradyarrhythmias and pneumothorax are rare but serious. Daily sessions are widely recommended in neuromuscular disease, although prospective trials have not shown that routine use slows the fall in vital capacity.

Suctioning technique and complications

AARC 2022 lists breath sounds, secretions seen in the tube and a sawtooth ventilator waveform as indications in children and adults, and a sudden rise in airway resistance in neonates; suction as needed, not on a schedule. Preoxygenate children and adults, use sterile technique for open suction, keep each pass to 15 seconds or less, and use shallow suctioning (to the tip of the tube) unless it fails. Saline instillation does not thin secretions and can cause desaturation and spread contamination.

Complications include hypoxemia, atelectasis from the vacuum, bradycardia from vagal stimulation, tachyarrhythmias, bronchospasm, mucosal trauma and bleeding, raised ICP, and infection. Closed in-line systems avoid disconnection and derecruitment and protect staff; with high PEEP, a patient who desaturates every time the circuit is opened needs a closed system and, if derecruitment occurs, a recruitment maneuver. A catheter that will not pass through a tracheostomy means the tube is blocked or displaced, an emergency covered on the Ventilator Liberation and Tracheostomy page.

Nasotracheal suctioning (AARC 2004) clears patients without an artificial airway who cannot cough secretions out. A nasopharyngeal airway protects the nose with repeated passes. Contraindications are relative except epiglottitis or croup (absolute): occluded nasal passages, nasal bleeding, acute head, facial or neck injury, coagulopathy, laryngospasm, irritable airway, upper respiratory infection, tracheal or high gastric surgery, myocardial infarction and bronchospasm.

Pain, splinting and lung expansion after surgery and rib fractures

Pain, sedation and anesthesia suppress deep breathing and cough, which is how atelectasis develops after chest and abdominal surgery. Treatment is maximal coughing and deep breathing, an incentive spirometer and early walking. Oversedation and antitussives are avoided, but severe pain also stops deep breathing and may be relieved only by an opioid, given in a dose sufficient to relieve it while the patient is reminded to cough and breathe deeply. In certain postoperative patients epidural analgesia or an intercostal nerve block relieves pain without causing respiratory depression. CPAP may help a patient who is not intubated and has no excess secretions; bronchoscopy is for suspected obstruction by a tumor or foreign body, or when other measures fail.

Splint the incision: a patient with a chest or abdominal incision holds a pillow tightly against it while breathing in, which eases the discomfort of a deep breath. The breath on the incentive spirometer is slow enough to keep the flow indicator in the middle of its chamber, is held for 3–5 seconds, and is repeated 10–15 times every 1–2 hours. A patient who feels dizzy takes the mouthpiece out and breathes normally before continuing. Increased shortness of breath, chest pain, fever or more coughing is reported to the provider.

Rib fractures cause the same problem: pain limits inspiration and cough, and atelectasis and pneumonia follow; in older adults even a minor fall with rib fractures can be fatal. Care aims at lung expansion. Analgesia starts with an NSAID, with acetaminophen added as needed; opioids are used only when nonopioid drugs do not control the pain, titrated to avoid respiratory depression; a patient who still cannot cough or breathe deeply is considered for an epidural or intercostal nerve block. Respiratory care is early mobilization, deep breathing exercises, incentive spirometry, supported coughing and frequent position changes; holding the flat of the hand or a pillow over the painful area eases deep breaths and coughs.

Safety No rib belt, strapping or taping for rib fractures: binding the chest constricts breathing and may predispose to atelectasis and pneumonia. Three or more fractures, or underlying cardiopulmonary disease, mean hospital admission. Fractured ribs are also a contraindication to percussion and to the vest.

When to hold therapy

  • Hemoptysis in CF (Flume 2010): continue airway clearance for scant bleeding (under 5 mL); stop it, and hold hypertonic saline, for massive bleeding (over 240 mL in 24 hours), and consider bronchial artery embolization.
  • Non-massive hemoptysis outside CF: pause vigorous clearance and irritant aerosols while the source is found.
  • Untreated pneumothorax: no PEP, IPPB or insufflation–exsufflation until a drain is in place.
  • Lobar collapse that persists after a day of clearance and suctioning with worsening gas exchange: therapeutic bronchoscopy. Routine bronchoscopy for secretions is not recommended.
  • No response after a fair trial: stop and record why, rather than continuing on a schedule.
