Recognize arrest and start

  1. Scene safe. Tap and shout. No response: shout for help, send someone for the AED and to call the code team or 911.
  2. Check breathing and (professionals) a carotid pulse together, for no more than 10 s.
  3. Not breathing or only gasping, and no pulse you are sure of: start compressions. An uncertain pulse counts as no pulse.
  4. Pulse present but breathing abnormal: rescue breaths (adult 1 every 6 s; child or infant 1 every 2 to 3 s) and recheck the pulse about every 2 min.
  5. Attach the AED the moment it arrives; compressions continue while the pads go on.
Exam tip Alone with a phone: speaker on, start CPR while you call. Alone with a child or infant and no phone: about 2 min of CPR first.

Try one

BLS, High-Quality CPR and Choking practice question

A group home resident collapses at lunch after a coughing fit with a hand at her throat. Staff lower her to the floor, she has no pulse, and the nurse starts compressions. When the nurse looks into the mouth before the first breaths, a chunk of sausage is sitting loose on the tongue. What should the nurse do?

  1. Sweep a finger deep into the throat to check for more food
  2. Turn her on her side and give 5 back blows before more CPR
  3. Leave it and give 2 breaths, since compressions will clear it
  4. Remove the sausage, then give 2 breaths and resume compressions

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Blind sweeps beyond what is visible can push material deeper and injure the pharynx, so they are suggested against.
  • B. Back blows are for a responsive patient; an unresponsive patient receives CPR, which also raises airway pressure.
  • C. Breaths could push the visible food back toward the airway; an object that can be reached is removed first.
  • D. Correct. A loose object that can be seen is taken out before the breaths; CPR then continues, with a look in the mouth each time the airway is opened.

Once a choking patient is unresponsive, CPR replaces back blows and thrusts (Singapore 2026), and each time the airway is opened for breaths the rescuer checks the mouth for the object (MedlinePlus). ILCOR suggests manual removal of items that can be seen and suggests against blind finger sweeps, and MedlinePlus advises removing a loose object but not grasping one lodged in the throat, because that can push it farther in. After removing the sausage, the nurse gives breaths, watches for chest rise and resumes compressions; if breaths still fail, more of the obstruction may lie lower in the airway.

Sources: AHA 2025 Part 7: Adult BLS; Lim et al.: Singapore BCLS and AED Guidelines (2026)

Every compression counts

  • Full recoil: lift your weight off the chest after every push.
  • Pauses under 10 s; compressions at least 60% of the time.
  • Swap compressors every 2 min, or sooner if depth or rate drifts.
  • Restart right after the shock or no-shock message; the AED reanalyzes every 2 min.
  • Hospital bed in CPR mode; do not stop to slide in a board or move to the floor. Lower the bed or use a step stool so your shoulders sit over your hands.
Safety Out of hospital, work where the patient lies. If the space blocks good CPR (wedged in a bathroom), a quick move to open firm floor is worth the seconds (classic EMS teaching).

Team roles

  • Assign roles early: compressor, airway, AED or monitor, recorder, leader.
  • Closed-loop: the leader names the person and gives the order, the receiver reads it back, then says when it is done.
  • EMS: request ALS early and work the arrest on scene; aim for return of circulation before moving the patient.

In depth

The one-page summary above is what the PDF prints. These sections go further.

Why rate, depth, recoil and pauses matter

Compressions produce blood flow only while they continue, and the chest must refill between them. ILCOR 2020 recommends a manual rate of 100 to 120/min (strong recommendation) and a depth of about 5 cm (strong), and suggests avoiding more than 6 cm (weak) and avoiding leaning between compressions (weak). The Korean 2025 guideline adds that depths beyond 6 cm are likely to raise complications and sets a chest compression fraction of 60% or more, kept as high as possible.

  • Hand position: lower half of the sternum in adults (ILCOR 2020, weak).
  • Ratio: 30:2 in adults (ILCOR 2020, weak); EMS may use 30:2 or continuous compressions with positive-pressure breaths until an advanced airway is placed (strong).
  • In hospital, once a tracheal tube or supraglottic airway is in, compressions continue without pausing for breaths (AHA teaching; ILCOR 2020 states this only for EMS systems, and the pediatric review says the same).
  • Rhythm checks every 2 min (ILCOR 2020, weak); Singapore 2026 says to resume compressions immediately after the shock.
  • Breaths: about 1 s each, just to visible chest rise; Korea gives 500 to 600 mL (6 to 7 mL/kg) as the adult target. Forceful, fast breaths push air into the stomach (classic teaching).
  • Feedback devices are suggested as part of a quality program and suggested against when used alone (ILCOR 2020, weak).
  • Precordial thump: recommended against (ILCOR 2020, strong).
Exam tip Depth drifts as rescuers tire, often without their noticing. A falling depth on the feedback screen is the cue to swap, even before the 2-minute check.

Surface and rescuer position

ILCOR 2020 suggests compressions on a firm surface when possible, turning on the bed's CPR (mattress-stiffening) mode during in-hospital arrest, and not moving the patient to the floor to gain depth; it made no recommendation on backboards. The Korean 2025 guideline, built on the 2025 ILCOR review, says an existing backboard may stay but placing a new one is not recommended (weak, very low certainty), and that moving to the floor delays CPR.

Rescuer position changes depth. In a simulation trial (Mayrand 2015, 55 rescuers analyzed), compressing with the arms at 90 degrees to the chest gave a mean depth of 41.4 mm against 32.2 mm at a lesser angle; a lowered bed (64 cm against 74 cm) trended deeper, and using a step stool was linked to a correct shoulder position. Even the better group averaged under the 50 mm target, which is why a feedback device and a low bed or stool matter for shorter rescuers.

Obesity: the opened sources give no different depth or rate for patients with obesity. A thick chest wall and a soft mattress mean more force is needed to reach the same depth, and compressors tire faster, so rotate early and do not interrupt compressions for a board.

Recognition, pulse checks and starting early

Unresponsiveness with absent or abnormal (agonal) breathing is treated as cardiac arrest. Professionals check breathing and pulse together for no more than 10 s; Singapore 2026 and the 2025 pediatric guidance both say to start CPR if the pulse is absent or uncertain. ILCOR 2020 strongly recommends that lay people start CPR for presumed arrest without worrying about harming someone who is not in arrest.

  • Telecommunicators should coach callers into CPR (AHA 2025 Part 4, class 1); compression-only instructions for adults (class 1), conventional CPR with breaths for children and infants (class 1).
  • A two-question approach (unresponsive? breathing normally?) identified 92% of out-of-hospital arrests in Part 4's evidence.
  • Telecommunicator CPR was linked to more than 5 times the likelihood of bystander CPR, started 7 min sooner.
  • Rescue breathing with a pulse: Korea 2025 states 1 breath every 6 s for adults (expert consensus; Singapore 2026 says every 5 s). For children and infants the 2025 pediatric review gives 1 breath every 2 to 3 s (20 to 30/min) when an advanced airway is in place or the pulse is present with inadequate breathing.
  • Heart-rate rule in children: Singapore 2026 starts CPR in an unresponsive child or infant with a pulse of 60/min or less. The 2025 pediatric review says to start CPR when pulse or breathing is absent, uncertain or hard to judge and gives no rate. AHA PALS teaching (not opened here) is a heart rate under 60/min with poor perfusion.
  • For an unmonitored arrest, ILCOR 2020 suggests a short period of CPR until the defibrillator is ready, then analysis without further delay.

Defibrillation details

Anterolateral placement is reasonable and anteroposterior is an acceptable alternative (ILCOR 2020), which helps when a dressing, chest tube, device or breast tissue is in the way. Pads larger than 8 cm are reasonable. Korea 2025 says the pads go on the bare chest, and in women without loosening or removing the bra and avoiding breast tissue (expert consensus). Singapore 2026 lists the preparation steps: not on a metal or wet surface, chest wiped dry, hair shaved, jewelry removed, medication patches removed and wiped.

Implanted pacemakers and ICDs do not prevent defibrillation; keep the pad off the device, at least 2.5 cm (1 in) from its edge (Febrasgo 2026), or use front-and-back placement. Singapore 2026 gives a wider margin of about four finger breadths from a pacemaker, which is more than 2.5 cm; the two figures are not the same distance, and the page uses the 2.5 cm minimum that the bank also keys. Pediatric pads: Singapore uses energy-attenuated pads for children up to 8 years or under 25 kg and adult pads if none are available; the 2025 AHA/AAP guidance likewise prefers an attenuated AED when available.

Public access defibrillation is a class 1 recommendation in AHA 2025 Part 4. In the PAD trial, a structured lay responder program with CPR and AED training produced 30 survivors among 107 arrests against 15 among 128 with CPR training alone (relative risk 2.0).

Persisting VF, P-wave asystole and filtered rhythms

VF that is still on the monitor at the next rhythm check has two possible histories. In true refractory VF the shock never terminated the rhythm. In recurrent VF the shock worked and VF returned during the 2 minutes of CPR that followed, unseen because the rhythm is not analyzed during compressions. Recurrence is far more common: in a review of defibrillator recordings from Victoria, Australia (Alhenaki 2026), 807 of 900 patients who had received three consecutive shocks (89.7%) had recurrent VF or pulseless VT and 93 (10.3%) had true refractory VF, and survival to discharge was higher with recurrence (32.2% against 14.0%).

So VF at a later check does not show that the pads or the energy failed. Keep shocking and compressing, give the antiarrhythmic on schedule (RCUK 2025 gives amiodarone after three shocks and again after five whether the VF is continuous or intermittent) and look for a reversible cause. A pad vector change or double sequential defibrillation may be considered for VF that persists after three or more shocks, but neither is routine (Korea 2025; RCUK 2025 does not recommend routine double sequential shocks).

  • P-wave asystole (ventricular standstill): P waves continue with no QRS complexes and no pulse. Asystole is the absence of ventricular contraction whether or not atrial activity is present (Korea 2025), so this is a nonshockable arrest: CPR, epinephrine 1 mg as early as possible and a search for reversible causes.
  • Check every apparent asystole for P waves: unlike true asystole, P-wave asystole is more likely to respond to pacing (RCUK 2025). Pacing is added to CPR and epinephrine, not used in place of them, and it is not routine in established arrest (see ACLS Algorithms in Brief).
  • Artifact-filtered ECG: some defibrillators display the rhythm with compression artifact removed. When ILCOR reviewed these algorithms in 2020, no study had tested them during real resuscitation for patient outcomes, and it suggests against their routine use. RCUK 2025 lets the filtered display guide whether the 2-minute rhythm check needs a pause (none if it shows asystole). Neither source moves the check earlier on a filtered trace: compressions continue, and the rhythm that decides a shock is the one read at the scheduled 2-minute check.
  • VF waveform analysis, such as amplitude, has no current role in choosing the moment to shock (RCUK 2025).
  • What justifies an unscheduled pause is a combination of signs of ROSC: return of consciousness or purposeful movement, a pulsatile arterial waveform, a sharp rise in ETCO₂. A rise in ETCO₂ alone is not enough (RCUK 2025).

CPR-induced consciousness

Compressions can perfuse the brain well enough for a patient without ROSC to open the eyes, speak or move. It is uncommon but increasingly reported: 112 of 16,558 out-of-hospital arrests in Victoria, Australia (0.7%), most often as body movement (Korea 2025 evidence review).

  • Awareness during compressions is not ROSC. The rhythm check and pulse decide the next step: a patient who is awake but in pulseless VF is still in cardiac arrest and gets the shock, continued CPR and drugs on schedule.
  • Small doses of a sedative, an analgesic or both may be considered to prevent pain and distress (RCUK 2025). Korea 2025 words it as the lowest effective dose when the awareness interferes with CPR or puts the patient or rescuers at risk.
  • Drug choice follows local protocol and critical care practice; RCUK 2025 gives small doses of fentanyl, ketamine or midazolam as examples.
  • Never give a neuromuscular blocking drug alone to a conscious patient (RCUK 2025; Korea 2025).

Termination-of-resuscitation rules and their limits

Termination-of-resuscitation (TOR) rules help EMS crews recognize out-of-hospital arrests in which transport is futile. The BLS rule needs all three: arrest not witnessed by EMS, no shock delivered and no ROSC in the field. The ALS rule needs all four: arrest not witnessed, no bystander CPR, no shock delivered and no ROSC after ALS care in the field (Kashiura 2016).

  • A rule applies only to the kind of patient it was validated in. The main validation studies enrolled adults with out-of-hospital arrest of presumed cardiac cause and left out non-cardiac causes. In a Japanese cohort of 11,505 arrests the ALS rule's specificity for a poor outcome was 0.963 in cardiac arrests and 0.833 in non-cardiac arrests, a group that included asphyxia and drug overdose. The authors advise caution there and say the rules should not be applied when a reversible cause is presumed; their study left out traumatic arrest (Kashiura 2016).
  • So an overdose arrest, a traumatic arrest or an in-hospital arrest is not ended on a TOR rule alone. RCUK 2025: TOR rules should not be the sole strategy for stopping an in-hospital attempt.
  • ETCO₂ of 10 mm Hg or less after 20 minutes of advanced life support carries a very poor prognosis in intubated patients. It is not applied to a patient without a tracheal tube and is never the only reason to stop (Korea 2025; RCUK 2025).

Anaphylactic arrest and epinephrine concentrations

Anaphylaxis collapses the circulation through vasodilation and capillary leak; up to one third of the circulating volume can be lost (RCUK 2021). While there is a pulse, the first drug is IM epinephrine: 500 mcg of the 1 mg/mL solution for an adult, repeated after 5 minutes if there is no improvement (RCUK 2021 and 2025).

  • Once the patient is in cardiac arrest, IM epinephrine is not absorbed reliably and giving it distracts from CPR. Follow standard advanced life support and give epinephrine IV or IO at arrest doses (RCUK 2021).
  • Fluid treats the cause: a rapid crystalloid bolus of 500 to 1,000 mL in an adult, IV or IO, then more as needed; severe anaphylactic shock can need 3 to 5 L (RCUK 2021). Korea 2025 gives at least 500 mL within 5 to 10 minutes.
  • Antihistamines have no role in treating the respiratory or cardiovascular features, and routine corticosteroids are not recommended; neither is given ahead of epinephrine and fluid (RCUK 2021).
  • Glucagon is considered for a patient on a beta-blocker whose anaphylaxis stays refractory to epinephrine and adequate fluid (RCUK 2021).
  • Consider prolonged CPR, including extracorporeal CPR: the cause is sudden and potentially reversible in a previously well patient (RCUK 2021; Korea 2025).
Adult arrest dose: 1 mg IV or IO every 3 to 5 min from either product. After a peripheral IV dose, give a 20 mL fluid bolus to carry the drug to the central circulation (Korea 2025).

Choking: the evidence behind the sequence

There are no randomized trials comparing choking interventions. The 2025 AHA adult change rests on a cohort of 709 obstruction cases in which back blows were associated with better outcomes than abdominal thrusts or chest compressions, together with case reports of fatal injuries from abdominal thrusts such as aortic dissection and gastric rupture (Tian 2026). The sequence also now matches the pediatric one, which makes it easier to teach. ERC 2025 uses the same 5 and 5 cycles.

The MOCHI registry (Igarashi 2025, 407 Japanese adults) compared each technique with no bystander intervention: abdominal thrusts relieved the obstruction in 30% and back blows in 27%; both were linked to better neurologic outcome, and back blows also to higher 30-day survival (adjusted hazard ratio 0.52). One of 24 patients given abdominal thrusts had a pneumothorax. It did not compare the two techniques head to head.

  • Unresponsive: start CPR. Compressions raise airway pressure and can move the object. Look in the mouth each time the airway is opened and remove only what you can see; blind finger sweeps are suggested against (ILCOR 2020).
  • Magill forceps under direct view are an option for skilled providers in an obstruction arrest (ILCOR 2020).
  • Suction anti-choking devices: ILCOR 2020 suggested against routine use; the 2025 ILCOR list dropped that line (Tian 2026), Korea 2025 still does not recommend routine use, and no source shows them better than back blows and thrusts.
  • After successful thrusts or compressions, ERC and ILCOR 2025 call for evaluation by a clinician because injuries can be hidden (Tian 2026). MedlinePlus adds: seek care for a cough, fever, trouble swallowing or wheeze in the following days.
  • Infants: abdominal thrusts are avoided because of the risk of liver injury (Tian 2026). Back blows alternate with 5 chest thrusts (Lee 2026, Tian 2026). AHA 2025 uses the heel of one hand on the center of the breastbone, just below the nipple line, for infant chest thrusts and dropped the two-finger technique (AHA newsroom; AAP HealthyChildren); some older patient pages still show two fingers.
  • Children over 1 year: 5 back blows alternate with 5 abdominal thrusts (Lee 2026, Part 6 abstract). Adult and child chest thrusts replace abdominal thrusts in pregnancy or obesity (MedlinePlus; Korea 2025).

Children and infants

Infants are under 1 year, children run from 1 year to puberty, and adolescents follow adult BLS (Lee 2026). A single rescuer uses 30:2 at every age; two rescuers use 15:2 before puberty. Compressions are at least one-third of the chest depth. The 2025 AHA/AAP guidance dropped the two-finger infant technique because it does not reliably reach depth; the heel of one hand or two thumbs with the hands encircling the chest are both acceptable, and the heel of one hand is used if you cannot encircle the chest. Depth in centimeters: MedlinePlus (2026) gives about 5 cm for a child and about 4 cm for an infant; the 2025 pediatric review gives only one-third of the chest, and Singapore 2026 uses 4 to 5 cm (child) and 3 to 4 cm (infant).

  • With an advanced airway, or a pulse with inadequate breathing: 1 breath every 2 to 3 s (20 to 30/min).
  • Rescue breaths with compressions improve survival in pediatric out-of-hospital arrest; lay rescuers should give them if willing and able.
  • Lone rescuer without a phone: about 2 min (5 cycles) of CPR before leaving to call (MedlinePlus).
  • Infant breaths: cover the mouth and nose with your mouth (classic teaching).
  • Newborns at birth follow neonatal resuscitation, and Singapore does not use an AED under 1 month.

Pregnancy, opioids and drowning

Pregnancy: once the fundus reaches the umbilicus, at about 20 weeks, the uterus compresses the vena cava when the patient is supine. A rescuer moves the uterus by hand to the left throughout CPR; Febrasgo says lateral tilting is not recommended because it lowers compression effectiveness. Hands stay on the lower sternum at normal depth and rate, and defibrillation is the same as for any adult with fetal monitors removed first (Febrasgo 2026). The hospital team prepares for emergency delivery at the site of arrest; that decision belongs to advanced providers (see OB Emergencies).

Emptying the uterus does not have to mean a cesarean. When the arrest happens in the second stage of labor with the fetus engaged, an operative vaginal delivery with forceps or vacuum is appropriate if it can be completed within the same time limit; otherwise the team performs a resuscitative hysterotomy where the patient lies, not in the operating room (Febrasgo 2026). AHA 2025 uses the term resuscitative delivery (see OB Emergencies). Fetal monitoring is not used during CPR; once circulation and stability return, the fetus is assessed again (Febrasgo 2026), and temperature control follows the usual adult criteria with fetal monitoring attached (see Post-Cardiac Arrest Care).

Opioids: an estimated 1 in 6 to 1 in 3 out-of-hospital arrests are overdose-related (AHA 2025 Part 4). Without a pulse, CPR and the AED come first; naloxone is given if available without stopping compressions (MedlinePlus), and the 2025 adult BLS algorithms now include it (Part 7 abstract). Part 4 supports lay possession and use of naloxone with legal protection (class 1). With a pulse and poor breathing, ventilate with a bag-mask and give naloxone.

Drowning: ventilation matters. Korea 2025 has trained responders start with breaths and recommends standard CPR with ventilation, with compression-only CPR for untrained lay rescuers. Dry the chest before the AED pads go on.

Teams, transport and debriefing

  • Out of hospital, prioritizing on-scene resuscitation toward sustained return of circulation before transport can be beneficial (Part 4, class 2a); survival to discharge was about twice as high without intra-arrest transport in adults.
  • Termination of resuscitation on scene is an ALS and medical-direction decision, not a BLS one.
  • An ALS-level clinician at the arrest (2a) and a team large enough for separate roles (2a) are beneficial; pit-crew style role assignment is discussed in the same evidence.
  • In hospital, code teams should include members with ALS training (class 1); dedicated teams with defined roles can help (2a).
  • Performance-focused debriefing (2a), using the device data (2a), immediate and delayed (2a).
  • Closed-loop communication: the sender gives the message and the receiver repeats it back (Bhangu 2022, Arnold 2025); announcing completion is common team practice.
Safety Moving CPR is poor CPR: compressions during carrying and in a moving ambulance are shallower and interrupted, and unrestrained crews are at risk. Bring advanced care to the patient instead.

Exam pearls

  • Gasping plus an uncertain pulse is cardiac arrest: compress, do not recheck.
  • Choking, still coughing forcefully: watch and encourage, do not hit or thrust.
  • Choking, cannot speak or cough: 5 back blows then 5 abdominal thrusts, repeated; chest thrusts in late pregnancy or a large abdomen; infants chest thrusts only.
  • Choking patient goes limp: floor, compressions, look before breaths, no blind sweep.
  • Medication patch under a pad: remove and wipe. Pacemaker under the pad site: move the pad away or go front-and-back.
  • AED says shock delivered or no shock: compressions now, not a pulse check.
  • High bed and a short rescuer: lower the bed or use a stool. Patient with obesity: same depth, no pause for a board.
  • Witnessed shockable arrest with an AED working: stay and work on scene; let ALS come to you.
  • Leader orders a drug: repeat it back, then report it given.
  • Pads on the wrong sides with a biphasic defibrillator: shock, do not swap them.
  • Mechanical CPR device running: shock through it. Manual compressions: hands off first, gloves or not.
  • Tube or supraglottic airway in place: the circuit stays on for the shock. Mask or cannula: off and 1 m away.
  • Awake during compressions with pulseless VF at the check: still an arrest. Shock, resume CPR, consider small-dose sedation.
  • Anaphylactic arrest: IV or IO epinephrine and rapid fluid boluses, not IM epinephrine or an antihistamine.
