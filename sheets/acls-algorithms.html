Try one

ACLS Algorithms in Brief practice question

After 30 minutes of resuscitation for an unwitnessed in-hospital arrest with an initial rhythm of asystole, there has been no ROSC. Reversible causes have been addressed, and ETCO₂ has remained below 10 despite good CPR. Which statement BEST guides the decision about terminating resuscitation?

  1. Guidelines mandate stopping at exactly 20 minutes
  2. Weigh all factors; no single rule decides
  3. Continue indefinitely until ROSC is achieved
  4. Only the attending, not the team, may consider it

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. There is no universal fixed time limit.
  • B. Correct. The decision considers witnessed status, rhythm, duration, ETCO₂, reversible causes, and patient goals.
  • C. Indefinite resuscitation without a reasonable chance of benefit is not recommended.
  • D. The team leader integrates team input with clinical factors.

No single factor or time limit determines when to stop resuscitation. Considerations include whether the arrest was witnessed, bystander CPR, the initial rhythm, time to CPR and defibrillation, total duration, response to interventions, ETCO₂ (persistently below 10 after 20 minutes of good CPR predicts poor outcome but is not used alone), reversible causes, and the patient’s goals of care. The team is included, and debriefing and family support follow.

Sources: AHA 2025 Part 9: Adult ALS; AHA ACLS Provider Manual (2025)

Bradycardia with a pulse

Usually under 50/min. Treat if it causes hypotension, acute confusion, shock, ischemic chest pain or acute heart failure; look for ischemia, drugs, hypoxia, hyperkalemia.

  1. Airway, oxygen if needed, monitor, IV, 12-lead ECG
  2. Atropine 1 mg IV, repeated every 3–5 min to a 3 mg total
  3. If atropine fails: transcutaneous pacing and/or dopamine 5–20 mcg/kg/min or epinephrine 2–10 mcg/min
  4. Expert consult and transvenous pacing
Safety Shock and no IV or IO yet: pace while getting access. Heart transplant: no atropine (denervated heart; paradoxical block reported); go to pacing or an infusion.

Tachycardia with a pulse

Usually 150/min or more. Unstable because of the rhythm (not, say, sepsis)? Synchronized cardioversion, sedated when feasible; if regular and narrow, adenosine may be tried while preparing.

After ROSC

  • FiO₂ 100% until SpO₂ is reliable, then 90–98% (PaO₂ 60–105 mm Hg)
  • PaCO₂ 35–45 mm Hg (ETCO₂ may read falsely low)
  • MAP 65 mm Hg or more
  • Early 12-lead; emergent cath for STEMI, cardiogenic shock, recurrent VT/VF or severe ischemia
  • Not following commands: hold 32–37.5 °C for at least 36 h; rewarm no faster than 0.5 °C/h
  • Glucose 70–180 mg/dL; EEG for seizures
  • Prognosis: multimodal, at least 72 h after ROSC or normothermia

High-quality CPR: perfusion physiology and feedback

Compressions move blood two ways: they squeeze the heart between sternum and spine (the cardiac pump) and raise intrathoracic pressure so blood leaves the chest (the thoracic pump). Done well, they deliver roughly a quarter to a third of normal cardiac output. The myocardium is perfused almost entirely during the release phase, driven by coronary perfusion pressure (CPP): aortic diastolic pressure minus right atrial diastolic pressure.

Paradis 1990 (JAMA) measured CPP in 100 arrests: ROSC occurred only when the maximal CPP reached 15 mm Hg or more, and those with ROSC reached a mean maximum of 25.6 mm Hg against 8.4. CPP builds over uninterrupted compressions and falls as soon as they stop, so every pause costs more than its own length. Epinephrine raises CPP; leaning, slow rates and overventilation lower it.

AHA targets, observational evidence. Idris 2015 (10,371 OHCA) found the best survival at 100–119/min.

Rescuers misjudge fatigue: on manikins, correct compressions fell from 93% in minute 1 to 39% by minute 3, yet rescuers felt tired only at about 4 minutes (Hightower 1995), hence 2-minute swaps and real-time feedback. EtCO₂ below 10 mm Hg means compressions need fixing and 20 or more is the goal (AHA 2025 supportive text), provided ventilation is steady. With an arterial line, diastolic (relaxation) pressure is the closest bedside proxy for CPP: the AHA 2010 algorithm said to improve CPR below 20 mm Hg, pediatric data link 25–30 mm Hg or more with survival (Berg 2018), and AHA 2025 sets no adult number.

Exam tip A sudden EtCO₂ rise of more than 10 mm Hg (AHA 2025) or spontaneous arterial waves suggest ROSC; confirm with a rhythm and pulse check. A brief rise after bicarbonate does not.

Defibrillation: physics, pads and refractory VF

A shock ends VF by depolarizing a critical mass of myocardium at once so an organized pacemaker can take over. Current, not the energy setting, does this, and it falls as transthoracic impedance rises with poor pad contact, air under the pad, chest hair and lung inflation. Biphasic waveforms end VF at lower energy than monophasic ones and compensate for impedance. In BIPHASIC (Stiell 2007, 221 OHCA), escalating 200–300–360 J ended VF more often than a fixed 150 J when several shocks were needed (82.5% vs 71.2%).

In Chan 2008 (in-hospital VF and pulseless VT), defibrillation was delayed beyond 2 minutes in 30% of patients, and survival to discharge fell from 39.3% to 22.2%. Shock monitored VF at once, compress until the defibrillator arrives, and resume compressions immediately after every shock without a pulse check, because the post-shock rhythm is usually slow or stunned.

  • Anterolateral: right pad below the right clavicle; left pad at the apex in the midaxillary line (V6), beside or under the breast, not on it.
  • Anteroposterior: front pad over the left precordium, back pad left of the spine below the scapula (the DOSE-VF vector-change position).
  • Pacemaker or ICD: pads at least 8 cm from the generator; free-flowing oxygen 1 m from the chest (ERC 2021).

Refractory VF persists after three standard shocks. DOSE-VF (Cheskes 2022, 405 OHCA in Canada) randomized these patients to standard shocks, vector change (pads moved to anteroposterior) or double sequential defibrillation (DSED: a second defibrillator on anteroposterior pads fired right after the first). Survival to discharge was 13.3%, 21.7% and 30.4%, and only DSED improved good neurologic outcome (RR 2.21). The trial stopped early for COVID-19 and was unblinded; AHA 2025 rates both techniques 2b, usefulness not established.

Exam tip After three failed shocks, look past the shock: CPR quality, drug timing, a reversible cause (often an occluded coronary artery) and ECPR if available.

Airway and ventilation during arrest

In witnessed VF the blood stays oxygenated for the first minutes, so compressions and shocks come first; in asphyxial arrests (drowning, overdose, airway obstruction, many in-hospital arrests) oxygen and ventilation are part of the treatment. Every positive-pressure breath impedes venous return. Aufderheide 2004 recorded professional rescuers ventilating at 30 breaths/min; in pigs, 30/min versus 12/min lowered CPP (16.9 vs 23.4 mm Hg) and survival (1 of 7 vs 6 of 7). Hence 30:2 without an advanced airway and, with one, a breath every 6 seconds over about 1 second, just enough for visible chest rise.

Initial intubation success was only 52% in PART; bag-mask caused more regurgitation in CAAM (15.2% vs 7.5%).

Confirm any advanced airway with waveform capnography and recheck it after every move.

Safety A ventilated patient who arrests comes off the ventilator and is bagged with 100% oxygen while you check DOPE: displacement, obstruction, pneumothorax, equipment. In severe asthma or COPD, trapped gas can cause PEA; brief disconnection lets it escape (AHA 2010).

Drugs in arrest: what the trials show

No drug given during cardiac arrest has consistently improved survival with good neurologic function. Epinephrine's α₁ effect raises aortic diastolic pressure and CPP; its β effects raise myocardial oxygen demand and promote arrhythmias. PARAMEDIC2 (Perkins 2018, 8,014 UK OHCA) compared 1 mg epinephrine with placebo: 30-day survival 3.2% vs 2.4% (OR 1.39), favorable neurologic survival 2.2% vs 1.9% (not significant), and severe neurologic impairment in 31.0% vs 17.8% of survivors.

In nonshockable in-hospital arrest, survival fell stepwise with each 3-minute delay to the first dose (Donnino 2014, 25,095 patients; adjusted OR 0.63 when given after more than 9 minutes versus 1–3), so epinephrine is given as soon as access allows. In shockable in-hospital arrest, epinephrine within 2 minutes of the first shock was associated with lower survival (Andersen 2016, OR 0.70), consistent with giving it after the second shock.

ALPS (Kudenchuk 2016, 3,026 shock-refractory VF OHCA) found survival to discharge of 24.4% with amiodarone, 23.7% with lidocaine and 21.0% with placebo. Neither drug was significantly better overall, but both beat placebo in bystander-witnessed arrests. AHA treats the two as alternatives.

In PEA, QRS width narrows the search (Littmann 2014, a teaching approach, not AHA guidance). A narrow complex suggests a mechanical problem that ultrasound can show: tamponade, tension pneumothorax, hypovolemia or massive PE. A wide, slow complex suggests hyperkalemia, a sodium channel blocker or a failing ischemic ventricle, and points toward calcium and bicarbonate. Pseudo-PEA (cardiac motion, no palpable pulse) does better than a still heart (Gaspari 2016).

Ultrasound lengthens pauses: pulse checks with it took 21 versus 13 seconds in Huis in 't Veld 2017 and 17 versus 11 seconds in Clattenburg 2018. Record a clip during the pause and review it during compressions; the leader should not be the scanner. In REASON (Gaspari 2016, 793 patients), cardiac activity predicted survival, yet 0.6% without it survived to discharge, so a still heart alone does not justify stopping. A dilated right ventricle appeared in 57% of intra-arrest TEE studies (Teran 2019), so in a prolonged arrest it does not prove PE.

From about 20 weeks the uterus compresses the vena cava and aorta; manual displacement keeps the patient supine for good compressions. Drugs and energies do not change, delivery is prepared from the first minute, and a magnesium infusion is stopped, with calcium for suspected toxicity.

Below 30 °C, AHA 2025 suggests one shock, then holding further shocks and IV drugs until the core is above 30 °C (2b); ERC allows up to three shocks and doubles the epinephrine interval between 30 and 35 °C. AHA 2020 had allowed standard drugs and shocks during rewarming. Both favor extracorporeal rewarming, and case series report good neurologic survival after hours of CPR.

ECPR restores flow with venoarterial ECMO during refractory arrest. ARREST (Yannopoulos 2020, 30 refractory VF OHCA, one center) stopped early with survival to discharge of 43% versus 7%. Prague OHCA (Belohlavek 2022, 256 patients) narrowly missed its endpoint (180-day good neurologic survival 31.5% vs 22.0%, P=0.09), and INCEPTION (Suverein 2023, 134 patients at 10 Dutch centers, half of which cannulated two or fewer) found 20% vs 16%. AHA calls ECPR reasonable in selected patients within a trained, equipped system (2a, 2023), and AHA 2025 Part 4 adds regional programs and experienced cannulators.

  • Favor ECPR: bystander CPR, shockable rhythm, intermittent ROSC or signs of life, short low-flow time; against: age cutoffs, high lactate, low pH, low EtCO₂ (AHA 2025 Part 4).
  • During cannulation, keep compressions going (a mechanical device frees the groin), have heparin and blood ready, and keep an exact low-flow clock; ELSO 2021 aims for flow within 60 minutes.
  • Mechanical CPR did not beat manual CPR in LINC or PARAMEDIC; use it for transport, the cath lab and ECPR.

Post-arrest care: targets, temperature and prognosis

Post-cardiac arrest syndrome combines brain injury, myocardial dysfunction, a sepsis-like ischemia-reperfusion response and the cause of the arrest. Stunning usually improves within about 24 hours (Laurent 2002); brain injury causes most deaths after OHCA admission.

HACA 2002 (275 VF survivors, 32–34 °C for 24 h) raised favorable outcome from 39% to 55%; TTM 2013 (939 patients) found 33 °C no better than 36 °C; HYPERION 2019 (581 nonshockable arrests) favored 33 °C (good outcome 10.2% vs 5.7%); TTM2 2021 (1,850 patients) found 33 °C no better than normothermia with fever prevention, and 33 °C caused more unstable arrhythmias (24% vs 17%). Hence the wide AHA range; the constant is preventing fever. Prehospital cold saline added rearrest (26% vs 21%) without benefit (Kim 2014).

  • Temperature: feedback-controlled surface or intravascular device, bladder or esophageal probe, rewarming no faster than 0.5 °C/h.
  • Shivering: score it (Bedside Shivering Assessment Scale) and step up from acetaminophen, buspirone, magnesium and skin counterwarming to sedation and opioid, paralysis last.
  • Expect at 33 °C: sinus bradycardia, cold diuresis, falling potassium, rising glucose; potassium climbs again on rewarming.
  • Evaluate for organ donation everyone who progresses to death by neurologic criteria or planned withdrawal (AHA 2025 Part 4, class 1).

Prognostication is multimodal and delayed: AHA 2020 counted the 72-hour minimum from normothermia, ERC-ESICM 2021 from ROSC, and both wait for sedation to clear. ERC-ESICM 2021 calls a poor outcome likely when a patient with a motor score of 3 or less at 72 hours or later has two or more of: absent pupillary and corneal reflexes, bilaterally absent N20 SSEP waves, a highly malignant EEG after 24 hours, NSE above 60 µg/L at 48 or 72 hours, status myoclonus within 72 hours, or diffuse anoxic injury on CT or MRI.

Safety Early withdrawal for a presumed poor prognosis makes the prediction come true. Myoclonus with a continuous, reactive EEG can accompany recovery (Lance-Adams syndrome), so characterize it with EEG before acting on it.

In-hospital arrest: systems, teams and debriefing

Nearly 300,000 in-hospital arrests occur in the US each year, and about 19% of adults survive to discharge (AHA 2025 Parts 3 and 4). Most are preceded by visible decline: in Schein 1990, 84% of ward patients who arrested had documented deterioration within 8 hours, usually in breathing or mental status. AHA 2025 rates early warning scores, rapid response teams and safety huddles 2a; the nurse's escalation is the intervention.

AHA 2025 Part 4 recommends code teams of ALS-trained members (class 1) and designated or dedicated teams with defined roles and simulation training (2a), the pit crew model. Roles: leader, two or more compressors rotating every 2 minutes, airway, defibrillator, access and drugs, recorder, and a CPR coach who reads the feedback so the leader can think about causes. Closed-loop communication (order, read back, report done), a leader who summarizes each cycle aloud, and members who speak up hold the team together.

AHA 2025 Part 3 makes family presence the recommended approach in most situations; in Jabre 2013, relatives not offered the chance to watch had more PTSD symptoms (adjusted OR 1.7), with no effect on the resuscitation. No fixed duration ends an attempt: hospitals whose median attempt in nonsurvivors was 25 rather than 16 minutes had more survival to discharge (adjusted RR 1.12; Goldberger 2012, 64,339 arrests).

AHA 2025 Part 4 rates performance-focused debriefing 2a, ideally with objective data: compression rate, depth and fraction, defibrillator and EtCO₂ tracings, and the code record. A trained facilitator leads, and a hot debrief right after the event and a cold one a day to weeks later surface different problems. In Edelson 2008, weekly data-driven debriefing plus real-time feedback raised ROSC from 44.6% to 59.4%, though not survival to discharge.

Drug delivery: the flush and infusion-rate arithmetic

A drug pushed into a peripheral vein during CPR needs help reaching the heart. After each peripheral drug bolus, give a 20 mL fluid bolus to move it into the central circulation (2025 Korean guideline). Raising the limb may add gravity, but no systematic studies support it. The flush does not change the dose or the schedule, and IO access is for when IV access cannot be established.

Weight-based infusions all use the formula printed on the dopamine label: rate (mL/h) = dose (mcg/kg/min) × weight (kg) × 60 ÷ concentration (mcg/mL). Convert pounds to kilograms first (lb ÷ 2.2), work out the concentration from the bag (total drug ÷ total volume, in the units of the dose) and round once, at the end.

A dose ordered per minute without a weight (lidocaine 1–4 mg/min) skips the weight step: mg/min × 60 ÷ mg/mL. Bag strengths vary; calculate from the bag in hand.

Tachycardia with a pulse: the five signs of instability

The tachycardia algorithm asks whether the rhythm is causing any of five things: hypotension, acutely altered mental status, signs of shock, ischemic chest discomfort or acute heart failure. Any one of them, caused by the rhythm, makes the patient unstable and calls for synchronized cardioversion, sedated when feasible.

A normal blood pressure does not rule out instability: new pulmonary edema (crackles, fast breathing, a falling SpO₂) is acute heart failure and counts on its own. A fast rate alone and palpitations are not on the list; a stable patient with a regular narrow rhythm starts with vagal maneuvers and adenosine.

Adenosine: expected effects, cautions and special patients

Adenosine briefly slows conduction through the AV node and interrupts reentry circuits that pass through it. Red cells and vascular endothelium take it up so quickly that its half-life in blood is under 10 seconds: push it over 1–2 seconds as close to the patient as possible and follow with a rapid saline flush, or it may not reach the heart.

A regular narrow-complex tachycardia in a patient with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome is a labeled indication for adenosine. The rhythm in which AV nodal blockers are dangerous is pre-excited atrial fibrillation, which is irregular and wide: there the 2023 ACC/AHA guideline contraindicates adenosine along with verapamil, diltiazem, beta-blockers, digoxin and amiodarone.

Pregnancy does not change the order of treatment for stable SVT: vagal maneuvers first, then IV adenosine, the first-line drug (EHRA 2025; Tamirisa 2024). The label anticipates no fetal effects from a naturally occurring nucleoside, but without studies it advises use only if clearly needed. An unstable patient gets synchronized cardioversion at the usual energies. Amiodarone can cause fetal harm (thyroid, cardiac, neurodevelopmental and growth effects) and is kept for life-threatening arrhythmias when other measures have failed.

Diltiazem for rate control: doses and limits

  • First bolus: 0.25 mg/kg of actual body weight IV over 2 minutes (about 20 mg for an average adult).
  • If the response is inadequate after 15 minutes, the second bolus is larger: 0.35 mg/kg over 2 minutes (about 25 mg).
  • For continued rate control in atrial fibrillation or flutter, an infusion starts at 10 mg/h (5 mg/h suits some patients), rises in 5 mg/h steps to 15 mg/h and runs for up to 24 hours.
  • IV diltiazem and an IV beta-blocker are not given together or within a few hours of each other (a label contraindication): bradycardia, AV block and depressed contractility can add.
  • Other label contraindications: sick sinus syndrome or second- or third-degree AV block without a ventricular pacemaker, severe hypotension or cardiogenic shock, atrial fibrillation or flutter with an accessory pathway (WPW), and ventricular tachycardia.

A weak ventricle changes the choice. For acute rate control in atrial fibrillation, the 2023 ACC/AHA guideline recommends a beta-blocker, or diltiazem or verapamil provided the EF is above 40%, and rates IV diltiazem or verapamil 3: Harm with known moderate or severe LV systolic dysfunction. The diltiazem label adds that oral diltiazem is contraindicated in acute myocardial infarction with pulmonary congestion on the admission x-ray and that experience with the IV form in impaired ventricular function is limited. Hypotension is the most common adverse event (symptomatic in 3.2%).

Amiodarone infusions: hypotension, concentration and interactions

Hypotension is the most common adverse reaction to IV amiodarone (16% in trials). It appears mostly in the first hours and is related to the rate of infusion, not the dose, so the first step is to slow the infusion; vasopressors, inotropes and volume are added if the pressure stays low. Bradycardia (4.9%) and AV block are also treated by slowing or stopping the infusion, with temporary pacing available.

  • Concentration: above 3 mg/mL in D5W, peripheral vein phlebitis is frequent. For infusions longer than 1 hour, do not exceed 2 mg/mL unless a central venous catheter is used.
  • Check the bag: the label's 150 mg in 100 mL is 1.5 mg/mL and 900 mg in 500 mL is 1.8 mg/mL; the same 900 mg in 250 mL would be 3.6 mg/mL, too concentrated for a peripheral vein.
  • Use a volumetric pump (smaller drops make drop-counting sets underdose by up to 30%), an in-line filter and, whenever possible, a dedicated central line.
  • Infusions longer than 2 hours go in glass or polyolefin containers of D5W. Amiodarone adsorbs to PVC tubing, but the trials used PVC tubing and the labeled doses already allow for it.

Lidocaine and procainamide: ceilings and monitoring

In refractory VF or pulseless VT, lidocaine is 1–1.5 mg/kg IV, then 0.5–0.75 mg/kg every 5–10 minutes, up to a maximum total dose of 3 mg/kg (2025 Korean guideline; EHRA 2025). For a 70 kg patient the ceiling is 210 mg; once it is reached, no more lidocaine is given.

For stable monomorphic VT, lidocaine is the less effective choice. It blocks sodium channels mainly in depolarized (ischemic) or very rapidly firing tissue, and in a randomized comparison it ended 3 of 14 tolerated VTs against 12 of 15 for procainamide (Gorgels 1996). EHRA 2025 calls IV lidocaine only moderately effective, an option for recurrent stable VT when beta-blockers or amiodarone fail or amiodarone cannot be used; with severe heart failure or acute MI it prefers amiodarone to procainamide. At usual doses lidocaine does not change contractility or arterial pressure, so the reason to pass over it is efficacy, not hemodynamics.

  • Lidocaine maintenance: 1–4 mg/min on a pump with constant ECG monitoring. The premixed 0.4% bag (4 mg/mL) runs at 15–60 mL/h and the 0.8% bag (8 mg/mL) at 7.5–30 mL/h.
  • Halve the rate after about 24 hours, because clearance falls during prolonged infusion; use lower rates in hepatic impairment and severe renal impairment (eGFR under 30).
  • Clearance is hepatic (CYP1A2 and CYP3A4). Fluvoxamine, a CYP1A2 inhibitor, raises IV lidocaine exposure by 71% and cuts its clearance by 41–60%; cimetidine, propofol and beta-blockers also raise levels, and inducers such as phenytoin lower them.
  • Toxicity follows the blood level. Nervous system signs include tingling of the tongue and lips, tinnitus, drowsiness, tremor and seizures. Stop the infusion at the earliest sign.
  • Lidocaine is contraindicated in Stokes-Adams syndrome, Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome and severe sinoatrial, AV or intraventricular block.

Procainamide slows conduction, so the PR, QRS and QT lengthen. If first-degree AV block develops during procainamide, the label advises reducing the dose; if the block persists, continuing is weighed against the risk of higher-grade block. Stop temporarily if the blood pressure falls 15 mm Hg or more; QRS widening over 25% is a warning and 50% is the stopping point. Expect lower rates in renal impairment and in patients over 50, because procainamide and its active metabolite NAPA are cleared by the kidneys. It is contraindicated in complete heart block and torsades de pointes.

Atropine, dopamine and epinephrine: label cautions

The atropine injection label lists no contraindications. Its warnings are weighed against the danger of the bradycardia: atropine may precipitate acute glaucoma, cause urinary retention with prostatic hypertrophy, turn partial pyloric stenosis into obstruction and thicken bronchial secretions in chronic lung disease. A history of glaucoma does not withhold the 1 mg dose from a patient with bradycardia and poor perfusion; afterward, watch for sudden eye pain, cloudy vision, halos around lights or a red eye and report them at once. Small doses may first slow the heart before the rate rises.

In coronary artery disease, atropine-induced tachycardia raises myocardial oxygen demand, so the label restricts repeated dosing to a total of 2–3 mg (0.03–0.04 mg/kg). The total is a ceiling, not a target: once the bradycardia has resolved, further doses are held, and a rate that overshoots with new chest pain or ST changes is reported as worsening ischemia. If symptomatic bradycardia returns, pacing or a dopamine or epinephrine infusion can take the place of more atropine.

Dopamine's only label contraindication is pheochromocytoma. About 75% of a dose is broken down by monoamine oxidase (MAO) and COMT, so an MAO inhibitor prolongs and strengthens its effect and can cause severe hypertension and arrhythmia. After an MAO inhibitor in the past 2–3 weeks (isocarboxazid, phenelzine, tranylcypromine, rasagiline, selegiline or the antibiotic linezolid), the label cuts the starting dose to no more than one-tenth of the usual dose: 0.5 mcg/kg/min in place of 5 mcg/kg/min. Infuse into a large vein; extravasation is treated by infiltrating phentolamine 5–10 mg in 10–15 mL of saline.

Some epinephrine products, Adrenalin among them, contain a sulfite that can cause allergic or asthmatic reactions in sensitive patients. The label states that the sulfite should not preclude epinephrine in a serious allergic or other emergency, even in a sulfite-sensitive patient, because the alternatives may not be satisfactory. In cardiac arrest the 1 mg dose is given on schedule, without waiting for another product or giving a pretreatment. As an infusion (labeled for hypotension in septic shock) the range is 0.05–2 mcg/kg/min, adjusted every 10–15 minutes in steps of 0.05–0.2 mcg/kg/min, diluted in a dextrose-containing solution (saline alone is not recommended) and run into a large vein.

Atrial fibrillation: rate control in lung disease and cardioversion drug choices

For AF with a rapid ventricular response in a hemodynamically stable patient, the 2023 ACC/AHA guideline recommends a beta-blocker, or diltiazem or verapamil provided the EF is above 40%, for acute rate control. Its IV doses: metoprolol 2.5–5 mg over 2 minutes, up to 3 doses; esmolol 500 mcg/kg over 1 minute, then 50–300 mcg/kg/min.

COPD is not a reason to withhold the beta-blocker: the guideline calls cardioselective beta-blockers reasonable for rate control of AF in patients with COPD (2a). The hesitation came from the fear of blocking inhaled beta-2 agonists or provoking bronchospasm, but a meta-analysis of randomized trials found that cardioselective beta-blockers changed neither respiratory function and symptoms nor the response to beta-2 agonists, and in observational studies beta-blocker use was associated with lower mortality in COPD. Asthma is different: beta-blockers can worsen reactive airway disease and are generally avoided there.

When AF returns immediately after a shock that restored sinus rhythm, the guideline's answer is an antiarrhythmic drug given before the next cardioversion and continued afterward, to help the shock succeed and sinus rhythm last. The technical steps (higher energy, an anteroposterior pad position, manual pressure on the pads) serve a different purpose: they improve energy delivery when a shock has not converted the rhythm.

Irregular wide-complex tachycardia and catecholaminergic polymorphic VT

An irregularly irregular wide-complex tachycardia has three main causes: AF with a bundle branch block (preexisting or rate-related), pre-excited AF and polymorphic VT. The comparison with an earlier ECG and the beat-to-beat look of the QRS separate them.

Catecholaminergic polymorphic VT (CPVT) is an inherited disorder of calcium handling in the heart muscle cell, most often from a variant in the cardiac ryanodine receptor gene (RyR2). The heart is structurally normal and the resting ECG is normal. Exercise or emotional stress provokes bidirectional VT (the QRS axis flips 180° from one beat to the next) or polymorphic VT, with syncope or sudden death, typically in children and young adults; about one-third of VT episodes may degenerate to ventricular fibrillation. Bidirectional VT is also seen in digitalis toxicity.

Beta-adrenergic stimulation triggers the calcium release that sets off the arrhythmia, so beta-blockers are the first-line treatment and the cornerstone of CPVT therapy. A nonselective agent is preferred (nadolol 1–2 mg/kg daily, or propranolol where nadolol is unavailable), and flecainide is added for breakthrough arrhythmias. Catecholamines push the wrong way: isoproterenol and epinephrine infusions are used to provoke the arrhythmia as a diagnostic test, and the pain and fear of an ICD shock can drive an electrical storm.

Exam pearls

  • CPP is aortic minus right atrial diastolic pressure; no patient reached ROSC below 15 mm Hg (Paradis 1990).
  • Compression fraction at least 60%; pre-shock pause under 10 seconds; charge during compressions.
  • Overventilation lowers CPP: with an advanced airway, 1 breath every 6 seconds and nonstop compressions.
  • EtCO₂ under 10 mm Hg means improve CPR; a sudden rise of more than 10 suggests ROSC.
  • Epinephrine: after the second shock in VF, as soon as possible in PEA or asystole; it improves survival, not proven neurologic recovery (PARAMEDIC2).
  • Amiodarone and lidocaine are alternatives after the third shock; neither raised overall survival to discharge in ALPS.
  • Narrow-complex PEA suggests a mechanical cause (tamponade, pneumothorax, PE, hypovolemia); wide suggests hyperkalemia, toxins or ischemia.
  • Pregnancy: manual left uterine displacement, and delivery within 5 minutes if there is no ROSC.
  • After ROSC: SpO₂ 90–98%, PaCO₂ 35–45, MAP 65 or more, 32–37.5 °C for at least 36 h, emergent angiography for STEMI.
  • Prognosis: no earlier than 72 hours, two or more concordant findings, never one test.
