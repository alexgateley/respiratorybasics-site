Post-cardiac arrest care: the targets

Post-cardiac arrest care after ROSC aims to stop a second injury to the brain and heart: avoid low and high oxygen, keep CO₂ normal, keep MAP at least 65 mm Hg, get a 12-lead, control temperature in anyone who cannot follow commands, and wait at least 72 hours before judging neurologic outcome.

ROSC bundle: the first hour

  1. Airway: intubate if comatose; confirm with waveform capnography.
  2. Breathing: full oxygen until a reliable SpO₂ or ABG, then titrate down; normal PaCO₂.
  3. Circulation: arterial line, fluids and vasopressors to MAP 65 or more; norepinephrine is the usual first choice.
  4. 12-lead ECG as soon as possible after ROSC; look for ST elevation.
  5. Find the cause: ABG and electrolytes, echo or POCUS, and CT (head, chest, pulmonary angiogram) when no cardiac cause is clear.
  6. Temperature: continuous core probe (esophageal or bladder); start temperature control if not following commands.
  7. Neuro: exam off sedation when possible, EEG if comatose, short-acting sedation.
Safety Do not hyperventilate. Low CO₂ constricts cerebral vessels and can add ischemia to an injured brain.

Post-Cardiac Arrest Care and Temperature Control practice question

A surface temperature control device is being set up for a comatose patient 1 hour after ROSC. The patient has an orogastric tube and an indwelling urinary catheter. Which temperature source should drive the device's feedback?

  1. Rectal temperatures charted by the nurse hourly
  2. Tympanic temperatures entered manually at 15-minute intervals
  3. A continuous esophageal or bladder core probe
  4. A skin probe on the chest under the cooling pad

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because rectal temperature approximates core temperature, but hourly readings are too infrequent for a device that adjusts its output minute to minute.
  • B. Tempting because tympanic readings are quick and noninvasive, but manual spot checks cannot give the continuous signal a feedback device needs.
  • C. Correct. Guidelines recommend continuous core monitoring with an esophageal or urinary bladder probe feeding the device.
  • D. Tempting because it reads continuously, but skin under a cooling pad reflects the pad rather than the core, so the device would steer toward the wrong number.

Guidelines recommend continuous core temperature monitoring with an esophageal or urinary bladder probe, and a temperature control device that uses that continuous signal for thermoregulatory feedback so the core stays within the target range. Feedback devices adjust the pad or catheter temperature continually, which spot readings cannot support. Surface and intravascular methods give similar survival and neurologic outcomes; intravascular cooling reaches target faster and fluctuates less, but either method is only as accurate as the core probe that steers it.

Sources: Singapore Post-cardiac Arrest Care Guidelines (2026); 2025 Korean CPR Guidelines: Post-Cardiac Arrest Care

Temperature control after cardiac arrest

Targeted temperature management is now called temperature control. Anyone who does not follow commands after ROSC gets it, whatever the rhythm or location of the arrest: a constant temperature between 32 and 37.5 °C for at least 36 hours, with active fever prevention afterward.

  • Use a device with temperature feedback and a continuous core probe (esophageal or bladder).
  • Do not actively warm a patient who is mildly hypothermic after ROSC.
  • No rapid cold IV fluid boluses in the field.
  • If cooled below normal, rewarm slowly and delay rewarming with instability, raised ICP, severe hyperkalemia or ongoing seizures.
  • Expect and treat shivering; cooling often slows the heart rate, which is usually fine if perfusion holds.
Exam tip Current exam answer: deliberate temperature control at a constant 32–37.5 °C and fever prevention, not routine cooling to 33 °C.

Seizures, glucose and sedation

  • EEG for every patient who stays comatose: seizures, including nonconvulsive status, occur in about 10–35%.
  • Treat clinical seizures and EEG seizures promptly; no prophylactic antiseizure drugs.
  • Myoclonus needs an EEG before treatment: not all of it is epileptic. Shivering can look like seizure activity.
  • Glucose about 140–180 mg/dL with an insulin infusion if needed; expect insulin needs to fall during rewarming.
  • Short-acting sedation (propofol, remifentanil) so the neuro exam can be done early.

Neuroprognostication: when and how

Do not predict a poor outcome early. Wait at least 72 hours after ROSC (after rewarming if cooled below normal), clear sedatives, paralytics and metabolic confounders, and use several tests together.

Post-cardiac arrest syndrome

After the whole body has been without flow and then reperfused, four problems overlap: hypoxic-ischemic brain injury, myocardial dysfunction (common, and usually starting to recover by 2–3 days), a sepsis-like systemic ischemia-reperfusion response with vasoplegia, and whatever caused the arrest. Brain injury is the most common cause of death after ROSC, most often through withdrawal of life support, which is why early prognosis must be cautious.

Oxygen and ventilation evidence

  • Right after ROSC, use the highest available FiO₂ until saturation can be measured reliably. EXACT, which lowered the early prehospital target to 90–94%, saw more hypoxemia.
  • BOX found no benefit from a lower PaO₂ target (68–75 mm Hg) in comatose patients.
  • TAME found no benefit from mild hypercapnia over normocapnia; normal PaCO₂ is the target.
  • During hypothermic control, check PaCO₂ often and use temperature-corrected or uncorrected gases consistently, as your lab does.

Hemodynamics after ROSC

  • Every patient needs an arterial line; add cardiac output monitoring (echo, PA catheter or transpulmonary thermodilution) if unstable.
  • Restore MAP with fluids and vasopressors before adding an inotrope; no vasopressor has proven superior.
  • A bradycardia under 30–40/min during cooling can be left alone if perfusion is adequate.
  • Cardiogenic shock that persists despite drugs may need mechanical support; see the cardiogenic section of Shock Types at a Glance.

The current guidance allows any constant target from 32 to 37.5 °C. Some experts still favor 32–34 °C for the most brain-injured patients, especially after long, unwitnessed arrests, because the trial populations had mostly shockable, witnessed arrests.

Nursing care during temperature control

  • Core temperature continuously; skin checks under pads every shift.
  • Shivering raises oxygen use and can look like seizure activity; treat it per protocol (IV magnesium is one option) and get an EEG for any new movement.
  • Cooling: cold diuresis and shifts lower potassium. Rewarming: potassium rebounds and insulin sensitivity rises, so glucose can fall.
  • Some centers keep sodium at 140–145 mmol/L and potassium at 4.0–4.5 mmol/L (tolerating 3.0–3.5 while cooled to avoid rebound).
  • Early trophic tube feeding, DVT prophylaxis, and stress ulcer prophylaxis if anticoagulated or coagulopathic.
  • Family updates: explain that outcome cannot be judged in the first days.

Favorable signs and outcome talk

  • Signs that recovery is likely: localizing or withdrawing to pain (GCS motor 4–5) at 72–96 h, a continuous normal-voltage EEG within 72 h, NSE 17 mcg/L or less at 24–72 h, and no diffusion changes on MRI at 2–7 days.
  • With neither good nor bad signs, outcome is indeterminate: keep observing and examining.
  • Neurofilament light and GFAP are promising early markers, but thresholds are not standardized.

Pregnancy after ROSC

A pregnant patient who stays comatose after ROSC gets temperature control by the same criteria as any other adult, with fetal monitoring attached during it and obstetric and neonatal specialists working closely with the ICU team (Korean 2025).

  • Fetal monitors come off during CPR because they get in the way and fetal status does not change the resuscitation. Once circulation and stability return, the fetus is assessed again with fetal heart rate monitoring, cardiotocography or ultrasound (Febrasgo 2026).
  • Once compressions are no longer needed, keep the uterus off the vena cava: left lateral position (Febrasgo: 90° left lateral decubitus), including after a resuscitative hysterotomy.
  • Keep MAP above 65 mm Hg; maternal hemodynamic stability is what oxygenates the fetus (Febrasgo 2026).
  • During maternal hypothermia the fetal heart rate can show a lower baseline and reduced variability. Absent variability with decelerations points to fetal hypoxia and acidosis (Febrasgo 2026).
  • With circulation restored, the arrest no longer sets the timing of delivery. The decision weighs signs of fetal compromise, maternal surgical risk and viability at 22 to 24 weeks or more (Febrasgo 2026).
  • Fever is avoided in every case. Febrasgo 2026 notes that the benefit of cooling below normal is not defined for patients who are still pregnant or have just delivered, where bleeding is a concern.
Exam tip Pregnancy does not change the indication for temperature control after ROSC. It adds fetal monitoring and an obstetric and neonatal team.

Exam pearls

  • STEMI after ROSC: cath lab even if comatose.
  • Comatose after ROSC: temperature control 32–37.5 °C, prevent fever.
  • SpO₂ 100% after the first measurement is not the goal; titrate down.
  • Hyperventilating to a PaCO₂ of 25–30 is wrong.
  • A single bad finding at 24 h is not enough to predict outcome.
  • Myoclonus without EEG correlate: do not treat as status epilepticus.
  • Pregnant and comatose after ROSC: same temperature control criteria, fetal monitoring back on.
