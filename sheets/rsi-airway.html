Try one

Airway and RSI practice question

After an oropharyngeal airway is inserted in a patient recovering from sedation, the patient begins to gag and retch. What should the therapist do?

  1. Remove the airway
  2. Push it in farther until the gagging stops
  3. Tape the airway in place so that it cannot be expelled
  4. Insert a larger size to hold the tongue forward

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Gagging means protective reflexes have returned. Leaving the airway in risks vomiting, aspiration and laryngospasm.
  • B. This can cause laryngospasm and trauma.
  • C. Securing it increases the risk of vomiting and aspiration.
  • D. A larger airway stimulates the pharynx more.

An oropharyngeal airway is used only when the gag reflex is absent. If obstruction continues after it is removed, the options are a jaw thrust, the recovery position or a nasopharyngeal airway. Suction should be within reach whenever an airway adjunct is in use.

Sources: Egan's Fundamentals of Resp Care, 13th ed.; Walls Manual of Emergency Airway, 6th ed.

RSI: the 7 Ps

  1. Preparation: roles, backup devices, suction, IV, drugs.
  2. Preoxygenation: 3 min tidal or 8 vital-capacity breaths; NIV if hypoxemic; nasal cannula for apneic oxygenation.
  3. Pretreatment: fix hypotension and hypoxemia first.
  4. Paralysis with induction: induction agent, then paralytic at once.
  5. Positioning: head up, sniffing or ramped.
  6. Placement with proof: waveform capnography.
  7. Post-intubation care: secure, x-ray, sedation, ventilator.
Safety Failed airway: limit attempts (2–3) and oxygenate with an SGA or bag-mask. Can't intubate, can't oxygenate: cricothyrotomy per protocol.

Confirm the tube

  • Waveform capnography plus clinical assessment: most reliable (AHA 2025, Class 1 in arrest).
  • Sustained CO₂: a waveform every breath, peaks over 7.5 mm Hg, steady or rising (not fading), for at least 7 breaths.
  • Breath sounds can't rule out the esophagus; x-ray checks depth, not placement.
  • Depth at the incisors: about 20–21 cm women, 22–23 cm men.
Safety No trace = wrong place. Without sustained CO₂, the default is to remove the tube.

The physiologically difficult airway: resuscitate first

In the ICU most peri-intubation harm is physiological, not anatomical. In INTUBE (Russotto, JAMA 2021; 2,964 patients in 29 countries), 45.2% had at least one major adverse peri-intubation event: cardiovascular instability 42.6%, severe hypoxemia 9.3%, cardiac arrest 3.1%. Induction removes sympathetic tone, positive pressure cuts venous return, and apnea stops CO₂ clearance while a shunted lung desaturates within seconds. Mosier's "HOp killers" (hypotension, oxygenation, pH) name the states to fix first; right ventricular failure is the fourth.

ProblemWhy induction makes it worseBefore induction
Hypotension, shock index ≥ 0.9Lost sympathetic tone; less venous returnVasopressor running; push-dose drawn; lower dose
HypoxemiaShunt and small FRC: fast desaturationNIV or HFNC preoxygenation; PEEP; head up
Metabolic acidosisApnea stops respiratory compensationShort apnea; match minute ventilation after
RV failure, pulmonary HTNHypoxemia, CO₂, PPV raise PVR; low MAPNorepinephrine or vasopressin; inhaled vasodilator

Fluids alone do not prevent collapse: in PREPARE II (Russell, JAMA 2022; 1,065 patients) a 500 mL bolus at induction did not reduce cardiovascular collapse (21.0% vs 18.2%). For most patients the more reliable step is a vasopressor started before induction. Push-dose phenylephrine (50–200 mcg) or epinephrine (10–20 mcg) covers the gap; label every syringe, because dilution errors are the main hazard.

In severe metabolic acidosis (DKA, salicylate toxicity, lactic acidosis) the patient may be moving 20–30 L/min to hold a PaCO₂ near 15–20 mm Hg. A default ventilator rate of 14–16 lets the pH fall further within minutes; set the rate around 30 and check a gas within 30 minutes. In RV failure the spiral is low MAP, RV ischemia, lower output and lower MAP: keep MAP up before induction, start an inhaled pulmonary vasodilator if available, avoid hypoxemia and hypercapnia, and use modest PEEP.

Safety Hypotension soon after intubation: think drugs and lost sympathetic tone, auto-PEEP (disconnect briefly and watch the BP), tension pneumothorax and a misplaced tube.

Preoxygenation and apneic oxygenation

Preoxygenation replaces the nitrogen in the functional residual capacity with oxygen; the target is an end-tidal O₂ of at least 85–90%. In Benumof's modeling a healthy, fully preoxygenated adult takes about 6–8 minutes to reach SpO₂ 90%; an obese or critically ill patient with shunt can get there in under 2. Head up 20–30° or ramped (ear level with the sternal notch) enlarges the FRC.

MethodWhat it deliversBest use
Nonrebreather, 15 L/minFiO₂ often only 0.6–0.7 (entrainment)Normal lungs; turn to flush rate

PREOXI (Gibbs, NEJM 2024; 1,301 ED and ICU patients) compared NIV with an oxygen mask: hypoxemia (SpO₂ under 85%) fell from 18.5% to 9.1%, cardiac arrest was 0.2% vs 1.1%, and aspiration did not increase (0.9% vs 1.4%). PreVent (Casey, NEJM 2019; 401 ICU patients) found gentle bag-mask ventilation between induction and laryngoscopy cut severe hypoxemia from 22.8% to 10.9% without more aspiration; older exam items may still expect "do not bag after paralysis".

Apneic oxygenation works because the alveoli take up about 250 mL/min of oxygen and return little CO₂, so gas flows toward them through an open airway; CO₂ still rises. The randomized trials are neutral: FELLOW (Semler, AJRCCM 2016; ICU) found lowest SpO₂ 92% vs 90%, and ENDAO (Caputo, Acad Emerg Med 2017; 200 ED patients, cannula at 15 L/min or more) 92% vs 93%. Observational data favor it and it is cheap, so most protocols keep it.

Safety Pulse oximetry lags arterial saturation, more in shock. Once SpO₂ starts to fall, the arterial value is already lower; stop and reoxygenate early.
Exam tip Apneic oxygenation needs a patent airway: keep the jaw thrust and the cannula on through the attempt.

Induction and paralysis: pharmacology in depth

Give the induction agent and then the paralytic at once, each as a rapid push with a flush. In shock, cut the induction dose by about half (a smaller central volume gives higher brain and heart levels) and raise rocuronium toward 1.2–1.6 mg/kg, because slow circulation delays onset. Dose succinylcholine by total body weight; many references dose rocuronium by ideal or lean weight in obesity.

Agent and adult doseHemodynamics and mechanismDuration and cautions
Etomidate 0.3 mg/kg (0.15–0.2 in shock)GABA-A; most neutral3–5 min; 11β-hydroxylase inhibition
Ketamine 1–2 mg/kg (0.5–1 in shock)NMDA antagonist; releases catecholamines10–15 min; depresses a depleted heart
Propofol 1.5–2.5 mg/kg (less if frail)GABA-A; vasodilator, negative inotrope5–10 min; most hypotension
Midazolam 0.15–0.35 mg/kgGABA-A; slow onset 1.5–2.5 minLongest; poor RSI agent
Fentanyl 1–3 mcg/kg, pretreatmentBlunts the laryngoscopy surgeICH, dissection, ischemia; skip in shock

Ketamine's direct effect on the myocardium is depressant, which shows when catecholamine stores are exhausted. That is the likely reason the RSI trial (Casey, NEJM 2026; 2,364 patients) found more cardiovascular collapse with ketamine than etomidate (22.1% vs 17.0%) and no difference in 28-day mortality. Etomidate suppresses cortisol synthesis for 6–8 hours; SCCM 2023 found no mortality difference and does not recommend routine steroids after one dose.

Succinylcholine depolarizes the acetylcholine receptor, causing fasciculations and then flaccid paralysis, and is hydrolyzed by plasma cholinesterase. Potassium rises about 0.5–1 mEq/L normally. After denervation, burns, crush, prolonged immobility or myopathy, extrajunctional receptors spread over the muscle and the rise can arrest the heart; the risk starts after about the first day and peaks around 7–10 days. It also triggers malignant hyperthermia and masseter spasm and causes bradycardia with repeat doses and in children.

Rocuronium is a competitive nondepolarizing blocker cleared by the liver and bile, so it lasts longer in liver failure and in older patients. The Cochrane review (Tran 2015) found succinylcholine superior overall for excellent intubating conditions but no significant difference at rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg. The short action of succinylcholine is rarely a rescue in the ICU: a critically ill patient desaturates long before breathing returns. Sugammadex 16 mg/kg reverses rocuronium within about 3 minutes but does not open an obstructed upper airway.

Exam tip Exams match agent to patient: etomidate for instability, ketamine for bronchospasm, rocuronium for high potassium or an injury days old, fentanyl pretreatment for raised ICP or aortic dissection.

Video laryngoscopy is now the default in critically ill adults. DEVICE (Prekker, NEJM 2023; 1,417 ED and ICU patients) found first-attempt success of 85.1% with video vs 70.8% with direct laryngoscopy, with similar severe complications; it stopped early for efficacy. A hyperangulated blade gives an easy view but a harder pass and needs a rigid stylet shaped to the blade.

The bougie helps most with a direct laryngoscope and a poor view. In BEAM (Driver, JAMA 2018; 757 ED patients, mostly direct) first-attempt success was 98% vs 87%, and 96% vs 82% with a difficult airway feature. In BOUGIE (Driver, JAMA 2021; 1,102 ED and ICU patients, mostly video) it was 80.4% vs 83.0% with a stylet, no difference, and severe hypoxemia was 11.0% vs 8.8%. Keep it ready as a rescue rather than routine with video.

Each attempt costs oxygen and swells the airway; Mort (Anesth Analg 2004) found more than two attempts multiplied hypoxemia, aspiration and arrest several-fold. The DAS critically ill guideline (Higgs, BJA 2018) limits Plan A to 3 laryngoscopy attempts plus 1 by an expert, then Plans B and C, oxygenation with a second-generation supraglottic airway (up to 3 attempts) or two-person face mask, then Plan D, front-of-neck access. The Vortex approach (Chrimes) gives each of the three lifelines (face mask, SGA, tube) one best effort of up to 3 attempts; capnography-confirmed oxygenation by any of them is the green zone, and failure of all three is can't intubate, can't oxygenate (CICO).

  1. Declare CICO out loud; give a full paralytic dose; keep oxygen on face and SGA.
  2. Extend the neck; find the cricothyroid membrane with the laryngeal handshake.
  3. Transverse stab through skin and membrane with a size 10 blade, cutting edge toward the operator.
  4. Rotate the blade 90° so the sharp edge points toward the feet; pull it toward the operator.
  5. Slide a bougie along the blade into the trachea; railroad a 6.0 cuffed tube; inflate and confirm CO₂.
  6. Membrane not palpable: 8–10 cm vertical midline incision, blunt dissection, then the same steps.
Safety Call CICO while oxygenation is failing, not after arrest. The nurse calls out time, saturations and attempts, opens the cricothyrotomy kit and keeps oxygen running.

A better view at laryngoscopy, and the plan after a supraglottic rescue

The Difficult Airway Society 2015 guideline (Frerk, BJA 2015) treats a poor attempt as a wasted attempt: change something before trying again (position, blade or device, bougie or stylet, depth of paralysis, operator). For a Macintosh-type blade the best position is the sniffing position, neck flexed and head extended; in obesity, ramp until the ear canal is level with the sternal notch.

External laryngeal manipulation brings the larynx into the line of sight. The laryngoscopist does it with the right hand, or an assistant applies backward, upward and rightward pressure (BURP) on the thyroid cartilage. With a video laryngoscope the assistant can watch the screen and see the effect of the pressure.

BURP is not cricoid pressure. Cricoid pressure is applied to the cricoid ring to limit regurgitation during rapid sequence induction; poorly applied, it can make laryngoscopy, mask ventilation and supraglottic airway insertion harder, and the guideline says to release it if laryngoscopy is difficult. A bougie helps when the view is grade 2 or 3a; passing it blind with a grade 3b or 4 view risks trauma and is not recommended.

After failed intubation (3 attempts, plus 1 by a more experienced colleague) the guideline moves to a second-generation supraglottic airway, with at most 3 insertion attempts. Once it oxygenates the patient, confirmed by capnography, the team is told to stop and think. The principle is to keep oxygenating while limiting aspiration risk and further airway attempts. Laryngeal mask cuffs: pressure, seal and leaks

A laryngeal mask seals around the laryngeal inlet, not inside the trachea. Unlike an endotracheal tube cuff, its seal does not improve as the cuff pressure rises: overinflation makes the mask more rigid and less able to mold to the patient's anatomy, so a leak can get worse (Merck Manual).

  • Poor seal with a tight, overfilled cuff: let some air out and recheck the seal. If the leak persists, change to a larger mask.
  • Manufacturers set the limit at an intracuff pressure of 60 cm H₂O. In four adult mask models about half of the maximum labeled volume (two thirds for the ProSeal) already reached 60 cm H₂O, and the full labeled volume exceeded it in every model (Ruananukun 2020). Use a cuff manometer.
  • Overinflation and long placement can compress the tongue and cause tongue edema. Cuff pressure is also implicated in sore throat and in the rare lingual, hypoglossal and recurrent laryngeal nerve injuries reported with these devices (Michalek 2015).
  • Do not answer a leak with more air or more ventilating pressure: excessive ventilation through a laryngeal mask is a cause of vomiting and aspiration.
  • The mask limits gastric inflation compared with bag-mask ventilation and gives some protection against passive regurgitation, but it does not isolate the airway the way an endotracheal tube does. In emergencies it is a bridging device.
Exam tip With an endotracheal tube, more cuff pressure means a tighter seal. With a laryngeal mask, the first step for a leak is less air, not more.

Nasotracheal intubation: uses and contraindications

Nasotracheal intubation is used infrequently: for a patient who is awake and breathing spontaneously, or when the mouth must be avoided or cannot open widely enough. Oral injuries, swelling or limited jaw motion that make laryngoscopy difficult are therefore reasons to consider the nasal route, not reasons against it (Merck Manual).

It is absolutely contraindicated with a midface fracture or a known or suspected basal skull fracture (Merck Manual). The most serious risk is accidental intracranial placement of the tube; sinus infection and mediastinal emphysema are others (Phillips 2017). Le Fort III fractures in particular are often associated with cribriform plate damage and dural tears (Saini 2021).

Check the face before choosing the route. Stabilize the head with one hand, grasp the upper alveolar arch (the upper front teeth and palate) with the other and pull forward: an upper jaw that moves is a midface fracture, and the structures that move with it show the Le Fort level (AO Surgery Reference). Periorbital bruising supports the diagnosis, and clear fluid from the nose or ear after trauma is assumed to be cerebrospinal fluid until proved otherwise.

  • Before a nasal tube: a topical vasoconstrictor (for example phenylephrine) and a topical anesthetic to the nasal mucosa.
  • Epistaxis is a serious complication, because blood in the airway hides the view; bleeding disorders and anticoagulants raise the risk.
  • Sinusitis is universal after 3 days, and tubes of 8 mm or larger, needed for bronchoscopy, can rarely be passed through the nose.
Safety Upper teeth and palate move when grasped, raccoon eyes or clear nasal drainage: nothing goes through the nose. Plan an oral or surgical airway.

Confirmation and the first hour after intubation

Waveform capnography confirms the trachea (sustained trace for at least 7 breaths, PUMA 2022); in cardiac arrest the values are low but the waveform persists. The chest x-ray confirms depth, not placement: tip about 3–5 cm above the carina with the head neutral. Neck flexion moves the tip toward the carina and extension moves it up, roughly 2 cm each way; a deep tube usually enters the more vertical right mainstem.

Cuff and tube care, and unplanned extubation

Keep the cuff at 20–30 cm H₂O, measured with a manometer, not by feel. Above about 30 cm H₂O it exceeds tracheal mucosal perfusion pressure (Seegobin 1984), risking ischemia, stenosis, tracheomalacia and tracheoesophageal fistula; below 20, secretions pooled above it leak into the lungs. Recheck each shift, after moving the tube or patient, and during air transport, because cuff volume expands with altitude.

The SHEA/IDSA/APIC 2022 compendium lists as essential: avoid intubation where NIV or high-flow works, minimize sedation, daily paired awakening and breathing trials, early mobility, head of bed 30–45°, and subglottic secretion drainage tubes for an expected stay over 48–72 hours. It recommends toothbrushing and no longer recommends routine chlorhexidine oral care; some units still use it.

  • Document depth at the teeth or lips each shift and after every repositioning.
  • Turn and transport with one person whose only job is the tube; take circuit weight off the tube.
  • Use a bite block if the patient bites; suction above the cuff before deflating it.

Secretions and biofilm can narrow the lumen of the endotracheal tube itself, raising airway resistance and peak inspiratory pressure and, at worst, blocking the tube. The AARC 2022 suctioning guideline (Blakeman, Respir Care 2022) says devices that clear the inside of the endotracheal tube may be used, in addition to standard suction catheters, when resistance is raised by secretion build-up. The same guideline says saline instillation should generally be avoided, routine bronchoscopy for secretion removal is not recommended, adult suction pressure stays below −200 mm Hg, and each pass lasts at most 15 seconds.

Unplanned extubation is mostly self-extubation, the rest accidental displacement during care. Risk factors are agitation and delirium, light sedation without close observation, weak securement, turning and transport, and the weaning phase; restraints do not reliably prevent it. Many self-extubations occur in patients close to weaning and a sizable share never need reintubation; accidental extubations more often do.

  1. Call for help and stay with the patient.
  2. Tube partly out: hold it, do not push it back blindly; check capnography.
  3. No CO₂ or tube out: deflate the cuff, remove it, suction, give high-flow oxygen by mask or bag-mask.
  4. Assess work of breathing, SpO₂ and mental status; prepare for a possibly harder reintubation.

Extubation readiness and post-extubation stridor

Pair a daily spontaneous awakening trial with a spontaneous breathing trial: in the ABC trial (Girard, Lancet 2008) this gave more ventilator-free days and lower 1-year mortality (number needed to treat about 7). An SBT lasts 30–120 minutes on pressure support 5–8 with PEEP 5 or on a T-piece. Stop it for RR over 35, SpO₂ under 90%, HR over 140 or a 20% change, SBP over 180 or under 90, agitation, diaphoresis or accessory muscle use.

ATS/CHEST 2017 recommends preventive NIV right after extubation in high-risk patients (for example age over 65, heart failure, COPD, hypercapnia). HIGH-WEAN (Thille, JAMA 2019) found high-flow alternating with NIV cut reintubation to 11.8% from 18.2% with high-flow alone; in low-risk patients high-flow cut it to 4.9% from 12.2% with conventional oxygen (Hernández, JAMA 2016). Do not use NIV to rescue established failure: Esteban (NEJM 2004) found higher ICU mortality, attributed to delayed reintubation.

Post-extubation stridor comes from laryngeal edema; risk rises with traumatic or repeated intubation, intubation over about 6 days, a large tube and female sex. For the cuff leak test, deflate the cuff on volume control and compare inspired with expired volume; a leak under 110 mL (Miller and Cole 1996) or under about 10–25% of tidal volume predicts stridor. ATS/CHEST 2017 suggests the test in high-risk patients (conditional, very low certainty) and, if it fails, systemic steroids at least 4 hours before extubation without retesting. François (Lancet 2007) gave methylprednisolone 20 mg 12 hours before and every 4 hours until extubation; laryngeal edema fell from 22% to 3%.

  • Sit upright; humidified oxygen; calm the patient (anxiety raises turbulent flow).
  • Nebulized epinephrine; watch for rebound as it wears off; IV steroid if not given.
  • Heliox (70:30 or 80:20) lowers turbulent resistance as a bridge.
  • Rising work of breathing: reintubate early with a smaller tube.

Swelling, compression and bleeding: angioedema, neck hematoma, massive hemoptysis

ProblemRecognizeAirway plan
ACE-inhibitor angioedemaLip, tongue or throat swelling with no hives or itchingStop the drug; intubate early for voice change, hoarseness, stridor or dyspnea; double setup
Hematoma after thyroid surgeryTrouble swallowing, neck swelling, anxiety, fast breathing; stridor may be lateOxygen, head up, senior help; airway compromise: open the wound, then intubate if needed
Massive hemoptysisBlood flooding the airways; asphyxiation is the usual cause of deathBleeding lung down; isolate the lungs; large tube; embolization

ACE inhibitors raise bradykinin levels. The swelling can start soon after the drug is begun or years later, favors the face and upper airway, and comes without urticaria or pruritus; ACE inhibitors cause about 30% of the acute angioedema seen in emergency departments. Epinephrine, glucocorticoids and antihistamines have not been shown to work in bradykinin-mediated swelling, so intubation is needed more often than in allergic angioedema, which usually shrinks quickly after epinephrine (Merck Manual).

Whatever the cause, once the airway is involved, securing it is the highest priority. Stridor, hoarseness, a changed voice and dyspnea predict the need for a definitive airway; swelling limited to the lips usually does not need one. Expect a difficult airway: manipulation can worsen the edema, a supraglottic airway sits above the obstruction and is not recommended, and noninvasive ventilation only buys time. The plan is an awake flexible-scope or video laryngoscope intubation with a double setup, meaning the cricothyrotomy equipment and operator are ready before the first attempt (Long 2019).

Stop the ACE inhibitor; the swelling usually resolves about 24 to 48 hours later. For severe or progressing swelling, C1 inhibitor concentrate, fresh frozen plasma, icatibant or ecallantide may be tried, but the focus stays on the airway.

Bleeding complicates 0.45% to 4.2% of thyroid operations. It occurs most often within the first 24 hours, about half within 6 hours, and the hematoma can compress the airway with little warning (Iliff 2022: Difficult Airway Society, BAETS and ENT UK). The warning signs are difficulty swallowing or discomfort, a rising early warning score, swelling, anxiety, tachypnea or difficulty breathing, and stridor (DESATS); stridor may be a late sign. Any of them calls for oxygen, a head-up position and immediate senior surgical review, with a senior anesthetist if there is airway compromise.

The sequence is oxygenate and evaluate, evacuate, intubate. With signs of airway compromise (desaturation, difficulty breathing, stridor, tachypnea) the wound is opened at the bedside before intubation is attempted, using the SCOOP steps; local anesthetic is not required:

  1. Skin exposure.
  2. Cut the sutures.
  3. Open the skin.
  4. Open the muscles, superficial and deep (strap) layers.
  5. Pack the wound.

If the airway does not improve after evacuation, an experienced anesthetist intubates following difficult-airway guidance: video laryngoscope for the first attempt, few attempts, and early front-of-neck access, because repeated attempts worsen laryngeal edema. IV dexamethasone and tranexamic acid may be considered, but neither works immediately.

Massive hemoptysis has no agreed volume; definitions run from 100 mL in 24 hours to more than 1 L in 48 hours. The anatomic dead space is only about 150 mL, so a modest amount of blood can stop gas exchange, and asphyxiation, not blood loss, is the usual cause of death (Charya 2021). Initial treatment has two aims: keep blood out of the uninvolved lung and stop the bleeding (Merck Manual).

  • Once the bleeding side is known, position the patient with the bleeding lung dependent (bleeding side down).
  • If intubation is needed, use a large tube (8.5–9 mm for men, 8.0–8.5 mm for women) so a therapeutic bronchoscope and a bronchial blocker can pass.
  • Isolate the lungs: advance the tube, with bronchoscopic guidance, into the mainstem bronchus of the non-bleeding lung, or block the bronchus of the bleeding lung with a balloon blocker.
  • Selective intubation of the right mainstem can occlude the right upper lobe bronchus; a double-lumen tube also isolates the lungs but is hard to place and too narrow for therapeutic bronchoscopy.
  • Stop the bleeding: correct clotting deficits (plasma, platelets, factor replacement), consider tranexamic acid, and use bronchoscopic measures such as cold saline lavage of the bleeding lung or topical vasoconstrictors; rigid bronchoscopy gives airway control and better suction.
  • Bronchial artery embolization is the preferred treatment, with success rates up to 90% (the bronchial arteries are the source in about 90% of cases); surgery is the last resort.
Safety Bleeding from the right lung: right side down, tube into the left mainstem bronchus. Good lung down, or a tube in the bleeding side, lets blood spill into the lung the patient depends on.

Cannot ventilate: laryngospasm, negative-pressure pulmonary edema, fentanyl rigidity

ProblemClueFirst move
LaryngospasmStridor, or strong efforts with no air movement, after extubation or airway stimulationJaw thrust, CPAP with 100% oxygen
Negative-pressure pulmonary edemaPink, frothy sputum and hypoxemia minutes after an obstructed airwayReopen the airway, then oxygen and CPAP or noninvasive ventilation
Fentanyl chest-wall rigidityStiff chest and locked jaw right after a rapid IV doseNeuromuscular blocker and intubation

Laryngospasm is a sudden, involuntary contraction of the laryngeal muscles that partly or completely closes the airway. It follows airway stimulation at an inadequate depth of anesthesia, blood or secretions on the larynx, airway procedures, and removal of an endotracheal tube or laryngeal mask (Ali 2025). Partial closure gives inspiratory stridor; with complete closure the patient makes vigorous efforts, with retractions and paradoxical chest movement, but moves no air, and SpO₂ falls.

  1. Call for help; remove the stimulus and suction blood or secretions from the pharynx.
  2. Open the airway with a firm jaw thrust and chin lift; add an oral or nasal airway if needed.
  3. Apply CPAP with 100% oxygen through a tightly sealed mask.
  4. If it does not break or SpO₂ keeps falling, deepen with a small IV dose of propofol.
  5. Still closed: give succinylcholine, ventilate, and intubate if needed.

Firm upward pressure in the laryngospasm notch, just below the earlobe behind the mandible, while lifting the jaw (Larson maneuver) is an added option. Trying to force breaths through fully closed cords can inflate the stomach, so opening the airway comes first and drugs should not be delayed: a vigorous adult can develop pulmonary edema after only a few obstructed breaths (Sibert 2020).

Negative-pressure pulmonary edema follows forceful inspiration against a closed upper airway. In adults the usual cause is laryngospasm after extubation; others are biting on the endotracheal tube, strangulation, a foreign body, and croup or epiglottitis in children. Intrathoracic pressure can fall to about −50 to −100 cm H₂O, which draws fluid from the pulmonary capillaries into the interstitium and alveoli. The edema is noncardiogenic and there is no fluid overload; young, otherwise healthy patients, more often male, are typical (Ma 2023; Shaikh 2025).

It usually appears within minutes of the obstruction, sometimes a few hours later: respiratory distress, falling SpO₂ and pink, frothy sputum, with diffuse infiltrates on the chest x-ray. Treatment is to reopen the airway, then oxygen and positive pressure: CPAP or noninvasive ventilation in an awake patient with a patent airway, intubation with PEEP in severe cases (Lemyze 2026). Diuretics are controversial because the patient is not volume overloaded. With support it typically improves within 12 to 48 hours, and most cases resolve within 12 to 24 hours (Ma 2023). Suggested prevention is to suction the pharynx before extubation and to extubate either deeply anesthetized or fully awake.

Fentanyl can cause muscle rigidity, particularly of the muscles of respiration; the label relates it to the dose and the speed of injection. In wooden chest syndrome the chest wall and abdomen stiffen and the jaw locks, and bag-mask ventilation meets high resistance (Rosal 2021).

The label's management is a neuromuscular blocking agent; when intubation is already planned, give the paralytic and intubate. For rapidly given anesthetic doses the label advises a full paralyzing dose of a blocker at the same time as the fentanyl. Naloxone also reverses the rigidity but removes the analgesia and can precipitate withdrawal. An opioid reversal agent, resuscitation and intubation equipment and oxygen must be at hand whenever fentanyl is given.

Tracheostomy care and emergencies

Tracheostomy allows lighter sedation, easier weaning, oral care and communication, but timing does not change survival: TracMan (Young, JAMA 2013) found 30-day mortality of 30.8% with early (day 1–4) vs 31.5% with late (day 10 or later) tracheostomy. The tract takes about a week to mature, so a tube displaced in the first week can be reinserted into pretracheal tissue (false passage).

  • At the bedside: obturator, spare tubes in the same size and one smaller, syringe, suction, bag-valve-mask and the bedhead sign.
  • Humidify; clean or change the inner cannula per the tube type.
  • Change ties with two people, one holding the flange; one finger fits under the ties.
  • Cuff 20–30 cm H₂O; suction to a measured depth, under about 15 seconds per pass.
Safety A speaking valve or cap goes only on a tube with the cuff fully deflated, or a cuffless tube. With the cuff up the patient cannot exhale.

The National Tracheostomy Safety Project (McGrath, Anaesthesia 2012) separates a tracheostomy with a patent upper airway (green bedhead sign) from a laryngectomy, where the mouth does not connect to the lungs (red sign). For a blocked or displaced tube:

  1. Call the airway expert; check the bedhead sign.
  2. Look, listen and feel at mouth and tube; put waveform capnography on the tube.
  3. High-flow oxygen to face and stoma (stoma only after laryngectomy).
  4. Remove the speaking valve or cap and the inner cannula.
  5. Pass a suction catheter: if it passes, suction and reassess.
  6. If it does not pass, deflate the cuff; no improvement, remove the tube. Pulseless: CPR.
  7. Oxygenate: bag-mask over the mouth with the stoma covered, or a small mask or SGA over the stoma.
  8. Expert: oral intubation with an uncut tube past the stoma, or a small cuffed tube into the stoma.

Early bleeding is usually wound oozing, managed with pressure, packing and correcting coagulopathy. Tracheo-innominate artery fistula is rare (about 0.1–1%), occurs mostly within 3–4 weeks and is often preceded by a small sentinel bleed. Overinflate the cuff; if bleeding continues, press a finger through the stoma against the artery and sternum (Utley maneuver), secure the airway from above with the cuff below the bleed, and call surgery.

Tracheostomy: percutaneous insertion, fenestrated tubes and teams

Percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy is a bedside, modified Seldinger technique: needle, guidewire, dilation, then the tube, ideally between the 2nd and 3rd tracheal rings and usually under bronchoscopic view (Ghattas 2021). The endotracheal tube is the only airway until the new tube is confirmed, so it is pulled back, not out.

  1. Set FiO₂ 1.0 and adjust the ventilator to keep minute ventilation while the bronchoscope partly blocks the tube; monitor SpO₂, capnography, ECG and blood pressure.
  2. Extend the neck over a shoulder roll; the bronchoscopist inspects and suctions the airway.
  3. After the skin incision and blunt dissection, partly deflate the endotracheal tube cuff and withdraw the tube under bronchoscopic view until its tip is in the subglottic space, just above the puncture site, with the 1st and 2nd tracheal rings visible.
  4. Hold the tube at that depth: accidental extubation and needle puncture of the cuff are the life-threatening hazards of this step (Al-Ansari 2006).
  5. When the bronchoscopist confirms the tracheostomy tube is in the trachea, move the ventilator circuit to it, inflate its cuff and place the inner cannula. Do not ventilate through the endotracheal tube with the tracheostomy cuff inflated (risk of pneumomediastinum).
  6. Remove the endotracheal tube only after the new airway is confirmed. The tracheostomy tube tip should sit at least 1–2 cm above the carina; secure the tube and watch for bleeding, subcutaneous emphysema and pneumothorax.

A fenestrated tracheostomy tube has an opening in the curve of the outer cannula that lets air pass up through the larynx for speech. The opening has costs. Tracheal mucosa can be drawn into it during suctioning, or it can lie against the tracheal wall, and granulation tissue, stenosis and tracheomalacia follow; granulation was found in 9 of 15 patients (60%) in one series (Pandian 2019). During positive-pressure ventilation gas can track between the cannulas and leak through the fenestration, which has caused subcutaneous emphysema (Berlet 2016).

For suctioning and for cuffed positive-pressure ventilation, swap in the nonfenestrated (solid) inner cannula: it closes the fenestration, protects the mucosa from the catheter and limits the leak. Many authors advise against fenestrated tubes in patients who need positive-pressure ventilation, and the UK Intensive Care Society advises against them early after the stoma is formed.

The AARC 2021 guideline for adults with a tracheostomy in acute care (Mussa, Respir Care 2021) makes three recommendations, each at evidence level B:

StrategyOutcomes it improves
Tracheostomy bundle approved by clinicians experienced in tracheostomy careTime to decannulation, adverse events, tolerance of oral diet
Multidisciplinary tracheostomy teamTime to decannulation, length of stay, adverse events, speaking valve use
Weaning and decannulation protocolTime to decannulation

Team membership varied between studies: in the North American studies it included a physician, a respiratory therapist and a speech-language pathologist; elsewhere nurses, physiotherapists, dietitians and social workers took part.

Exam tip Of the three strategies, the multidisciplinary team is the one tied to shorter length of stay and more speaking valve use.

Exam pearls

  • LEMON predicts hard laryngoscopy, MOANS hard bag-mask, RODS a hard SGA, SHORT a hard cricothyrotomy.
  • Fix hypotension, hypoxemia and acidosis before induction; set a high rate after intubating a patient in metabolic acidosis.
  • Hypoxemic patient: preoxygenate with NIV; apneic oxygenation needs an open airway.
  • Etomidate is the most hemodynamically neutral; propofol causes the most hypotension; ketamine bronchodilates.
  • Succinylcholine raises potassium about 0.5–1 mEq/L; avoid in hyperkalemia, MH risk, myopathy, and burns or crush after the first day.
  • Capnography confirms the trachea; the x-ray confirms depth (tip 3–5 cm above the carina).
  • Cuff 20–30 cm H₂O; failed cuff leak test in a high-risk patient means steroids at least 4 hours before extubation.
  • Speaking valve only with the cuff fully deflated.
  • Blocked tracheostomy: oxygen to face and stoma, remove the inner cannula, pass a suction catheter, deflate the cuff, then remove the tube.
  • BURP is pressure on the thyroid cartilage to improve the view; cricoid pressure is for regurgitation and is released if the view is poor.
  • Working supraglottic rescue: wake the patient if surgery is not urgent; in the ICU, intubate through the device with a flexible scope.
  • Laryngeal mask leak: lower the cuff pressure first; keep it no higher than 60 cm H₂O.
  • Midface or basal skull fracture: no nasotracheal tube.
  • Massive hemoptysis: bleeding lung down, tube into the mainstem bronchus of the good lung, then embolization.
  • Airway compromise from a neck hematoma after thyroid surgery: open the wound (SCOOP) before intubating.
  • Laryngospasm: jaw thrust and CPAP with 100% oxygen, then propofol, then succinylcholine. Fentanyl rigidity: neuromuscular blocker.
  • Fenestrated tracheostomy tube: nonfenestrated inner cannula for suctioning and positive-pressure ventilation.
