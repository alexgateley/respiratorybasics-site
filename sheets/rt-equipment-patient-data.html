Try one

Equipment, Gas Supply and Patient Data practice question

A patient with a hoarse voice has a harsh, high-pitched sound on every inspiration. It is loudest with the stethoscope over the neck, expiration is quiet, and the lung fields have no added sounds. Where is the airway narrowing most likely to be?

  1. In the intrathoracic trachea or mainstem bronchi
  2. In the small airways, from bronchospasm
  3. At or above the vocal cords, outside the chest
  4. At a fixed lesion below the vocal cords

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Obstruction of the trachea or bronchi inside the chest produces mainly expiratory stridor, not a sound confined to inspiration.
  • B. Bronchospasm produces wheezes heard over the lung fields, mostly during exhalation; clear lung fields and a sound centered on the neck argue against it.
  • C. Correct. Stridor that is mainly inspiratory points to supraglottic or glottic narrowing, and the hoarse voice also places the problem at the larynx.
  • D. A fixed glottic or subglottic lesion typically gives biphasic stridor, heard in both phases of breathing; here expiration is quiet.

Stridor is a high-pitched, mainly inspiratory sound made by turbulent flow through a narrowed segment of the upper airway, usually outside the chest. Its timing helps localize the narrowing: inspiratory stridor points to the supraglottic or glottic level, expiratory stridor to the intrathoracic trachea or bronchi, and biphasic stridor to a fixed glottic or subglottic lesion. Auscultate the nose, mouth, neck and chest to find where it is loudest. Common adult causes include vocal fold dysfunction, vocal fold edema or paralysis, postextubation laryngeal edema, laryngeal tumors and allergic reactions; drooling, a tripod position, hypoxemia or a falling level of consciousness signal an airway emergency.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Medical gas identity and connections

Federal labeling rules (21 CFR 201.328) require high-pressure medical gas cylinders to be colored on the shoulder so the color shows from above, and portable cryogenic containers to carry a 360-degree wraparound label naming the gas. Color helps recognition but does not state a mixture, and colors differ by country (one Indian review describes oxygen cylinders with a black body and white shoulder). The label is what identifies the contents, so a cylinder with a torn or unreadable label is set aside and reported, even if the shoulder colors and the regulator fit look right.

Indexed connections are a second barrier. Small cylinders use a yoke with the Pin Index Safety System (oxygen pins 2 and 5); low-pressure connections such as wall outlets and flowmeter inlets use the Diameter Index Safety System, in which thread diameters differ by gas. A fitting that will not connect suggests the wrong gas: the response is to stop and read the label, never to change, adapt or force the fitting.

FDA and NIOSH (2006) traced fires at the regulator-valve junction to oxygen leaking across reused nylon crush gaskets, which can ignite from flow friction. Their advice: crack the cylinder valve briefly before attaching the regulator to blow debris out of the port; fit one clean seal, either a new single-use crush gasket or the metal-bound elastomeric washer made for repeated use; keep the valve, regulator and gasket free of oil and grease; tighten the T-handle by hand, never with a wrench; and open the post valve slowly, closing it at once if gas escapes at the junction.

Cylinders are kept upright and secured against falling, moved on a cart rather than rolled, inspected for damage, and never modified at the pressure relief device (CGA). Oxygen supports combustion: see PFTs and Oxygen Delivery for fire precautions and the cylinder factors used in duration math. Worked example: an E cylinder at 1,000 psi running at 4 L/min lasts about 1,000 × 0.28 ÷ 4 = 70 minutes.

Liquefied gases, cylinder test dates and oxygen at home

Oxygen and air are stored as compressed gas, so the amount left falls with the gauge pressure and can be calculated from it. Nitrous oxide is stored as a liquid with gas above it. As gas is drawn off, more liquid vaporizes, so the gauge pressure stays the same for as long as liquid remains, and a steady reading does not mean a full cylinder. In an E cylinder the pressure begins to fall only when about 400 L are left, out of about 1,590 L when full. The contents of a nitrous oxide cylinder are found by weight: weigh it and subtract the empty weight (about 5.9 kg for an E cylinder); 1 lb (0.45 kg) of nitrous oxide is about 247.5 L of gas.

Cylinders are also shipping containers, and the Department of Transportation requires each one to be requalified (pressure tested) on a schedule and stamped with the date: the month and year of the test, with the requalifier number set between them. Aluminum DOT 3AL cylinders are requalified every 5 years (the 12-year interval in the table is for cylinders used as fire extinguishers). Steel DOT 3A and 3AA cylinders are requalified every 5 years, or every 10 years when they meet the listed conditions and carry a five-pointed star stamped right after the test date. A cylinder that is past due may not be filled and offered for transportation, however sound it looks: it is set aside and sent for requalification first. A cylinder filled before the due date may stay in service until it is emptied. Example: an aluminum cylinder stamped 3 20 with no star came due in March 2025.

In a car, the oxygen unit rides upright on the floor or on the seat beside the patient, held by a seat belt, with a window slightly open and nobody smoking. It does not go in the trunk and is never left in a hot car (American Lung Association, MedlinePlus). A supplier safety data sheet for compressed oxygen gives the number behind the heat rule: cylinder temperature should not exceed 52 °C (125 °F), and cylinders are kept upright and firmly secured. On a hot day, spare cylinders therefore go with the patient at stops.

Flowmeters and blenders in detail

A Thorpe tube is a float in a vertical tapered tube; it depends on gravity and reads correctly only upright. FDA classifies two kinds. The uncompensated tube is calibrated with its outlet open to the atmosphere, so back pressure from a kinked line or a high-resistance device changes the reading, classically making it read lower than the flow actually delivered. The compensated tube is calibrated at a reference outlet pressure (the needle valve sits downstream of the float) and stays accurate with downstream resistance; the float jumps briefly when it is plugged into a live outlet with the valve closed.

A Bourdon gauge flowmeter is a pressure gauge behind a fixed orifice, calibrated as flow with the outlet open (21 CFR 868.2300). It works in any position, which suits transport cylinders, but it cannot sense a downstream obstruction: a kink raises the pressure, so the needle reads the same or higher while the patient receives less. A cylinder needs a regulator ahead of a flowmeter.

Air-oxygen blenders proportion two 50-psi supplies. In one cleared design (a single 510(k) summary) the alarm sounds when the supply pressures differ by about 20 psi, and the higher-pressure gas then flows straight to the outlet until the difference is back to about 6 psi; other designs and settings differ. At a 21% setting an oxygen loss may not alarm, and a blender may stay silent if both supplies fall together. The response to a blender alarm is to check wall outlets, hoses and cylinders and to analyze the delivered oxygen, not to recalibrate the blender.

Liquid oxygen, concentrators, piped-gas failure and MRI

Bulk liquid oxygen is stored at about −160 to −180 °C (it boils at −183 °C); one liter of liquid yields about 861 L of gas, and it slowly evaporates even when unused, which matters for home liquid reservoirs. Supply is regulated to about 50 to 55 psi for flowmeters and ventilators. Oxygen-failure alarms sense pressure, not flow, so they do not detect a wrong gas in the line.

Concentrators pass room air through zeolite sieve beds that adsorb nitrogen and deliver about 92 to 96% oxygen when working well. Open Critical Care states that a device should have a low-oxygen alarm below 82%; WHO specifications require more than 82% at maximum rated flow, so individual devices differ. Common units deliver 3, 5, 8 or 10 L/min, and sieve beds have a finite life (about 25,000 hours is quoted). A concentrator needs electricity, so a home patient keeps a backup cylinder and tells the fire department, electric company and telephone company that oxygen is in use (MedlinePlus). A low-purity alarm means switching to the backup cylinder and calling the equipment provider.

If piped oxygen fails, each ventilated patient is moved to a cylinder supply or a manual resuscitator in order of need, the delivered FiO₂ is verified, and the cylinder inventory is managed until the system is restored. Before MRI, the ventilator, cylinder, pumps and monitors must be labeled for that MRI environment: the field may always be on, a steel oxygen tank at the door can be pulled into the bore, and freeing it may require quenching the magnet.

Oxygen analyzers

Electrochemical analyzers measure oxygen partial pressure, displayed as a percentage. A galvanic (fuel) cell makes its own current from a lead anode that is consumed, responds in about 20 seconds and is unaffected by water vapor. A polarographic (Clark) cell uses a membrane-covered electrode driven by the analyzer battery; its membrane deteriorates over about 3 years. Both are calibrated at two points, room air (21%) and 100% oxygen, before use; a cell that drifts or cannot reach 100% is replaced. Paramagnetic analyzers, used in anesthesia machines, are fast enough for breath-by-breath readings but need a water trap.

Ventilator pre-use check

Each ventilator has a check specified by its manufacturer that is run in standby after the ventilator is set up with a new breathing circuit and before the patient is connected. Names differ (preoperational check, pre-use check, short self test), but the content is similar: a leak test (tightness or circuit pressure test) with the patient end blocked, flow sensor calibration, oxygen sensor calibration where needed, and on some ventilators a measurement of circuit resistance and compliance. It is run with the components that will be used on the patient.

A failed leak test is treated as a circuit problem first. One manufacturer card gives the order: check the breathing circuit for a disconnection, a poorly seated connection or a leak; check the flow sensor; check that the expiratory valve set is installed and seated; repeat the test without the humidifier (a pass then points to a leak in the humidifier setup); replace the circuit, flow sensor and expiratory valve set; and only if it still fails, have the ventilator serviced. On another ventilator, a pressure drop during the circuit pressure test prompts the operator to inspect the circuit for leaks and retest. So the first response is to reseat the humidifier chamber and every connection and repeat the test, not to bypass it and not to send the ventilator away at once. The check comes before the patient is connected, so a ventilator that still fails is not put on a patient.

Transporting a ventilated patient

Moving a ventilated patient always carries risk and is undertaken only after the benefit has been weighed against it (AARC 2002). Adverse events have been reported in up to about 70% of in-hospital transports, and up to one third of mishaps are equipment related; mechanical ventilation and ventilation with PEEP are patient risk factors (Waydhas 1999). The AARC lists the ventilatory hazards: hyperventilation during manual ventilation, causing respiratory alkalosis, dysrhythmias and hypotension; loss of PEEP or CPAP, causing hypoxemia or shock; disconnection from the ventilator; accidental extubation; and loss of the oxygen supply.

The aim is that ventilation and oxygenation stay constant during the move, so a patient who depends on PEEP or a high FiO₂ travels on a transport ventilator set to reproduce the ICU settings, with the manual resuscitator carried as the backup. Transport ventilators give more constant ventilation than hand bagging in some comparisons (AARC), and mechanical ventilation during in-hospital transport has been judged superior to manual ventilation for oxygenation, constant tidal volume and regular cycles (Fanara 2010). In one series, PaCO₂ and pH changed by a mean of 9 mm Hg and 0.08 with hand bagging and by 4 mm Hg and 0.05 with a portable ventilator, although complications were still seen in 44% of the ventilator group, so monitoring matters with either method. PEEP is not lowered for convenience before the trip: its loss is one of the named hazards. The AARC asks that a transport ventilator have enough portable power for the trip, independent control of tidal volume and rate, full support (assist-control or IMV), constant volume despite changing impedance, airway pressure monitoring, a disconnect alarm, PEEP, and an FiO₂ of 1.0.

  • Settings: FiO₂, PEEP, rate, exhaled tidal volume and the pressure and disconnect alarms are set and tested on the transport ventilator before leaving.
  • Oxygen: enough for the whole trip plus a 30-minute reserve, not the trip time alone.
  • Carry: a self-inflating bag and mask of the right size, emergency airway supplies, suction (portable, or confirmed at the destination), drugs, a monitor, pulse oximetry, and capnography.
  • Destination: confirm it is ready, with oxygen connectors, wall suction, a defibrillator, power and room to work.
  • People: a registered nurse and a respiratory therapist go with every ventilated patient, and one of the team must be able to manage the airway after an accidental extubation.
  • Monitoring matches stationary care: continuous ECG and pulse oximetry, blood pressure, airway pressures on the ventilator, and intermittent tidal volume and breath sounds.

Self-inflating bag: keeping the FiO₂ high

A self-inflating resuscitator refills by itself, with or without oxygen, so a good chest rise says nothing about the FiO₂. Oxygen reaches the bag from a reservoir (about 2,600 mL on one adult model) that fills only when the oxygen flow is adequate. If the flow cannot keep up, the reservoir empties, its inlet flap valve opens and the bag draws in room air; during hand ventilation this happens when the tidal volume and rate are high and the oxygen flow is too low. To deliver close to 100% oxygen, the oxygen flow has to exceed the minute volume being given. Figures for one adult bag: at 8 L/min it delivers almost 100% at 400 mL × 12/min but only 68% at 600 mL × 24/min, while at 15 L/min the delivered fraction can exceed 95% across a wide range of volumes and rates.

Practical rule: run the bag at high flow (about 15 L/min), watch that the reservoir stays filled, and plan the transport cylinder for that flow instead of turning the flow down to make it last. A reservoir that lies flat after each squeeze means the oxygen flow is too low for the ventilation being given: raise the flow to about 15 L/min. A PEEP valve can be fitted to the expiratory port of many bags; it keeps PEEP but does not make up for an oxygen flow that is too low.

Cut pilot line; bronchoscopy through the tube

A cuff that will not hold air is not always a torn cuff: the fault can lie in the inflation line, the pilot balloon or its one-way valve, and those do not necessarily require a new tube. When the pilot line has been cut and reintubation or tube exchange would be hazardous (difficult airway, severe hypoxemia, high PEEP), the line can be repaired in place: an intravenous cannula with its needle removed, or a blunt needle, is pushed into the cut end, a three-way stopcock and syringe are attached, the cuff is reinflated and the stopcock is closed to hold the air. Cannula sizes that fit: 22 or 24 gauge for 6.0 to 7.0 mm tubes, 18 to 22 gauge for 7.5 and 8.0 mm tubes. A sharp needle can cut through the line. Without a pilot balloon the cuff is easily overinflated, so a cuff manometer on the stopcock is used to set the usual pressure (20 to 30 cm H₂O). A hemostat on the line only holds whatever air is still in the cuff. The repair is temporary: once the patient is stable the tube is changed electively, over an airway exchange catheter when the airway is difficult.

Bronchoscopy in a ventilated patient is done through the tube, with a swivel adapter that seals around the scope, and mechanical ventilation is not a contraindication. The scope fills much of the lumen: a 5 to 6 mm scope takes up about two thirds of a 7 mm tube and about 40% of a 9 mm tube. Peak pressure rises and, unless the pressure limit is raised, the set tidal volume is not delivered; expiratory flow is impeded, so gas traps and intrinsic PEEP develops (over 35 cm H₂O has been reported with small tubes), with a risk of barotrauma and of hypotension from reduced venous return. The tube should be at least 1.5 to 2 mm wider inside than the scope, or at least 8 mm.

Preparation: give FiO₂ 1.0 before, during and after the procedure, and reset the alarms: raise the high-pressure limit so that the tidal volume is still delivered and check the low minute volume alarm. Use a mandatory mode, not a spontaneous one, and watch exhaled tidal volume, SpO₂, ECG and end-tidal CO₂ throughout. The patient is not extubated for the procedure, and the ventilator is not left as it was. Lowering the starting PEEP has been suggested against barotrauma, but blind PEEP changes are called hazardous, so PEEP is not simply taken off to make room for the scope. If SpO₂ stays below 88%, the scope is withdrawn and FiO₂ or PEEP is raised until saturation is back to 95% or more. If inspiratory pressure was raised in pressure control for the procedure, it is returned to baseline before the scope comes out.

Reprocessing, sharps and leak tests

The Spaulding scheme sets the minimum: items entering sterile tissue or the vascular system are sterilized (biopsy forceps break the mucosa and count as critical); items touching mucous membranes or nonintact skin, including respiratory therapy and anesthesia equipment, bronchoscopes and laryngoscope blades, get at least high-level disinfection; items touching intact skin get low-level disinfection. Cleaning comes first, because disinfection fails on soiled surfaces.

For flexible scopes the sequence is a leak test before immersion, cleaning, high-level disinfection or sterilization, rinse, alcohol rinse and forced-air drying, and storage that protects the scope from recontamination. Tap water can recontaminate equipment with nontuberculous mycobacteria, Legionella and Pseudomonas, which is why CDC prefers a sterile-water rinse and accepts tap or filtered water only when followed by alcohol and forced air.

OSHA forbids bending, recapping or removing contaminated needles unless no alternative exists, and then only with a one-handed technique or a device; shearing or breaking needles is forbidden. Sharps containers are closable, puncture resistant and leakproof, upright, not overfilled, and as close as feasible to where sharps are used.

Blood gas quality control, worked through

Control results are plotted on a Levey-Jennings chart against the mean and standard deviations. In the classic multirule, a value beyond 2 SD is only a warning that prompts a look at the other rules. The run is rejected only if a rejection rule fails. Random error (scatter) is usually shown by 1-3s or R-4s; systematic error (bias, as from an aging electrode, protein on a membrane or calibration drift) by 2-2s, 4-1s or 10x. R-4s is read within one run; 4-1s and 10x are often read across runs and control levels, and computer QC can apply them without waiting for a 2 SD warning. A steady climb of one control over several runs, still inside the limits, is building systematic error and is fixed before it fails.

PatternDecision

CLIA (42 CFR 493.1267) requires calibration or calibration verification as the manufacturer specifies, one control sample each 8 hours of testing with low and high values on each day of testing, a control each time patients are tested unless the instrument verifies its own calibration at least every 30 minutes, and documentation of everything. Electronic QC checks the sensors and electronics; control material still has to run on that schedule. After a failure, the laboratory must evaluate all patient results from the failed run and since the last acceptable run, act and document (493.1282). Proficiency testing samples are run in the routine workload by the staff who normally test, with no discussion with other laboratories, and records are kept 2 years (493.801).

Why 2 SD is only a warning: used as a rejection rule, 1-2s falsely rejects about 9% of good runs with two control measurements, about 14% with three and almost 18% with four, while 3 SD limits falsely reject only about 1% (Westgard). Rejecting every run with a control beyond 2 SD, on top of the multirule, therefore adds frequent rejection of runs that are in control; it does not add safety. The multirule keeps false alarms low by using 1-2s as a warning and confirming a problem with the more specific rules.

In practice a new control lot is run in parallel with the lot in use, before the old lot runs out: Westgard advises about 20 measurements over 2 to 4 weeks, from which the laboratory calculates the new mean and SD and so verifies or replaces the insert values. Package-insert ranges are not adopted unchecked, the old lot mean and limits are not carried over to the new lot, and control results must meet the laboratory criteria before patient results are reported (493.1256(f)).

Calibration verification means testing materials of known concentration in the same way as patient samples to show that the test system is accurate across its reportable range. Under the general CLIA rule (493.1255) it is done at least every 6 months and after the events in the table, at a low, a mid-point and a high value; routine controls do not count as calibration verification, but a calibration procedure that itself uses three or more levels (low, mid and high) at least every 6 months meets the requirement (CMS). Blood gas analyses are the exception. For pH, PCO₂ and PO₂ the specific rule is 493.1267(a): calibrate or verify calibration as the manufacturer specifies. The CMS interpretive guidelines say that a laboratory that meets the manufacturer instructions and 493.1267 does not have to meet the 493.1255 requirements, so CLIA itself does not demand a three-level verification of the blood gas sensors every 6 months or after a repair. Other analytes measured on the same analyzer, such as electrolytes, hemoglobin and ionized calcium, stay under the general rule (Iowa State Hygienic Laboratory).

After a sensor module or another critical part of a blood gas analyzer is replaced, two things come before patient samples: the calibration or calibration verification that the manufacturer specifies for that repair, and control testing that meets the laboratory criteria (493.1256(d)(6) and (f)). If the manufacturer instructions call for a multi-level verification, that is what the laboratory has to do. Controls are seldom available at the very high end of the blood gas range; where needed, results there can be checked by splitting patient samples between two analyzers (CMS interpretive guidelines).

Reading the chest radiograph

Check the film before the lungs. Rotation: the medial clavicle heads should be equidistant from the spinous processes; rotation distorts the mediastinum and the lung density. Inspiration: about ten posterior ribs at the midclavicular line. Projection: portable films are AP, magnify the heart by about 20% and are almost always suboptimal.

Lines and tubes: ETT tip about 3 to 5 cm above the carina (the carina lies at T4 to T5; one source cited gives 2 to 5 cm, and Kelly says only above the carina), knowing that neck flexion moves it toward the carina and extension away, by about 2 cm; central venous catheter tip at the cavoatrial junction (too high risks thrombosis and malfunction, too low arrhythmia); pulmonary artery catheter in a main pulmonary artery and never beyond the hilum; IABP tip about 2 cm below the left subclavian origin; enteric tube side port below the gastroesophageal junction, and a tube ending above the diaphragm may be in the airway.

Pneumothorax shows as a lung edge with no markings beyond it; end-expiratory views help detect small ones. In a supine patient air collects anteriorly and at the base, and an abnormally deep, lucent costophrenic angle (deep sulcus sign) may be the only clue. Tension is diagnosed clinically, not by waiting for a film; ultrasound is more sensitive than a plain film.

About 75 mL of fluid blunts the posterior costophrenic angle on an upright film, and a meniscus rises along the lateral wall; a supine effusion only makes one side look hazier. The opaque hemithorax: shift toward the opacity means collapse (a mucus plug, or a tube in the right mainstem bronchus occluding the left); shift away means a massive effusion (more than about 4 L) or mass; no shift suggests consolidation, or an effusion over a collapsed lung.

Chest examination and breathing patterns

Airless lung conducts sound, so consolidation gives increased fremitus, bronchial breath sounds, egophony (E heard as A) and whispered pectoriloquy over a dull area. Pleural fluid separates lung from wall: dull, with decreased or absent fremitus and breath sounds, and egophony at the upper border where compressed lung lies. Pneumothorax also lowers fremitus and breath sounds but is hyperresonant. Crackles suggest atelectasis, edema or interstitial disease; wheezes small-airway narrowing; a rub pleural inflammation.

Stridor is high-pitched and mainly inspiratory when the obstruction is in the extrathoracic upper airway; it can be expiratory or biphasic, and it signals a possible airway emergency. Breathing patterns: Cheyne-Stokes waxes and wanes in cycles with apnea (heart failure, neurologic disease, drugs); Biot breathing is irregular apnea alternating with 4 or 5 equal deep breaths that start and stop abruptly (central nervous system injury such as meningitis); Kussmaul breathing is deep and regular (metabolic acidosis); paradoxical breathing (the abdomen moves inward on inspiration) signals respiratory muscle fatigue or weakness.

Clubbing is diagnosed by a nail profile angle over 180° or a phalangeal depth ratio over 1; it is most common with lung cancer and is also seen in cystic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis; a benign hereditary form starts early in life.

Central cyanosis (whole body, including tongue and mucous membranes) needs at least about 5 g/dL of unsaturated hemoglobin, roughly an arterial saturation below 85%; its absence does not exclude hypoxemia, and in severe anemia it may never appear. Peripheral cyanosis of the lips, fingers and toes reflects slow flow with high extraction, as in low cardiac output, while the arterial saturation stays normal; if vigorous massage improves nail-bed color, the cyanosis is likely peripheral.

Arterial sampling and specimen quality

Modified Allen test: with the hand raised and the fist clenched, both arteries are compressed until the palm blanches, the hand is opened and the ulnar artery released. Color back within 5 to 15 seconds is normal; slower means poor ulnar collateral flow, and another site (usually the other radial) is chosen. The test has marked interobserver variability and predicts hand ischemia poorly (pooled sensitivity 77%, specificity 93%), and Doppler is the better check where available, but exams and many policies still expect it before a radial puncture.

Capillary samples do not assess oxygenation, and venous values do not replace arterial PaO₂, PaCO₂ or pH (AARC 2013). Sampling and pre-analytic errors are covered on ABG Interpretation.

A sputum sample is worth culturing when the Gram stain shows more than 25 neutrophils and fewer than 10 squamous epithelial cells per low-power (×100) field. Many squamous cells with few neutrophils means saliva: the result would grow mouth flora, so a new deep-cough or induced specimen is collected.

Pulse oximetry in depth

In critically ill patients with an SaO₂ of 90% or more, oximeter bias is under 2% with precision under 3%; agreement worsens below 90%. FDA lists poor circulation, skin pigmentation, skin thickness, skin temperature, current tobacco use and nail polish as factors that affect readings. FDA issued its safety communication in 2021, held advisory panels in November 2022 and February 2024, and issued draft guidance on January 7, 2025 that is not yet final.

Sjoding (2020) defined occult hypoxemia as an SaO₂ below 88% despite an SpO₂ of 92 to 96%. It occurred in 11.7% of such paired readings in Black patients vs 3.6% in White patients in one hospital, and in 17.0% vs 6.2% across 178 hospitals. The practical rule: when a decision hinges on a few points, or the patient looks worse than the number, get an arterial sample on a CO-oximeter.

Low perfusion and motion are the commonest causes of error, and the plethysmographic waveform is the quality check. In critically ill patients with low perfusion, forehead reflectance sensors agreed better with arterial values than finger sensors. Black, purple and dark-blue nail polish raised readings slightly with wide error, and turning the probe sideways did not remove it.

Two-wavelength oximeters count carboxyhemoglobin as oxyhemoglobin, so SpO₂ stays falsely reassuring in carbon monoxide poisoning. Multiwavelength pulse CO-oximeters estimate COHb, but in suspected poisoning their limits of agreement were about −12% to +14%, they could not measure COHb below an SaO₂ of 85%, and pooled sensitivity at a 10% cutoff was only 0.65. Laboratory CO-oximetry confirms or excludes poisoning while 100% oxygen continues.

Respiratory muscle testing and the stress index

MIP and MEP are volitional tests. MIP is measured after a full exhalation to residual volume (values about 30% higher than from FRC) and MEP from total lung capacity, each a maximal effort held 1 to 2 seconds, sitting, through a mouthpiece with a small (about 2 mm) leak that stops the cheeks and a closed glottis from adding pressure. Do 3 to 8 maneuvers and record the highest; repeat efforts should agree within about 10%. Lower limits of normal: MIP 60 cm H₂O in women and 80 in men (reported as negative pressures), MEP 120 and 150. Air leaks around the mouthpiece in facial weakness give falsely low values.

In intubated or uncooperative patients a one-way valve lets the patient exhale but not inhale, so each effort starts from a lower volume with rising drive; the highest value usually comes after 15 to 20 seconds, and the measurement takes about 25 seconds. A brief occlusion underestimates strength and should not drive a weaning decision on its own.

The stress index describes the shape of the airway pressure-time curve during volume control with constant (square-wave) flow in a passive patient. Volume enters at a steady rate, so the slope reflects compliance. A straight rise (index 1) suggests neither tidal recruitment nor overdistension; a curve bending upward (over 1) means compliance falls as the lung fills, suggesting overdistension, and PEEP, tidal volume or both are lowered; a curve bending downward (under 1) suggests tidal recruitment and room to raise PEEP. Patient effort invalidates it. Plateau pressure and auto-PEEP are on Ventilator Modes.

Respiratory drive (P0.1) and inspiratory time in pressure control

P0.1, the airway occlusion pressure, is the fall in airway pressure during the first 100 ms of an inspiratory effort against an occluded airway. The occlusion is too brief for the patient to react to it (reaction time is over 0.15 s), and because no gas flows and lung volume does not change, the value is largely independent of resistance and compliance. It therefore reflects the output of the respiratory centers, the respiratory drive, not muscle strength (that is the MIP) and not airway mechanics. Healthy adults at rest: about 0.5 to 1.5 cm H₂O, with large breath-to-breath variation, so three or four readings are averaged.

In ventilated patients a P0.1 above about 3.5 to 4 cm H₂O indicates high drive and excessive effort, and a value below about 1.1 to 1.5 cm H₂O (the cutoffs studies have used) suggests low drive, as with too much ventilator assistance or sedation. A P0.1 that rises while strength and mechanics are unchanged means that something is driving the patient harder: look for hypoxemia, hypercapnia, acidosis, pain, agitation, delirium or fear, a lung process stimulating chest receptors, or support that no longer meets demand. Limits: ventilators measure it in different ways, and those that estimate it without a true occlusion can underread when drive is high; expiratory muscle activity can inflate it; and as a weaning predictor on its own its results have been variable.

In pressure control the clinician sets the inspiratory pressure and the inspiratory time, and flow and volume are the result. Flow is highest at the start of the breath, when the gap between airway and alveolar pressure is widest, and decays as the alveoli fill. It continues until alveolar pressure equals the set pressure or the set inspiratory time ends, whichever comes first. The flow-time scalar therefore shows whether the inspiratory time is long enough. Inspiratory flow still above zero when the breath cycles off means alveolar pressure has not reached the set pressure (the plateau is below it), so a longer inspiratory time adds volume without any rise in set pressure, up to the point where flow reaches zero. Past that point more time adds no volume, only mean airway pressure. The time needed depends on the inspiratory time constant (resistance × compliance).

The cost of a longer inspiration is a shorter expiration, so after the change check that expiratory flow still returns to zero before the next breath; if it does not, gas is trapping (auto-PEEP), and in pressure control auto-PEEP lowers the tidal volume. Because the pressure is fixed, tidal volume in this mode changes whenever resistance or compliance changes, and ventilation can fall without a pressure alarm: watch exhaled tidal volume and minute ventilation.

Exam pearls

  • Label over color, color over fit: a matching regulator never proves the contents.
  • Hissing at the yoke: close the valve before touching the connection.
  • Bourdon reads high when kinked; uncompensated Thorpe reads low.
  • Blender alarm means a supply problem, not an FiO₂ problem; still analyze FiO₂.
  • Galvanic analyzer: two-point calibration at 21% and 100%.
  • Failed QC: fix, rerun, then look back to the last good run.
  • Opaque hemithorax: toward = collapse, away = fluid.
  • Supine trauma film with a deep lucent angle: pneumothorax.
  • SpO₂ 93% in a darkly pigmented, distressed patient: check SaO₂.
  • Stress index over 1: too much distension.
  • Nitrous oxide: a steady gauge only means liquid remains; weigh the cylinder.
  • Cylinder past its requalification date: do not fill it.
  • Home oxygen: 6 feet from heat, no petroleum jelly on the face, never stored in a trunk.
  • PEEP-dependent patient going to CT: transport ventilator matching the ICU settings; the bag is the backup.
  • Flat reservoir on the resuscitation bag: raise the oxygen flow to about 15 L/min.
  • P0.1 measures drive; MIP measures strength.
  • Pressure control with inspiratory flow not back to zero: more inspiratory time gives more volume.
  • One control beyond 2 SD is a warning; rejecting on it throws out good runs.
